# Advice to the CPS: criminal liability and conflicting evidence

## Executive conclusion

There is presently a **realistic prospect of convicting Meridian Structures Limited of corporate manslaughter** if the prosecution case is framed around the company’s own defective system of temporary-works control, rather than solely around a contention that a particular individual forged a certificate or personally ordered the final lift. The strongest features are the admitted physical absence of three ties, the defective allocation of mutually incompatible safety-critical roles to Mr Delacroix-Hale, the lack of an independent check or effective handover, the apparent use of a plainly unsound 2,000 kg certificate, and evidence capable of showing Construction Director Ferris’ involvement in the high-risk lifting regime and commercial pressure. The jury could conclude that senior-management management or organisation was a substantial element in a gross breach causing death. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 1 Law</a>; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/8" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 8 Law</a>

The case against **Mr Ferris personally for gross negligence manslaughter** is materially weaker at this stage. There is evidence capable of contradicting his denial of operational involvement, but there remains no direct admissible evidence that he knew of the missing ties, created or authorised the false documentation, or authorised the resumption of lifting at about 11:13. Professor Danecourt’s analysis potentially makes the decision to resume lifting, apparently made on site by Mr Delacroix-Hale, an independent central cause. The CPS should not charge Mr Ferris with gross negligence manslaughter until the identified digital, radio, driver, governance and disclosure enquiries are completed.

A charge against Mr Ferris under **HSWA 1974, s 37**, predicated principally on Meridian’s breach of its duty to non-employees under HSWA 1974, s 3, has better prospects than personal manslaughter. It will require proof that Meridian committed the underlying health-and-safety offence and that it was committed with Mr Ferris’ consent or connivance, or was attributable to his neglect. The signed high-risk lifting-plan approval, health-and-safety portfolio, failure to cure the incompatible role concentration, and evidence of commercial pressure can support that case, but the nature and extent of his actual safety responsibility must be proved rather than assumed. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/37/section/3" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, s 3 Law</a>; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/37/section/37" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, s 37 Law</a>

This is an England and Wales analysis. The prosecution should apply the Full Code Test in two stages: first, whether the admissible evidence provides a realistic prospect of conviction; second, whether prosecution is required in the public interest. A workplace death involving a potentially systemic safety failure presents a very strong public-interest case. The present issue is therefore the evidential stage and, especially, whether the evidence can safely establish the senior-management and personal-liability routes.

## 1.1 Senior management, attribution, and the divergence between the corporate and individual routes

### A. Statutory route against Meridian

For corporate manslaughter, the prosecution must prove:

1. Meridian was an organisation within the Act.
2. Meridian owed Mr Wieczorek a relevant duty of care.
3. The way Meridian’s activities were managed or organised caused his death.
4. That management or organisation amounted to a gross breach of that duty.
5. The way Meridian’s activities were managed or organised by its senior management was a substantial element in that breach.

The statutory definition is functional, not dependent upon a job title. “Senior management” comprises persons playing a significant role either in decisions about how the whole or a substantial part of the organisation’s activities are managed or organised, or in actually managing or organising those activities. A gross breach is conduct falling far below what could reasonably be expected of the organisation in the circumstances. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 1 Law</a>

Meridian was the design-and-build contractor and principal contractor at the site. The relevant duty is capable of arising both from its conduct of construction operations and, depending on the employment-status evidence, from its responsibilities to people performing services for it. The existence of that duty is a question of law for the judge after necessary factual findings. [D01, paras 3-5 and 17]; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/2" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 2 Law</a>

The evidence supporting the senior-management element is as follows.

| Evidence | Potential significance |
|---|---|
| Mr Ferris was Construction Director, held the health-and-safety portfolio, and was the reporting line for Meridian’s health-and-safety officers. There was no separate board-level health-and-safety or SHEQ director after a February 2023 restructure. | This is strong evidence that he played a significant role in the management of a substantial part of Meridian’s construction and safety activities, not merely a distant commercial role. [D06, paras 1-2] |
| Ms Kettleborough says she raised Mr Delacroix-Hale’s accumulation of roles with Mr Ferris in July and September 2023. She wrote the register annotation stating that the TWC role was to move to her when resources allowed, after a conversation with Mr Ferris; nothing then changed. | This is evidence that Ferris knew, or at least was squarely alerted to, a safety-critical organisational defect and did not correct it. [D06, paras 3-4] |
| Mr Delacroix-Hale simultaneously held the roles of Site Manager, Temporary Works Coordinator, Appointed Person for lifting, and Permit Issuing Officer. HSE describes this as a material organisational failing. | This is central to the corporate case because it is an alleged organisational system failure, rather than an isolated operational error. [D01, para 13] |
| The lifting plan named Mr Delacroix-Hale as Appointed Person. It permitted lifting until a 23 mph gust, although the crane manual set a 20 mph maximum for the relevant panel sail area. The plan was countersigned by Ferris in the “Director approval — high risk activity” box. | This is particularly important evidence of Ferris’ direct connection with management of the high-risk system that governed the incident. It also directly challenges his claim that he had no operational role. [D12, Part 3; D07, Part A] |
| On 13 November, Ferris sent a message saying that Milestone 6 had to certify by Friday or Meridian would fund the cost itself. Delacroix-Hale later reported that the “paper” was in order, to which Ferris replied “Good.” | The messages are capable of supporting an inference of commercial pressure and of Ferris’ participation in the management culture surrounding certification. They do not, by themselves, prove that he authorised unsafe work or knew of missing ties. [D11, s 1] |
| Ferris directed covert welfare-cabin monitoring after the disclosure to HSE, stating that material from it should go to him rather than the site team. | This post-incident material does not prove the original breach, but it is capable of supporting the proposition that Ferris exercised direct control over safety-related information flows and site-management responses. [D13, Part 4] |

The prosecution should not make the corporate count depend exclusively on proving that Ferris himself gave the order to load LB-7. The more sustainable case is that senior management tolerated or created a system in which:

- one person held the roles of TWC, Appointed Person and Permit Issuing Officer;
- a proper independent check and controlled certification process were absent or ineffective;
- a bay was permitted to be loaded notwithstanding an unresolved defect or, at minimum, without a reliable handover;
- safety-critical paperwork could be produced late, without a satisfactory audit trail;
- lifting controls set a threshold above the crane manufacturer’s stated limit; and
- commercial certification pressure was permitted to intrude upon operational safety decisions.

Those matters are legally relevant to grossness because the jury must consider any proved non-compliance with health-and-safety legislation, its seriousness and the risk of death it created. It may also consider the company’s policies, systems, accepted practices and attitudes where they encouraged or tolerated the failure. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/8" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 8 Law</a>

### B. Is Mr Delacroix-Hale “senior management”?

The answer is fact-sensitive. His title, Site Manager, is not decisive. The question is whether he played a significant role in managing or organising a substantial part of Meridian’s activities. The prosecution can argue that he did so at least in relation to the entire Brackenmoor project, because he had combined control of site management, temporary works, lifting authorisation and permits. [D01, para 13; D14, Part 4]

The defence will argue that he was a site-level operational manager, not an individual managing a “substantial part” of the company’s activities as a whole. That argument has force if Meridian was a much larger contractor with numerous autonomous projects, but the material presently available does not establish the size or structure of the wider construction division. The CPS should obtain Meridian’s organisational chart, delegations, board minutes, project-value data, safety-governance records and reporting lines before placing substantial weight on Delacroix-Hale as a statutory senior manager.

Ferris is the stronger candidate for statutory senior management because his own reporting structure, portfolio and signature on a high-risk lifting plan point beyond site-level supervision. [D06, paras 1-4; D12, Part 3]

### C. Bilta and Singularis: why the D16.3 attribution argument does not answer the corporate-manslaughter charge

Meridian’s response at D16.3 argues that the person who created the certificate, if anyone did, acted outside the scope of authority and that the person’s knowledge should not be attributed to Meridian. It invokes *Bilta* and *Singularis*. [D16.3]

Those authorities establish that attribution is not a mechanical exercise. The question whether to attribute an agent’s act or state of mind depends on the context and purpose of the legal claim or rule in question. [*Jetivia SA v Bilta (UK) Ltd (in liquidation)* [2015] UKSC 23 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/23); [*Singularis Holdings Ltd v Daiwa Capital Markets Europe Ltd* [2019] UKSC 50 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2019/50)

That proposition has limited force here for two reasons.

1. **The Corporate Manslaughter Act provides its own statutory attribution test.** The question is not simply whether the knowledge or fraud of a particular employee should be imputed to Meridian under an ordinary agency rule. The statutory question is whether the way the organisation’s activities were managed or organised by senior management was a substantial element in the gross breach. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 1 Law</a>

2. **The Crown’s primary case need not be that a rogue employee’s dishonest act is Meridian’s act.** The alleged defects include the company’s own staffing, role allocation, control systems, lifting-plan approval, temporary-works governance, and response to known warnings. Those are corporate-management issues even if the jury cannot identify who generated TWDC-BW-07. [D01, paras 10-15 and 18-19; D06, paras 2-8; D12, Part 3; D14, Part 4]

The defence can properly argue that a concealed fraud by Delacroix-Hale was not a senior-management act. However, that submission only gains real force if the jury accepts that Meridian had an otherwise adequate system, that Ferris had no relevant knowledge or safety-management role, and that Delacroix-Hale’s misconduct defeated rather than exposed the company’s systems. The present evidence, if accepted, permits the contrary conclusion.

### D. Corporate route versus personal routes: the precise differences

| Issue | Meridian: corporate manslaughter | Ferris: gross negligence manslaughter | Ferris: HSWA 1974, s 37 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duty | Meridian must owe the deceased a relevant duty of care. | Ferris must personally owe Mr Wieczorek a duty of care. His direct control, assumption of responsibility or operational authority must be proved. | Meridian must first have committed an underlying health-and-safety offence. |
| Breach | The relevant breach is in the way Meridian’s activities were managed or organised. | The breach must be Ferris’ own act or omission. | Ferris must have consented to, connived in, or negligently contributed to Meridian’s underlying offence. |
| Seniority | Essential: senior management’s management or organisation must be a substantial element in the breach. | Not an element. A director’s status may be evidence of duty and responsibility, but it is not sufficient. | Ferris must be a director, manager, secretary or similar officer, which he is as Construction Director. |
| Causation | The corporate management or organisation must cause death. | Ferris’ personal breach must cause death. | Causation of death is not an express statutory element of s 37, although the death makes the underlying failure and seriousness compelling. |
| Grossness | The company’s breach must fall far below reasonable corporate standards. | Ferris’ breach must be so serious, and involve a risk of death, that it is properly criminal. | No separate gross-negligence threshold, but consent, connivance or neglect must be proved. |
| Key present vulnerability | Whether Ferris and/or Delacroix-Hale can be shown to be statutory senior management and whether their management was a substantial element. | Whether Ferris knew, controlled, approved or failed to stop the unsafe work sufficiently to found an individual duty and causative gross breach. | Whether the evidence proves more than poor corporate systems and establishes Ferris’ personal consent, connivance or neglect. |

For individual gross negligence manslaughter, ordinary negligence principles determine breach and causation. The jury must then decide whether the seriousness of the breach, involving a risk of death, makes it criminal. [*R v Sellu* [2016] EWCA Crim 1716 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2016/1716)

## 1.2 Mr Pyle: contradictions, admissibility, and whether the Crown should call him

### A. The conflicts must be presented candidly

D04 and D05 are not reconcilable on their central content.

- In D04, Pyle said the scaffold was in good order, the ties were in, LB-7 was satisfactory, he made the tablet record and signed the paper handover. [D04, paras 3-5]
- In D05, he said three through-ties and façade fixings were absent, he recorded a “do not load” defect on the tablet, he did not sign the paper handover, and he believed the signature had been reproduced from another document. [D05, paras 3-7]
- In D04, Pyle said he was on site all morning on 14 November. [D04, para 10]
- In D05, he said he did not arrive until about 11:00, and that he had arranged for Aaron Vasilenko-Pratt to present his card earlier. [D05, paras 9-10]

D05 is also not wholly corroborated by D13 Part 1. The turnstile log records Pyle’s card at 06:48 and Vasilenko-Pratt’s card at 06:49. However, the system used card presentation only: biometric verification was disabled, anti-passback was disabled, it did not photograph cardholders, and tailgating was not detected. The entry proves presentation of Pyle’s card, not Pyle’s physical presence. It is therefore consistent with his D05 account only if another person presented his card, but it does not prove that account. [D13, Part 1]

The additional anomaly that Vasilenko-Pratt’s card was presented twice, 0.8 seconds apart, does not establish who presented Pyle’s card or whether Pyle himself attended early. It should not be overstated. [D13, Part 1]

D11 independently records a message at 08:02 saying that Gareth was at the depot and would be in at 11:00. That is capable of supporting D05’s broad timing account, but it was written by Mr Braithwaite-Oyelaran, who was present for the D04 statement and is therefore not an independent source free from the same potential interest. [D11, s 1; D04, heading and para 13; D05, para 2]

### B. How the Crown can call Pyle and use both statements

The Crown is not prohibited from calling a witness merely because he has contradicted himself, has been cautioned, or is potentially implicated in misconduct. The Crown must, however:

- disclose both statements, the payments, the caution, the presence and conduct of Braithwaite-Oyelaran, and all material affecting credibility;
- not present D05 as reliable without confronting the contrary D04 account;
- not knowingly advance a case it considers false; and
- make a principled assessment that Pyle’s live evidence, tested in open court, assists rather than distorts the search for truth.

If Pyle gives oral evidence and admits making the earlier inconsistent D04 statement, D04 is admissible as evidence of the truth of matters stated in it, not merely as an attack on credibility. The same applies if the previous inconsistent statement is proved under the 1865 Act procedure. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/119" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 119 Law</a>

If the Crown calls him and he resiles from D05, it must first seek the judge’s leave to treat him as adverse. Before proving the earlier inconsistent statement, the Crown must identify the relevant occasion and circumstances to Pyle and ask whether he made it. The Crown may then contradict him by other evidence and, with leave, prove the inconsistent statement. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1865/18/section/3" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Procedure Act 1865, s 3 Law</a>

Section 120 CJA 2003 is not the primary route for the D04/D05 contradiction. Its main relevance is narrower:

- if a prior statement is admitted to rebut an allegation that the witness fabricated his oral evidence, its contents can become substantive evidence;
- where a witness refreshes memory from a document and is cross-examined on it, that document may become substantive evidence; and
- particular prior statements made while events were fresh may become substantive evidence if the statutory conditions are fulfilled. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/120" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 120 Law</a>

The Crown should rely principally on s 119 and the 1865 Act procedure, not attempt to use s 120 as a mechanism to bypass Pyle’s central credibility difficulties.

### C. D04: the presence of Mr Braithwaite-Oyelaran

The circumstances of D04 need careful handling. Pyle requested that Braithwaite-Oyelaran remain. He was twice asked whether he wanted him to leave and declined. The inspector noted that Pyle was visibly distressed at paragraphs 6 and 14. [D04, heading and note]

That does not establish coercion. It does, however, make D05’s allegation material: he says that, before the inspector arrived, Braithwaite-Oyelaran said “Keep it simple,” which Pyle interpreted as an instruction, and that he feared for his job and professional card. [D05, paras 1-2]

The Crown should call the inspector to prove the neutral circumstances of D04, including:

- Pyle’s request that Braithwaite-Oyelaran remain;
- the inspector’s two enquiries as to whether he wanted him to leave;
- Pyle’s demeanour;
- any contemporaneous note of precisely what was said before and during the statement; and
- whether Braithwaite-Oyelaran ever spoke, prompted, interrupted or communicated non-verbally.

The Crown should also interview Braithwaite-Oyelaran under caution if that has not already happened, obtain his telephone and messaging material, and investigate whether he knew of the tablet record, the defect, the paper handover or the later payments.

### D. Payments and motive

Pyle disclosed payments of £2,500 in January 2024 and £2,000 in February 2024 from Arbuthnot Slade Investigations, said to be instructed by solicitors acting for the developer. He describes them as consultancy fees and denies being told what to say. He failed initially to disclose them when agreeing to make his further statement. TowerLine also owed him £6,340 in wages and holiday pay. [D05, paras 13-15]

Those payments create a serious and obvious line of cross-examination:

- Were the payments genuinely for consultancy work?
- What work was done, when, and for whom?
- Were the payments linked to a prospective civil claim or Coldbrook’s commercial position?
- What did the investigators ask Pyle about the accident, the inspection record and Meridian?
- Did they disclose the payments to police, HSE or the CPS immediately?
- Did Pyle receive any promise of help in the TowerLine liquidation?
- Was any consideration, immunity or assistance agreement discussed beyond the recorded explanation of the possibility of assistance under SOCPA 2005?

The D05 note records that no immunity was offered. That should be proved by the solicitor and investigator records, not simply asserted. [D05, note]

The question also refers to **D01, para 25**. The supplied extract of D01 available for this advice contains paragraphs 1-21 but not paragraph 25. I therefore cannot safely state the content of D01, para 25. Before final charge, the CPS should retrieve and review it, identify whether it contains a further account of the Pyle payments or their disclosure, and disclose it as material bearing on credibility. It would be unsafe to characterise that paragraph without its text.

### E. Should the Crown call Pyle?

**Recommendation: the Crown can properly call Pyle, but only if it decides that his evidence is necessary and it deploys him as an openly compromised witness, not as a witness whose evidence is accepted unreservedly.**

The proper opening position would be:

1. Pyle made two materially incompatible accounts.
2. He had potential motives affecting each account: perceived pressure from TowerLine for D04, and later payments and financial distress for D05.
3. The jury must determine whether either account is true, partly true, or neither.
4. The objective evidence should be used to test, rather than merely repeat, his narrative.

The Crown should not call him merely to introduce D05 as a document if it anticipates that he will refuse to confirm it or will assert privilege against self-incrimination. He should be called only if the prosecutor has a good-faith basis to expect meaningful live evidence and cross-examination.

If Pyle refuses to answer material questions, the Crown must consider whether any hearsay route is lawfully available, whether admission would be fair, and whether the case should proceed without him. That issue cannot be resolved from the bundle alone.

### F. What survives if Pyle is not called?

A substantial corporate case survives, though the proof of specific prior warning to Delacroix-Hale becomes weaker.

The surviving evidence includes:

- the HSE survey finding that the three specified through-ties were absent and that there were no anchor installations or drilled holes at the relevant positions; [D01, para 10]
- Professor Danecourt’s agreement that the absence of the three ties is established and not seriously arguable; [D20.1, para 1]
- the TowerLine tablet extraction, showing a locally committed 13 November record identifying missing ties and directing that LB-7 not be loaded; [D08, Part 2]
- the forensic provenance of that tablet record, including its local commit time, failed synchronisation and absence of post-commit edits; [D08, Part 2]
- the absence of any record of remedial work, materials requisition or operative carrying out a repair; [D08, Part 3]
- the questionable provenance and apparently reproduced Pyle signature on the paper Version A record; [D08, Part 1]
- the absence of a validly completed permit acceptance and absence of a recorded resumption authority; [D14, Part 4]
- the certificate metadata, absence from Halbrook’s register, lack of a job number and lack of a digital signature; [D09, Parts 1-3]
- the email in which Ashgrove-Pike stated that no panel should be used until a certificate was obtained and the tie schedule signed off; [D09, Part 4]
- the evidence of a defective lifting plan and the contemporaneous messages about the bay and commercial certification; [D11, s 1; D12, Part 3]
- the machine-generated crane record showing a resumed lift before the collapse. [D14, Part 2]

Without Pyle, the Crown should avoid asserting that a particular person was directly told by Pyle that three ties were missing, unless another admissible source establishes it. The Crown can still contend that Meridian’s systems permitted the unsafe bay to be used and that objective records support the existence of a defect.

## 1.3 Suspension and resumption of lifting: timing and authority

### A. What can safely be found

The strongest available reconstruction is:

1. Lifting was suspended at or about **10:40**.
2. Lifting resumed at **11:13:26**.
3. The resumed lift remained engaged when the collapse occurred at **11:22:04**.
4. Ms Kettleborough did not authorise the resumption.
5. The evidence points to Mr Delacroix-Hale as the person most likely to have directed the final lift, but the bundle does not prove the identity of the authorising person conclusively.

The site daily log records “Lifting ops SUSPENDED - high wind. Instruction of FK” at 10:40. It also contains a legible, but struck-through, 11:05 entry stating “Lifting ops resumed following reassessment.” [D14, Part 1]

Ms Kettleborough says that she verbally instructed Delacroix-Hale to suspend lifting at 10:40, recorded this in the daily log herself, and did not authorise resumption. She says she later heard the crane and sent the 11:19 group message asking whether lifting was still occurring. [D06, paras 9-15]

The group messages corroborate much of that account. At 10:41, Kettleborough posted that lifting was suspended and confirmed to Ruaridh in the cabin. Delacroix-Hale replied “Noted” at 10:44. At 11:06, Vasilenko-Pratt asked whether the crane was slewing again; at 11:07, Delacroix-Hale responded, “Last one then we’re off.” [D11, s 1]

Mr Aksoy independently recalls a stoppage, followed by lifting restarting. He could not identify who authorised it. He recalls a radio voice he thought might have been Delacroix-Hale saying words to the effect of “one more and we’re done for the morning,” but he expressly cannot be certain of the speaker. [D03, paras 10-12]

### B. The least susceptible evidence

The **crane’s automatic data logger** is least susceptible to challenge on the question whether lifting stopped and restarted. It is machine-generated, was downloaded by the crane-hire company, and does not depend on a later human recollection or manuscript entry. It shows:

- no hook load from 10:39:47 until 11:13:26;
- a new lift, L-12, engaging at 11:13:26;
- that lift not being released before the collapse; and
- a shock-load/anti-two-block alarm at 11:22:04. [D14, Part 2]

This evidence strongly corroborates the fact of suspension and resumption. It does **not** identify who gave authority to resume. It proves the event, not the decision-maker.

The daily log is useful but vulnerable: the 11:05 resumption entry was struck through without the required initials, date or explanation. [D14, Part 1] The manual anemometer log is vulnerable because the instrument was not identified by serial number, no calibration certificate was produced, it was not recovered, entries were in one hand and ink including the post-collapse entry, readings were taken at ground level rather than working level, and it omitted any suspension entry. [D12, Part 2]

The Met Office record is independent and certified, but it is not a direct measurement at LB-7. It expressly contains a location-and-height caveat. Its value is therefore strongest as corroborative meteorological context, not as a direct substitute for a deck-level measurement. [D12, Part 1]

### C. Whose authority?

The lifting plan required an express authority from the Appointed Person, recorded in the daily log, before resumption. It named Delacroix-Hale as Appointed Person. [D12, Part 3]

The prosecution can argue that Delacroix-Hale was the probable de facto authoriser because:

- he was the named Appointed Person; [D12, Part 3]
- he was told of the suspension and replied “Noted”; [D11, s 1]
- shortly before resumption he stated, “Last one then we’re off”; [D11, s 1]
- he held multiple relevant control roles; [D01, para 13]
- the daily log’s original resumption entry bears his initials, albeit it was later struck through; and [D14, Part 1]
- he issued the permit to load LB-7. [D14, Part 4]

The defence can contend that:

- the log does not record an express authority to resume;
- the struck-through 11:05 entry is procedurally defective;
- the actual lift recommenced at 11:13:26 rather than 11:05;
- the group message may describe, rather than authorise, a decision taken by someone else; and
- Aksoy cannot reliably identify the radio speaker. [D03, paras 10-12; D14, Parts 1-2]

The correct prosecution formulation is therefore not “it is proved that Delacroix-Hale expressly authorised resumption.” It is: **the objective timing evidence proves resumption; the contemporaneous communications and role allocation provide a strong circumstantial case that Delacroix-Hale authorised or directed it; the required formal record is missing.**

There is presently no equivalent direct evidence that Ferris authorised resumption on the morning of 14 November. His approval of the lifting plan is relevant to the pre-existing system, but it is not proof that he gave the final operational instruction. [D12, Part 3; D07, Part A]

## 1.4 Causation: Dr Vantrease and Professor Danecourt

### A. The legal question

For both corporate manslaughter and gross negligence manslaughter, the prosecution must prove that the alleged breach caused the death. The breach need not be the sole cause. The critical question is whether it made a significant contribution to death, rather than a merely minimal or insignificant one. For individual gross negligence manslaughter, the jury must first find duty, breach and causation, then decide whether the breach was so grave, involving a risk of death, that it should be treated as criminal. [*R v Sellu* [2016] EWCA Crim 1716 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2016/1716)

### B. Dr Vantrease’s case

Dr Vantrease’s opinion is that the missing ties were the primary cause, the 1,410 kg loading against a 750 kg design load was a secondary cause, and wind was not a cause. She considers that a correctly tied and correctly loaded bay would have survived a 43 mph gust with a margin of safety greater than 2.0. She concludes that, with ties installed and the actual load, the bay probably would not have collapsed; and with absent ties and a 750 kg load, it would probably have collapsed within days. [D19, paras 1.1-1.5]

If accepted, that analysis strongly supports the Crown. It permits a jury to conclude that:

- the absence of ties was a substantial and operative cause;
- the excessive load was a substantial and operative cause;
- a proper temporary-works system would have stopped the bay being used; and
- the lifting decision was at most the occasion for the failure, rather than an intervening cause.

However, Dr Vantrease’s opinion is vulnerable on several factual and procedural bases. She did not inspect the tablet extraction, assumed that Rev C was the controlled drawing, relied on the HSE survey and selected photographs rather than personally verifying original component positions, and assumed that the Aksoy recording was accurate. [D19, paras 2.1-2.5] Her undisclosed financial links and success-uplift provision are also likely to be deployed against her impartiality and weight, although those issues are principally for the admissibility and professional-conduct analysis. [D19, Appendix G]

### C. Professor Danecourt’s analysis

Professor Danecourt agrees that the three through-ties were absent and says that is not seriously arguable. She rejects the proposition that wind was not a cause. On her dynamic analysis, a fully tied bay loaded to 1,410 kg in a 43 mph gust may have been at or beyond ultimate capacity. [D20.1, paras 1-5]

Her key conclusion is that the collapse resulted from the combination of three conditions: missing ties, overload and wind. She says none was individually sufficient, that removing any one would probably have allowed the bay to survive that morning, and that she cannot rank the conditions. [D20.1, para 6]

This does **not** defeat the Crown’s causation case. Properly understood, it assists the Crown against Meridian:

- If removing the missing ties would probably have avoided collapse that morning, their absence was causative.
- If removing the overload would probably have avoided collapse that morning, loading was causative.
- If not resuming the lift in the prevailing wind would probably have avoided collapse that morning, resumption was causative.

The “coincidence of three conditions” formulation is therefore not an assertion that no condition caused the death. It is a multiple-causation opinion. It supports the proposition that each condition made a more-than-minimal contribution to the fatal event.

It is particularly damaging to Meridian’s anticipated “wind alone” case because Danecourt expressly agrees that the ties were absent, agrees the 2,000 kg figure was plainly wrong and irreconcilable with a reconstructable calculation, and agrees that the concentration of the three temporary-works roles was indefensible. [D20.1, paras 1, 7 and 9]

### D. Where Danecourt assists Ferris and where she assists the Crown

Danecourt is more helpful to Ferris on the **individual manslaughter** count than on the corporate count. She says that, if she is right, the decision to resume lifting at 11:13 was itself capable of causing the collapse of a compliant bay. That shifts immediate causal focus towards the on-site operational decision, apparently connected to Delacroix-Hale, rather than the earlier corporate approval of the lifting plan. [D20.1, paras 4-6]

But it does not exonerate Ferris if the prosecution can prove that his conduct included approving a defective high-risk plan, failing to correct known role-concentration concerns, or exerting pressure for certification such that unsafe systems were tolerated. Those would remain causally relevant in a corporate case, and potentially in a s 37 case. [D06, paras 2-4; D11, s 1; D12, Part 3]

The principal difficulty for a Ferris gross-negligence manslaughter charge is not merely general causation. It is the more exact question: **what personal act or omission of Ferris, rather than the acts of Delacroix-Hale or Meridian’s system generally, caused the death?** The present material does not answer that question securely enough for a personal manslaughter charge.

## 1.5 Ferris’ interview silence, deletions, factory reset, and further enquiries

### A. Section 34 CJPOA 1994

Section 34 permits a jury to draw such inferences as appear proper where an accused, when questioned under caution before charge, fails to mention a fact later relied on in defence which he could reasonably have been expected to mention at the time. It is not an automatic adverse-inference provision and does not displace the prosecution’s burden of proof. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/33/section/34" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994, s 34 Law</a>

Ferris gave a prepared statement, answered some questions, and gave “no comment” answers to others. His solicitor’s note records that the forensic extraction report, access-control data and photograph metadata had not been provided in advance despite a written request. [D07, Part A and Part B; D07, solicitor’s note]

The Crown should distinguish carefully between categories of silence.

#### 1. No adverse inference should be sought where non-disclosure made an answer unfair or unrealistic

Ferris was asked to comment on forensic-report material that his solicitor said he had not seen. He expressly responded that he was not obliged to comment on a report he had not seen. [D07, Part B]

The prosecution should be cautious about inviting an inference from that silence. Whether a fact could reasonably have been expected to be mentioned depends on the circumstances at the time. If the defence was denied material necessary to answer a technical allegation, the jury may regard silence as equivocal.

The CPS should obtain:

- the disclosure schedule;
- the defence request of 26 September 2024;
- the material actually supplied before interview;
- the timing of service of the forensic reports;
- the full interview audio and transcript; and
- a statement from the interviewing officer and, if necessary, the solicitor as to what was explained.

#### 2. An inference may be available if Ferris advances a new factual explanation at trial

The position changes if Ferris later relies on a specific factual explanation which he could reasonably have given at interview. Examples may include:

- a detailed assertion that his driver used the visitor badge on 14 November;
- a detailed account of where he was at 09:58;
- a specific innocent explanation for deletion of the five messages;
- a specific explanation for disabling backup and enabling disappearing messages; or
- a specific explanation for the 2,000 kg certificate.

At present he did advance some explanations:

- the visitor badge was habitually used by his driver; [D07, Part B]
- the image might have been transferred to his phone and later uploaded; [D07, Part B]
- he was in Manchester and stayed at the Radisson; [D07, Part B]
- he wiped a telephone before a trade-in; [D07, Part B]
- his files-tidying comment meant completing, not destroying, documents. [D07, Part B]

Those explanations are already before the jury. Section 34 cannot be used to criticise him for failing to mention facts he in fact mentioned.

### B. Deleted messages and reset phone: evidential status

The deleted-message evidence is potentially powerful but must be put accurately.

The forensic examiner reports that five messages sent from the Ferris account on 13 November were deleted for all participants at 22:07 on 14 November. The group’s disappearing-messages setting was enabled at 22:03, and backup was disabled at 21:58. The examiner says this pattern was not consistent with routine housekeeping in his experience, while making no finding about the reason for the deletions. [D11, paras 2.1-2.6]

The phone attributed to Ferris was factory-reset at 06:12 on 22 November, before execution of the search warrant at 07:40, and contained no recoverable user data. [D11, para 2.2; D07, Part B]

This material is admissible circumstantial evidence capable of supporting an inference of consciousness of guilt or of an attempt to prevent scrutiny. However, it does **not** prove:

- the content of the deleted messages;
- that Ferris personally operated the account or device at the relevant moments;
- that the deleted messages concerned LB-7 or the safety certificate; or
- that the reset was necessarily dishonest rather than connected to an intended phone upgrade.

The examiner expressly says that device attribution is based on account registration and associated conventions, and does not prove physical composition of any particular message. [D11, para 4.1] The evidence becomes substantially stronger if the CPS can obtain corroboration from carrier records, device-management records, cloud-account audit logs, Apple account data, contemporaneous trade-in communications, and evidence of who had physical possession of the phone.

The deleted messages also gain relevance from their context. Before the deletions, Delacroix-Hale said “Sorted. Paper is in order” in reply to one of the deleted messages, and Ferris replied “Good.” The parent message’s content is unavailable. [D11, ss 1 and 2.5] The Crown can argue that the missing exchange may have concerned documentation, but cannot invite the jury to speculate about its content as though it had been proved.

### C. Missing CCTV

The absence of the three cameras covering the relevant east-elevation area is important. Meridian said cameras 3, 4 and 7 were not recording because of an NVR fault. Yet its maintenance ticket says the fault was resolved on 3 November, eleven days before the collapse. The cameras missing are precisely those covering the collapse area. [D13, Part 3]

That discrepancy is capable of supporting an inference that Meridian’s disclosure and preservation processes were defective, and potentially that evidence was lost or withheld. It does not, without further evidence, prove deliberate destruction by Ferris or any other person.

## Charging recommendation

### Meridian Structures Limited

**Charge corporate manslaughter, subject to completing the targeted enquiries above.** There is already a realistic prospect of conviction if the jury accepts the physical evidence, the objective digital and crane evidence, and the senior-management/system case. The prosecution should plead the gross breach as cumulative organisational failure, not as a one-person fraud case. [D01, paras 10-15 and 18-19; D06, paras 1-8; D11, s 1; D12, Part 3; D14, Parts 2 and 4; D20.1, paras 1 and 7]

### Aldwyn Ferris: gross negligence manslaughter

**Do not presently charge.** There is evidence capable of disproving Ferris’ claimed distance from the project, but the evidential gap remains too large on personal duty, actual knowledge, personal causation and the grossness of his individual conduct. The missing enquiries may materially change that assessment. [D07, Part A and Part B; D11, ss 1-2; D12, Part 3; D13, Parts 1-2; D20.1, paras 4-6]

### Aldwyn Ferris: HSWA 1974, s 37

**Keep under active consideration; likely chargeable after further governance and knowledge enquiries.** The corporate underlying case under HSWA 1974, s 3 is substantial because Meridian’s undertaking exposed non-employees at the site to safety risks. The question is whether the company offence was committed with Ferris’ consent or connivance or through his neglect. His portfolio, the prior role-concentration warnings, high-risk approval signature, commercial-pressure message and apparent management of post-incident information provide an evidential basis, but should be corroborated through the further enquiry programme. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/37/section/3" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">D01, paras 3-5, 13 and 17-19; D06, paras 2-4; D11, s 1; D12, Part 3; Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, s 3 Law</a>; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/37/section/37" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, s 37 Law</a>

**Controlling factual fork:** can the CPS obtain reliable evidence that Ferris personally knew of the LB-7 defect or false certification, or that he personally directed or authorised the unsafe operational decision on 14 November?
