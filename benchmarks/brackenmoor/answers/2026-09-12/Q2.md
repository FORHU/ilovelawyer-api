# Advice for the TCC Trial: Commercial and Insurance Consequences

## Overall conclusions

1. **Payment:** Meridian has a strong contractual claim to the £1,842,500 applied for in Interim Application 18. Coldbrook’s Pay Less Notice was served two days after the contractual deadline. Its “five clear days” construction is arithmetically untenable on the dates in the bundle. The substantial counterweight is that the £610,000 Milestone 6 element was allegedly supported by a forged certificate, and the Court may refuse to permit the payment regime to be used to enforce an application infected by proven fraud. The separate “invalid application” case cannot presently be determined because clause 4.7.4 and the application attachments said to be missing are not contained in the retrieved material. The agent’s 30 November certificate is highly significant evidence against Coldbrook’s immediate fraud case, but is not necessarily conclusive if it was itself procured by deception.

2. **Termination:** The purported termination under clause 8.4.1 was ineffective: the default notice was not emailed despite clause 1.7 saying that a notice not emailed “shall be of no effect”; and, independently, 19 December was premature because the stated fourteen-day cure period did not expire until 22 December. Coldbrook’s potentially viable route is the immediate-fraud termination power in clause 8.4.4. That route depends on proving that, on 19 December, Coldbrook actually held, and objectively could reasonably hold, the contractual belief that Meridian had acted fraudulently. The five-month-later metadata report cannot itself establish the reasonableness of a belief held on 19 December, but the precise allegations in the 8 December notice may evidence an earlier evidential basis. The common-law acceptance alternative is likely excluded by clause 8.1’s unamended “sole means” wording.

3. **Caldera coverage:** Caldera has serious arguments, particularly if it proves a deliberate or reckless pre-contractual misrepresentation and proves what it would have done absent that breach. Its best claims concern the proposal’s statements about checking levels, safety management, and the board-level safety role. Meridian’s strongest answer is the 4 September broker email, sent to both Mr Pilbeam and underwriting support, coupled with Mr Pilbeam’s same-day file note. That evidence is capable of establishing Caldera’s actual or deemed knowledge of the Category 2 checking arrangement and the abolition of the SHEQ Director role. General Condition 7 is likely to remain available in principle, but Meridian is unlikely to satisfy the statutory requirement that its non-compliance could not have increased the risk of this very loss. General Condition 19 faces a substantial statutory transparency obstacle insofar as it puts Meridian in a worse position than the Insurance Act’s fraud regime.

4. **Mr Aksoy and the dependants:** The best practical course is to pursue both Meridian directly and TowerLine’s insurer through the Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010. The direct Meridian claim is materially stronger than its “independent contractor” defence suggests because Meridian was the principal contractor, controlled the construction-phase safety system, and the alleged failures concern functions that it retained: temporary-works control, permits, loading, lifting, site induction and coordination. Mr Aksoy’s personal-injury limitation deadline is likely **14 November 2026**, absent a later date of knowledge. The Fatal Accidents Act claim is likely subject to the same date unless a later relevant date of knowledge is established. Proceedings should be protected immediately, if they have not already been validly issued.

## Bundle inconsistencies and evidential cautions

The Court should not resolve the following matters by assuming that one document is correct.

### 1. The Pay Less Notice timing contention

Coldbrook says that service on 12 December complied with a requirement for “five clear days excluding the day of service and the final date”. Yet the final date was 15 December. Excluding 12 and 15 December leaves only 13 and 14 December: two clear days, not five. The contractual chronology itself identifies 10 December as the last day for a Pay Less Notice. This is not a fine point of construction but an irreconcilable arithmetic defect in Coldbrook’s pleaded timing analysis. [D15 Part 2, pp 1-2; D15 Part 3, p 2]

### 2. Certification versus alleged falsification

Vellacott Marr, Coldbrook’s Employer’s Agent, signed a Milestone 6 certificate on 30 November 2023 confirming that loading platforms were complete and independently certified. [D15 Part 2, p 1] Yet the certificate later relied upon for LB-7, TWDC-BW-07, had a blank Temporary Works Coordinator countersignature field and job-number field, lacked a digital signature, contained a typed name rather than a verifiable signature, and reported a 2,000 kg working load. [D09 Part 1, p 1] Halbrook’s register has no LB-7 entry, job number, invoice or digital signature, and its managing director says Halbrook did not issue it. [D09 Part 3, p 3]

There is no necessary contradiction if the Employer’s Agent was deceived or certified on incomplete information. Conversely, its certification is evidence that a person acting for Coldbrook was then satisfied that the milestone conditions had been met. The Court must decide what the agent was shown, what it understood, and whether any fraud was attributable to Meridian rather than an unauthorised individual.

### 3. The date and provenance of TWDC-BW-07

The document is dated 27 October 2023, but its metadata identifies a 13 November 2023 creation date, records Meridian’s r.delacroix-hale account as author, and identifies a template modified shortly before creation. [D09 Part 2, pp 1-2] The forensic examiner nevertheless expressly says that filesystem and PDF dates can be manipulated and cannot exclude the possibility that it was retyped from a genuine earlier certificate that has since been lost. [D09 Part 2, p 2] That is strong circumstantial evidence of fabrication, not conclusive proof.

### 4. The alleged “nine months” of defence funding

The question refers to nine months of funding with full knowledge. The chronology says Caldera funded the defence continuously from December 2023 to January 2025, approximately fourteen months, and reserved rights on 2 February 2025. [D17 Part 4, p 3] The nine-month period appears instead to be the approximate interval from the metadata report served on 6 May 2024 to the reservation of rights letter. [D17 Part 4, p 3] The legal significance depends on **what Caldera knew, when**, and which coverage defence is in issue.

# 2.1 Meridian’s entitlement to £1,842,500

## Conclusion

Subject to the fraud and valid-application issues, Meridian is contractually entitled to the full £1,842,500 because Coldbrook gave neither a payment notice by 6 December nor a timely Pay Less Notice by 10 December. Clause 4.9.5 states that, where neither notice is given, the sum in the contractor’s Interim Application is the sum due. [D15 Part 1, pp 1-2]

The strongest point against that conclusion is that £610,000 of the notified amount was expressly claimed for Milestone 6 and the milestone application may have depended on a certificate which, on Coldbrook’s case, was fabricated. If that is proved, the Court may regard the payment claim as tainted by fraud rather than merely as an overvaluation dispute.

## A. The contractual payment timetable

The material dates are:

| Event | Date | Consequence |
|---|---:|---|
| Interim Valuation Date | 24 November 2023 | Contractual valuation date |
| Meridian’s IA/18 | 24 November 2023 | Claimed £1,842,500, including £610,000 for Milestone 6 |
| Due date | 1 December 2023 | Seven days after valuation date |
| Payment Notice deadline | 6 December 2023 | No notice was given |
| Pay Less Notice deadline | 10 December 2023 | Five days before final date |
| Pay Less Notice | 12 December 2023 | Served late |
| Final date for payment | 15 December 2023 | No payment made |

[D15 Parts 1-3, pp 1-2]

The amendment says that a Pay Less Notice must be given “not later than 5 days before the final date for payment”. [D15 Part 1, p 1] The final date was 15 December. [D15 Part 2, p 2]

The ordinary contractual reading, supported by the chronology agreed for the bundle, is that the last permissible date was 10 December. [D15 Part 2, p 2] A notice dated and emailed on 12 December, and received in hard copy on 13 December, therefore came too late. [D15 Part 3, p 2]

The later authority retrieved on payment-notice construction confirms that the question is objective, contextual and practical; a valid payment notice must, in substance, state the relevant sum and calculation basis, and validity is commonly one of fact and degree. It also confirms that contractual requirements more restrictive than statutory requirements must be observed. <a href="https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/tcc/2026/1530" class="legal-ref jurisprudence" target="_blank">*Oakland Wantage Care Home Ltd v Stepnell Ltd* Jurisprudence</a>

That assists Coldbrook as to the **content** of PLN/1: it clearly identifies a nil sum and sets out four bases for it, namely the Milestone 6 objection, temporary-works cross-claims, liquidated damages and loss from the incident. [D15 Part 3, p 2] But it does not overcome a mandatory timing failure.

## B. The five-day calculation

Coldbrook’s argument is not merely weak; on the pleaded dates it is mathematically impossible.

If the day of service, 12 December, and the final payment date, 15 December, are both excluded, the only intervening dates are 13 and 14 December. There are two clear days.

If the final date is included, there are three days: 13, 14 and 15 December.

If service day is included, there are four days: 12, 13, 14 and 15 December.

None yields five days.

The better submission is therefore:

1. “Five days before” fixes 10 December as the last day.
2. No wording says “five clear days”.
3. A clear-days construction, if adopted, would move the deadline earlier rather than later.
4. The contract’s own contemporaneous sequence records 10 December as the deadline. [D15 Part 2, p 2]

## C. The Grove and Bexheat line

Meridian’s reliance on the Grove/Bexheat line is sound at the level of principle: a failure to serve a compliant notice normally has immediate cash-flow consequences, leaving the employer to advance the substantive valuation and cross-claim issues through the appropriate later process rather than unilaterally withholding the notified sum.

The principle should not be overstated. It does not transform a demonstrably fraudulent claim into an enforceable entitlement. Nor does it necessarily prevent the Court from managing a fraud dispute where compelling evidence is unavailable at the notice stage.

The Court of Appeal’s decision in *Gosvenor London Ltd v Aygun Aluminium UK Ltd* confirms that, when fraud is invoked to resist enforcement or seek a stay, the Court must examine the material overlap between evidence available in the adjudication and the evidence later invoked to resist enforcement. <a href="https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2018/2695" class="legal-ref jurisprudence" target="_blank">*Gosvenor London Ltd v Aygun Aluminium UK Ltd* Jurisprudence</a> That is relevant by analogy. Coldbrook cannot simply withhold payment because fraud is later alleged; the Court will ask what Coldbrook knew, could have deployed, and whether its current fraud evidence is materially different.

## D. Fraud: SG South and Gosvenor

Coldbrook’s fraud case is substantial on the present materials:

- the certificate said on its face to be dated 27 October 2023; [D09 Part 1, p 1]
- the forensic material places its creation on 13 November 2023; [D09 Part 2, pp 1-2]
- the file identifies a Meridian-domain author; [D09 Part 2, p 1]
- the underlying template was modified under the same account shortly beforehand; [D09 Part 2, p 1]
- Halbrook has no job number, invoice or certificate issue record for LB-7; [D09 Part 3, p 3]
- the alleged certificate’s 2,000 kg capacity conflicts with the 750 kg capacity on the authenticated current drawing. [D01 para 11, p 2; D09 Part 5, p 3]

But it remains an allegation requiring proof. The metadata expert does not exclude an earlier genuine certificate later retyped, though he had seen no evidence of one. [D09 Part 2, p 2] Halbrook’s former checker is outside the jurisdiction, has declined to provide a signed statement, and has not been cross-examined. [D09 Part 4, p 3]

### Effect on the £1,842,500

There are two possible analyses.

**Analysis 1: fraud not proved, or not attributable to Meridian.**  
Meridian should recover the notified sum. Coldbrook’s late notice prevents it from withholding on the basis of valuation, defects, delay damages or unquantified incident losses. Coldbrook’s substantive claims may remain available in the main TCC claim or adjudication under clause 9.2. [D15 Part 1, p 1; D15 Part 3, p 2]

**Analysis 2: fraud proved and materially connected with IA/18.**  
The Court may refuse to permit Meridian to enforce at least the £610,000 Milestone 6 element, and possibly the payment application as a whole if the fraud is found to have been deployed to secure the payment machinery. The better view is that the Court should distinguish the £610,000 certification-dependent component from otherwise independently due work, unless the evidence establishes that the entire IA/18 was a fraudulent presentation.

The strongest point against Meridian is that the milestone was expressly included in IA/18, and the contract required both relevant Category 2 check certificates and the Employer’s Agent’s satisfaction. [D15 Part 1, p 1; D15 Part 2, p 1] If the evidence shows Meridian knowingly produced a fabricated certificate to obtain that milestone, the “pay now, argue later” rationale has sharply reduced force.

## E. Coldbrook’s alternative: no valid application

Coldbrook says IA/18 was invalid because it was not accompanied by substantiation required by clause 4.7.4. [D15 Part 3, p 2] Meridian says this point was not taken at the time. [D15 Part 3, p 2]

This issue cannot safely be resolved on the material presently available because:

- clause 4.7.4 is not included in the displayed contractual extracts; and
- the actual contents and attachments of IA/18 are not set out in the retrieved text.

The Court will need to decide:

1. whether clause 4.7.4 imposed a condition of validity or merely an evidential obligation;
2. exactly what IA/18 contained when served;
3. whether any alleged missing material was required for Coldbrook to identify the sum due;
4. whether Coldbrook’s conduct, including the later certification by its agent, bears on waiver, estoppel or contractual construction.

Absent clear language making substantiation a condition precedent to a valid application, this is likely a difficult route for Coldbrook. It cannot rely on a technical deficiency while its Employer’s Agent subsequently certified the milestone, unless it proves that the certification was itself induced by the same deception.

## F. Significance of the 30 November certificate

The Employer’s Agent signed the Milestone 6 certificate on 30 November, after IA/18 on 24 November but before the due date and payment-notice deadline. [D15 Part 2, p 1]

It assists Meridian in four ways:

1. **Substantive entitlement:** the milestone condition includes the Employer’s Agent being satisfied. The certificate is direct evidence that this element was met. [D15 Part 1, p 1; D15 Part 2, p 1]

2. **Reasonableness and knowledge:** it makes Coldbrook’s claim that it reasonably believed the milestone had no basis harder to establish.

3. **Invalid-application argument:** it weakens the suggestion that the application was so deficient that no payment obligation could arise.

4. **Estoppel/waiver case:** it supplies factual material for Meridian’s pleaded position that Coldbrook acted on a shared assumption or waived objections. [D15 Part 3, p 3]

It is not, however, a complete answer to fraud. The certificate may be voidable in effect if obtained by deceit. The Court will need the evidence of the agent’s inspection, the material supplied to it, its knowledge of the accident, and its role for both Coldbrook and the lender. The dual-role conflict allegation and the claimed but undisclosed information barrier are material to the weight of the certificate. [D15 Part 3, p 3]

# 2.2 Effectiveness of the 19 December termination

## Conclusion

Termination under clause 8.4.1 failed. The default notice was contractually ineffective because it was not emailed, and the termination was in any event premature. Coldbrook’s only serious contractual answer is clause 8.4.4. On the current evidence, clause 8.4.4 is arguable but factually precarious because the strongest forensic evidence of falsification was not served until 6 May 2024.

The strongest point against Meridian is that the 8 December default notice alleged a detailed and specific fraud case, including that the certificate had been created on or about 13 November. That may enable Coldbrook to show that it held an objectively reasonable belief before the later formal metadata report was served. [D16 Document 16.1, p 1]

## A. Clause 8.4.1: defective default notice

Clause 8.4.1 permits a notice of specified default and termination only if the contractor continues that default for fourteen days from receipt. [D15 Part 1, p 1]

Clause 1.7 requires notices to be:

- in writing;
- delivered by hand or recorded delivery;
- **and additionally** sent by email to the stated address.

It adds that a notice not emailed in accordance with the clause “shall be of no effect”. [D15 Part 1, p 1]

The default notice was delivered by hand on 8 December but not emailed. [D16 Document 16.1, p 1; D16 Document 16.4, p 2] That is fatal to reliance on clause 8.4.1. The word “shall”, the cumulative “and additionally”, and the express “of no effect” consequence make this a mandatory condition, not a merely directory service provision.

Coldbrook’s argument that email was directory gives insufficient weight to the final sentence. That sentence identifies the agreed contractual consequence of non-compliance.

## B. The fourteen-day period

Even if the 8 December notice were effective, it gave Meridian fourteen days from receipt to cure. The bundle records the expiry date as 22 December. [D15 Part 2, p 2] Termination was purportedly effected on 19 December, eleven days after hand delivery. [D16 Documents 16.1-16.2, p 1]

Accordingly, the 8.4.1 termination was premature. A termination notice before the contractual cure period expires is not cured by the fact that the employer would have been entitled to terminate later.

## C. Interaction between clauses 8.4.1.6 and 8.4.4

Clause 8.4.1.6 makes a material misrepresentation as to statutory health-and-safety compliance or a Schedule 3 certificate a “specified default” for clause 8.4.1 purposes. [D15 Part 1, p 1]

Clause 8.4.4 says that, notwithstanding clause 8.4.1, if the Employer reasonably believes the Contractor has acted fraudulently, it may terminate immediately in writing and no cure period applies. [D15 Part 1, p 1]

The better construction gives independent effect to both:

- a material misrepresentation that falls short of fraud proceeds through clause 8.4.1 and the fourteen-day cure process;
- fraud engages clause 8.4.4 and permits immediate termination;
- “notwithstanding clause 8.4.1” means that the fraud power is not subordinated to the cure machinery.

To construe clause 8.4.1.6 as making every certificate misrepresentation subject only to cure would deprive clause 8.4.4 of much of its intended work.

## D. Does clause 8.4.4 require objectively reasonable grounds?

Yes. The clause requires that Coldbrook “reasonably believes” that Meridian acted fraudulently. [D15 Part 1, p 1] The natural contractual reading requires:

1. an actual belief held by Coldbrook when it served the termination notice; and
2. facts or material then available that made the belief objectively reasonable.

It does not require Coldbrook to prove fraud conclusively before termination. If it did, “reasonably believes” would add no meaningful protection. But an unsupported allegation, hindsight, or evidence first obtained after the decision cannot retrospectively create a reasonable belief.

## E. Coldbrook’s position on 19 December 2023

The formal Northmoor metadata report was served on 6 May 2024, nearly five months after termination. [D17 Part 4, p 3] It therefore cannot be used as the factual basis of a belief actually held on 19 December, though it may corroborate facts that were already known then.

### Matters supporting reasonable belief at the time

- The default notice on 8 December specifically alleged that no certificate had been issued and that the document was created by or for Meridian on or about 13 November. [D16 Document 16.1, p 1]
- The certificate was facially irregular: blank TWC countersignature, blank job number, typed signature, and no digital signature. [D09 Part 1, p 1]
- The accident had occurred on 14 November, involving the same LB-7 loading platform. [D01 paras 6-8, p 1]
- The certificate stated a 2,000 kg working load, while the HSE’s authenticated drawing stated 750 kg. [D01 para 11, p 2; D09 Part 5, p 3]

### Matters undermining reasonable belief

- Vellacott Marr issued a certificate on 30 November confirming that it was satisfied the loading platforms were complete and independently certified. [D15 Part 2, p 1]
- The forensic examiner’s conclusion is not conclusive and permits an alternative explanation, namely retyping from a lost genuine certificate. [D09 Part 2, p 2]
- Halbrook’s former checker had sent an email on 26 October saying he had run the bay through, intended to formalise matters, and warned not to use anything until a certificate was obtained. That evidence does not establish an issued certificate, but shows a genuine prior interaction rather than invention from nothing. [D09 Part 4, p 3]

### Reasoned conclusion

Coldbrook has an arguable clause 8.4.4 case but does not yet have a safe one. It must disclose the material actually considered by the decision-maker on 19 December, including communications from Halbrook, Vellacott Marr, site staff, its solicitors and any forensic enquiries then undertaken. The Court should not permit Coldbrook to substitute the May 2024 report for the December 2023 state of knowledge.

## F. Common-law acceptance of repudiation

The termination notice also purported to accept a repudiatory breach at common law. [D16 Document 16.2, p 1]

That route is likely excluded. Clause 8.1, which was not amended, states that the provisions of section 8 “shall be the sole means by which the employment of the Contractor may be brought to an end”. [D16 Document 16.4, p 2]

The words “sole means” are unusually strong. They are likely to exclude a free-standing common-law termination right for the contractor’s employment under the contract, while preserving the specific immediate-fraud right expressly included within section 8.

The strongest point against this conclusion is that a party must use clear wording to abandon fundamental common-law rights, and “employment” might be argued to refer only to the JCT contractual employment mechanism rather than the contract as a whole. But the express exclusivity language makes Meridian’s contrary argument the stronger one.

## G. Consequences if Coldbrook’s termination was wrongful

If neither clause 8.4.1 nor clause 8.4.4 justified termination, Coldbrook’s termination and site-takeover direction would amount to a repudiatory breach. [D16 Document 16.2, p 1] Meridian purported to accept that repudiation on 21 December. [D16 Document 16.3, pp 1-2]

Meridian could then pursue damages intended to place it in the position it would have occupied had Coldbrook performed, subject to proof and mitigation. The likely heads include:

- unpaid sums properly due, including the IA/18 claim if otherwise valid;
- loss of profit on unperformed work;
- demobilisation and disruption losses;
- financing and cash-flow consequences, if legally recoverable and proved;
- the value of plant, materials and work in progress, subject to contractual valuation provisions.

Coldbrook could still advance damages for breaches committed before its repudiation, including any proven fraudulent conduct, defective work, delay, and completion-cost consequences. A wrongful termination does not retrospectively absolve Meridian from prior breach.

## H. Consequences if termination was valid

If clause 8.4.4 was validly exercised:

- Coldbrook could take possession and complete the works;
- Meridian would lose its future entitlement to perform;
- the parties’ financial rights would be governed by the post-termination accounting provisions of the JCT contract, which are not included in the retrieved extracts;
- Coldbrook could seek completion costs and loss caused by Meridian’s antecedent breach;
- Meridian’s claim for future profit would fail, although it could retain claims for sums accrued before a valid termination, subject to set-off and final accounting.

# 2.3 Caldera’s avoidance and declinature arguments

## Conclusion

Caldera has credible grounds to investigate and potentially reduce or avoid liability, but it does not have an assured route to complete avoidance.

Its strongest case is that the proposal stated that all temporary works were Category 3 checked by an independent external consultant and that a SHEQ Director retained board-level health-and-safety responsibility, whereas the actual arrangement was materially different. [D17 Part 1, p 1] The strongest answer is that the broker’s 4 September email expressly corrected the Category 3 answer and the SHEQ Director statement, was sent to the individual underwriter and underwriting support, and is supported by Mr Pilbeam’s same-day note. [D17 Part 3, p 2]

## A. Duty of fair presentation: sections 3, 7 and 8

The Insurance Act requires the insured, before a non-consumer insurance contract is entered into, to make a fair presentation of the risk. A fair presentation must disclose every material circumstance known or which ought to be known, or provide sufficient information to put a prudent insurer on notice to make further enquiries; material factual representations must be substantially correct. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/3" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 3 Law</a>

A matter is material if it would influence a prudent insurer deciding whether to accept the risk and, if so, on what terms. A fair presentation can comprise more than one document or oral presentation, and a representation may be withdrawn or corrected before the contract is entered into. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/7" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 7 Law</a>

An insurer has a remedy only if it shows that, but for the breach, it would either not have contracted at all or would have contracted on different terms. The insurer bears the burden of proving that any qualifying breach was deliberate or reckless. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/8" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 8 Law</a>

### The alleged misrepresentations

| Proposal answer | Caldera’s alleged true position | Initial assessment |
|---|---|---|
| All temporary works managed to BS 5975 and an independent TWC appointed on every project | Brackenmoor’s Site Manager was also TWC, Appointed Person and Permit Issuing Officer | Potentially material |
| All temporary works Category 3 checked externally | Category 2 checking used at Brackenmoor and allegedly all eleven live projects | Potentially highly material |
| No HSE enforcement, notices or investigations beyond 2021 Improvement Notice | HSE had received concerns in June and October 2023 | Materiality and Meridian’s knowledge disputed |
| SHEQ Director held board responsibility | Role removed in February 2023 | Potentially material, depending on replacement arrangements |

[D17 Part 1, p 1]

The factual case for materiality is strong. The accident involved allegedly inadequate temporary-works management, a disputed Category 2 certificate, missing ties, and an overloaded bay. [D01 paras 10-15, p 2; D09 Parts 1-5, pp 1-3]

But materiality alone is not enough. Caldera must prove the statutory counterfactual: what it would have done had it been fairly presented with the risk. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/8" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 8 Law</a>

## B. The broker email and Caldera’s knowledge

The 4 September email said:

- Q15 said Category 3, but Meridian in fact ran Category 2 with an external checker;
- this had been flagged verbally to Mr Pilbeam;
- Mr Pilbeam allegedly said underwriters were content and the matter made no difference to rate;
- the SHEQ Director role had been folded into the Construction Director role.  

[D17 Part 3, p 2]

It was sent to Mr Pilbeam and copied to Caldera’s underwriting support address. [D17 Part 3, p 2] Mr Pilbeam’s underwriting file also contained a same-day handwritten note, “W&T call — TW query — see email”. [D17 Part 3, p 2]

### Meridian’s argument

Meridian can submit that this is powerful evidence that:

1. the inaccurate answers were corrected before inception;
2. the corrected information formed part of the fair presentation;
3. Caldera actually knew, or at least had information requiring it to investigate, the Category 2 and safety-director facts;
4. Caldera cannot establish inducement, particularly where its own file suggests that the precise point was queried.

This argument is particularly strong for Q15 and Q22.

### Caldera’s answer

Caldera can say:

- the email received no response;
- Mr Pilbeam denies recollection and says he would not have accepted Category 2 checking without a rate adjustment;
- the email does not cure Q14, namely the lack of independence of the Site Manager/TWC;
- it does not establish that Caldera knew the role concentration at Brackenmoor;
- it does not cure Q16, which concerned HSE contacts;
- there is no proof that a prudent underwriter, rather than Mr Pilbeam individually, accepted the corrected position.

The Court should not treat the absence of the email from the underwriting file as neutral. Caldera had a seven-year file-retention policy, but the email has not been retained despite the broker’s copy and the note referring to it. [D17 Part 3, p 2] That is capable of supporting an adverse evidential inference, though it is not automatic.

## C. Sections 8 and Schedule 1: remedy

If Caldera proves a qualifying breach, section 8 directs the Court to Schedule 1 for the remedy. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/8" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 8 Law</a>

The parties’ pleaded positions are:

- Caldera: the breach was deliberate or reckless, therefore avoidance without premium return;
- Meridian: any breach was neither deliberate nor reckless;
- Caldera alternatively: it would have declined or charged a higher premium;
- Meridian: there is no reliable underwriting evidence and Mr Pilbeam’s note supports acceptance.  

[D17 Part 6, p 3]

The factual answer is likely to turn on underwriting evidence. Caldera must call evidence addressing its underwriting criteria at the inception date, not merely post-loss assertions by a former employee with no recollection.

The strongest point against Meridian is that Mr Ferris signed a declaration confirming fair presentation despite the obvious mismatch between the stated Category 3 system and the actual Category 2 system alleged by Caldera. [D17 Part 1, p 1] The strongest point against Caldera is the broker’s contemporaneous correction email and corroborating underwriter note. [D17 Part 3, p 2]

## D. General Condition 7, sections 10 and 11

GC7 requires compliance at all times with statutory duties and Meridian’s written temporary-works procedure, and labels compliance a condition precedent to liability. [D17 Part 2, p 1]

Section 10 abolished the former rule that any breach of warranty automatically discharged an insurer from all liability. A breach can affect liability for loss occurring after breach and before remedy, subject to the statutory qualifications and waiver. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/10" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 10 Law</a>

Section 11 restricts reliance on terms that tend to reduce risk of a particular kind, location or time. Even where the term applies, the insurer may not rely on non-compliance if the insured proves that the non-compliance could not have increased the risk of the loss which actually occurred in the circumstances in which it occurred. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/11" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 11 Law</a>

### Does GC7 survive section 11?

**Likely answer: yes in principle, but subject to section 11 if GC7 is of the relevant statutory character.**

GC7 is not merely a condition about a remote feature of risk. It requires compliance with statutory safety duties and the insured’s own temporary-works procedure. [D17 Part 2, p 1] The alleged non-compliance directly concerns the risk that materialised: inadequate tying, temporary-works control, checking, loading and lifting control. [D01 paras 10-15, p 2]

Meridian’s section 11 argument is therefore difficult. It would need to prove that the failures alleged could not have increased the risk of this collapse. On the provisional HSE case:

- three specified through-ties were absent; [D01 para 10, p 2]
- the absence reduced overturning resistance by an estimated 38 to 61 percent; [D01 para 10, p 2]
- the load was approximately 1,410 kg, compared with the authenticated 750 kg design figure; [D01 para 11, p 2]
- wind may have been determinative for an untied bay; [D01 para 12, p 2]
- the concentration of safety-critical roles was provisionally treated as a material organisational failing. [D01 para 13, p 2]

Those facts strongly support Caldera’s argument that any breach increased the risk of the actual loss.

The strongest contrary point is that section 11’s applicability first depends upon the true character of GC7. Meridian can argue that it is not a term defining the risk as a whole and operates by reference to particular safety risks. But, even if that threshold is cleared, Meridian faces the statutory burden under section 11(3), which is formidable on these facts. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/11" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 11 Law</a>

## E. GC19 and the alleged fraudulent device

GC19 provides that if a claim is in any respect fraudulent, or fraudulent means or devices are used by or for the insured to obtain a policy benefit, all policy benefit is forfeited. [D17 Part 2, p 1]

Meridian sent Caldera a claim pack on 24 November 2023 that included TWDC-BW-07 and described it as the independent Category 2 design-check certificate dated 27 October 2023. [D17 Part 4, p 3]

### Is this a fraudulent device or a collateral lie?

If Meridian knowingly submitted a fabricated certificate as genuine, it is much more likely to be a fraudulent device than a collateral lie:

- it bears directly on the existence and adequacy of the safety-control system;
- it bears on the actual cause and severity of the accident;
- it is relevant to GC7;
- it may be relevant to Exclusion 4;
- it was presented to support Meridian’s coverage position.

The Supreme Court decided that the fraudulent-claims rule does not apply to an otherwise justified claim merely supported by a collateral lie. <a href="https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2016/45" class="legal-ref jurisprudence" target="_blank">*Versloot Dredging BV v HDI Gerling Industrie Versicherung AG and others* Jurisprudence</a> A lie is collateral where it is irrelevant to the existence and amount of the insured’s entitlement. That is not naturally this case.

Meridian can nevertheless argue in the alternative:

1. the certificate was genuine or was a retyped version of a genuine earlier document;
2. no dishonesty by Meridian is proved;
3. even if inaccurate, liability for the injuries arose from the incident itself rather than the later description in a claims letter;
4. Caldera would have been liable for the underlying third-party claims regardless of the certificate.

The third proposition is factually weak if GC7 is a valid defence, because the alleged falsity of the certificate is closely connected with the safety breach said to defeat cover.

## F. Statutory fraud remedy and transparency: sections 12, 16 and 17

Section 12 states that where an insured makes a fraudulent claim, the insurer is not liable to pay it, may recover sums paid in respect of it, and may by notice terminate the contract from the time of the fraudulent act. The effect on later relevant events is prescribed by the section. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/12" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 12 Law</a>

A non-consumer term which places an insured in a worse position than the Act’s protections is ineffective unless the transparency requirements are met. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/16" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 16 Law</a> Those requirements are that the insurer takes sufficient steps to draw the disadvantageous term to the insured’s attention and that the term is clear and unambiguous as to its effect, subject to actual knowledge by the insured or its agent. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/17" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 17 Law</a>

GC19’s “all benefit under this Policy shall be forfeited” wording is capable of being more adverse to Meridian than the statutory position. [D17 Part 2, p 1] Therefore, Caldera must establish section 17 transparency if it seeks to rely on GC19 to impose a more severe consequence than section 12.

The absence of a policy statement expressly said to comply with section 17 does **not** itself determine the point. Section 17 does not prescribe a ritual formula. But it is strong evidence for Meridian because D17 says there was no compliant transparency statement concerning GC19. [D17 Part 2, p 2]

Caldera’s best response is actual knowledge: it may argue that Meridian, a substantial contractor using a Lloyd’s broker, and its broker had actual knowledge of GC19. That will require evidence of the placement process, policy presentation, renewal documentation and advice.

## G. Election, waiver and estoppel

Caldera:

- received notice on 15 November 2023; [D17 Part 4, p 3]
- received TWDC-BW-07 on 24 November 2023; [D17 Part 4, p 3]
- funded Meridian’s defence from December 2023 to January 2025, spending £486,000; [D17 Part 4, p 3]
- knew of the formal metadata report from 6 May 2024; [D17 Part 4, p 3]
- took coverage advice on 18 July 2024 but continued funding; [D17 Part 4, p 3]
- first reserved rights on 2 February 2025; [D17 Part 5, p 3]
- purported to avoid and decline only on 11 April 2025. [D17 Part 4, p 3]

This is strong material for Meridian’s waiver, election or estoppel case. In particular, the insurer’s continuation of funding after the metadata report and after coverage advice may be conduct inconsistent with a prompt election to avoid or deny cover.

However, election is not made out merely because an insurer investigates or funds a defence. The question is whether, with sufficient knowledge of the relevant breach and of its right, Caldera unequivocally communicated or acted upon an affirmation of cover. The 2 February letter expressly denied waiver, election or admission. [D17 Part 5, p 3]

### Practical conclusion on this issue

- The reservation letter can protect Caldera prospectively.
- It does not automatically erase the legal significance of the preceding period.
- The chronology is particularly damaging to Caldera on the pre-contractual Q15 and Q22 matters, because the broker email was sent before inception and its underwriting file contained the same-day reference.
- It is less damaging on the alleged claim fraud because formal metadata evidence was not served until May 2024.

# 2.4 Recovery routes for Mr Aksoy and the dependants of Mr Wieczorek

## Immediate conclusion

Mr Aksoy and the dependants should preserve and, if necessary, issue claims now against:

1. **Meridian**, directly in negligence;
2. **TowerLine**, with rights against its insurer pursued under the Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010;
3. potentially **Northgate**, subject to investigation of its role in engaging, inducting and supervising agency workers.

The claims should not await the outcome of the criminal case, inquest, insurance coverage dispute or TCC dispute. Those processes concern overlapping facts but do not resolve the same civil causes of action.

## A. Claim against TowerLine and Brackenridge Mutual

TowerLine entered creditors’ voluntary liquidation on 3 February 2025. [D01 para 4, p 1] This enables an injured third party potentially to use the Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010.

Section 1 transfers the insolvent insured’s rights under the insurance contract, in respect of the liability, to the injured third party. A claimant may bring proceedings against the insurer without first obtaining a judgment against the insolvent insured, though it cannot enforce the transferred insurance rights until the liability and amount are established. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/10/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010, s 1 Law</a>

### Was cover bound orally?

TowerLine’s previous public and employers’ liability policy expired at midnight on 31 October 2023 and the renewal was not completed. [D17 p 4] A broker email dated 30 October said that underwriters had agreed to “hold covered on existing terms” pending the completed proposal and that TowerLine was covered from 1 November. [D17 p 4]

That is cogent evidence that an interim contract of insurance was made orally or by conduct. But Brackenridge Mutual denies cover and says the broker had no authority to hold covered. [D17 p 4]

### Best argument for Mr Aksoy

Mr Aksoy should plead that:

- the email records an agreement already reached with underwriters;
- it was not merely a unilateral broker assurance;
- “covered from 1 November” is clear operative language;
- the incident was on 14 November, within the stated held-covered period;
- any dispute about proposal forms concerned a later administrative condition, not the formation of interim cover.

### Strongest point against the claim

The email’s legal effect depends on the broker’s authority and the true scope of the alleged oral agreement. If the broker was not authorised by Brackenridge to bind it, or if return of the proposal was a condition precedent to cover rather than a condition of renewal administration, no policy may have been formed.

The claim should nonetheless be brought. TowerLine’s insolvency makes this route too important to leave untested.

## B. Direct claim against Meridian

Meridian was the principal contractor. [D01 para 3, p 1] A principal contractor’s statutory construction-phase duties include planning, managing, monitoring and coordinating health and safety, so far as reasonably practicable, to ensure construction work is carried out without health-and-safety risks. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/51/section/13" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015, regulation 13 Law</a>

The duties specifically include cooperation between contractors, coordination of health-and-safety requirements, ensuring workers apply prevention principles, provision of a suitable site induction, and preventing unauthorised access. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/51/section/13" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015, regulation 13 Law</a>

A contractor must also plan, manage and monitor work under its control; ensure workers under its control receive suitable supervision, instruction and information; and provide information about serious and imminent danger and relevant risks. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/51/section/15" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015, regulation 15 Law</a>

### Mr Aksoy

Mr Aksoy was employed by TowerLine, but that does not end the inquiry. He alleges that Meridian controlled the rule that no one could load a bay without a signed handover. [D03 para 4, p 1] The bundle also identifies Meridian as the principal contractor, alleges a Meridian temporary-works procedure, and identifies a Site Manager holding the simultaneous roles of TWC, Appointed Person and Permit Issuing Officer. [D01 paras 3 and 13, pp 1-2]

The direct negligence claim should be based on Meridian’s own alleged acts and omissions, not simply on vicarious liability for TowerLine:

- allowing or directing loading at LB-7;
- inadequate control of the temporary-works design and checking regime;
- failure to verify ties and façade fixings;
- inadequate permit and handover arrangements;
- allowing lifting to restart in adverse wind;
- failure to coordinate contractors and protect workers exposed to the operation.

The HSE’s provisional findings support that pleaded case. It found that three specified through-ties were absent, with an estimated 38 to 61 percent reduction in overturning resistance. [D01 para 10, p 2] It found an actual load of about 1,410 kg compared with the authenticated design figure of 750 kg. [D01 para 11, p 2] It also considered that wind could have been determinative of an untied bay’s failure. [D01 para 12, p 2]

### Meridian’s defence

Meridian will argue that Mr Aksoy was an employee of an independent subcontractor and that TowerLine retained responsibility for its own employees and scaffolding. That is a real point, especially if TowerLine alone controlled erection, inspection and use of LB-7.

But the evidence of Meridian’s retained control and statutory principal-contractor role materially weakens an attempt to characterise it as a remote employer of an independent contractor.

The Supreme Court’s non-delegable-duty analysis concerns relationships of custody, dependence and assumed positive protective responsibility. <a href="https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2013/66" class="legal-ref jurisprudence" target="_blank">*Woodland v Essex County Council* Jurisprudence</a> It is not the best primary route here: experienced workers on a multi-contractor construction site may not satisfy that exceptional doctrine. The stronger case is ordinary negligence based on Meridian’s own retained safety functions and control.

## C. The agency-worker position: Mr Wieczorek

Mr Wieczorek was engaged through Northgate Site Solutions Ltd. [D01 para 3, p 1] He was on the LB-7 deck receiving the suspended load when the bay failed, fell about 21 metres, and died from multiple blunt-force injuries. [D01 paras 6-7, p 1]

The fact that he was agency labour does not remove Meridian’s duties as principal contractor. Regulation 13 applies to the construction phase and requires the principal contractor to coordinate health and safety and ensure, so far as reasonably practicable, that work occurs without risk. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/51/section/13" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015, regulation 13 Law</a>

The factual case is strengthened by the unresolved induction evidence. The induction register bears an entry in Mr Wieczorek’s name, but the signature may not match his agency assignment signature; the named trainer says she was on leave; and Meridian’s HR record indicates the leave cancellation was recorded only on 20 November. [D01 para 16, p 2] That evidence is not resolved, but it makes induction and supervision a live issue.

The dependants should pursue claims against Meridian, TowerLine and, after investigation, Northgate. Liability may ultimately be apportioned between defendants, but a claimant need not accept that the allocation dispute prevents recovery from a party whose breach materially contributed to the death.

## D. Fatal Accidents Act 1976

Where death is caused by a wrongful act, neglect or default that would have entitled the injured person to recover damages had they survived, an action lies for the benefit of statutory dependants. The Act defines dependants broadly, including a spouse, children, parents and certain other family members. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/30/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Fatal Accidents Act 1976, s 1 Law</a>

The action should ordinarily be brought by the executor or administrator. If there is no personal representative, or none brings the action within six months of death, one or more dependants may bring it. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/30/section/2" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Fatal Accidents Act 1976, s 2 Law</a>

The widow, Mrs Wieczorek, is identified as an interested person in the inquest. [D02 p 1] On the present material, she is likely a statutory dependant, but the financial-dependency evidence has not been reviewed and must be obtained.

A claim can include bereavement damages for a spouse, civil partner or qualifying cohabiting partner. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/30/section/1A" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Fatal Accidents Act 1976, s 1A Law</a> The statutory figure displayed in the retrieved legislation is £15,120, though current applicability at the date of judgment should be checked before pleading a specific amount. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/30/section/1A" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Fatal Accidents Act 1976, s 1A Law</a>

The dependants’ substantive damages claim may include:

- loss of financial dependency;
- loss of services dependency;
- funeral expenses, where recoverable;
- bereavement damages for a qualifying claimant;
- an estate claim for losses before death, if applicable.

The exact heads and valuation require evidence of earnings, remittances, household services, tax position, pension arrangements, immigration/family evidence, and the estate’s representation.

## E. Limitation as at 12 September 2026

### Mr Aksoy’s personal-injury claim

For a negligence, statutory-duty or similar claim involving personal injury, the ordinary limitation period is three years from the accrual date or, if later, the claimant’s date of knowledge. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1980/58/section/11" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Limitation Act 1980, s 11 Law</a>

The accident was on 14 November 2023. [D01 para 6, p 1] On the current facts, Mr Aksoy knew immediately that he had been injured in a workplace collapse and who the material potential defendants were. Accordingly, the prudent deadline is:

**14 November 2026.**

That is approximately two months from today. Proceedings must be issued and served in accordance with procedural requirements in time; a pre-action letter alone does not stop limitation.

### Mr Wieczorek’s estate and dependency claims

The Fatal Accidents Act claim cannot be brought after three years from the date of death or the relevant dependant’s date of knowledge, whichever is later. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1980/58/section/12" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Limitation Act 1980, s 12 Law</a>

Mr Wieczorek died on 14 November 2023. [D01 para 7, p 1] The conservative limitation date for both the estate’s surviving personal-injury claim and the dependants’ Fatal Accidents Act claim is therefore:

**14 November 2026.**

A later date of knowledge might be arguable for a particular dependant or personal representative, but it should not be relied upon. Mrs Wieczorek was participating in the inquest by June 2025. [D02 p 1] That makes a later-knowledge argument increasingly unattractive.

### The TowerLine insurer route

The Third Parties Act transfers TowerLine’s insurance rights; it does not create a new and longer underlying personal-injury limitation period. The liability claim and coverage claim should therefore be pursued together and protected before 14 November 2026.
