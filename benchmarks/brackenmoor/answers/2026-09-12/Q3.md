# Advice on Admissibility, Privilege, Disclosure and Professional Conduct

## Overall conclusions

The principal evidence-management issues arise across four distinct fora, and the answer is not uniform:

1. **Crown Court:** applies the criminal hearsay regime in the Criminal Justice Act 2003, may consider exclusion under PACE 1984, s 78, and must ensure a fair criminal trial.
2. **TCC:** determines privilege, mistaken disclosure, expert permission and civil evidential weight under the CPR. It is the most important forum for the CPR 31.20 application concerning D20.3 and for CPR Part 35 issues.
3. **Employment Tribunal:** is less formal about admissibility but must conduct a fair hearing. The central questions are relevance, fairness, reliability, privacy/data-protection breaches and whether the disputed material itself supports whistleblowing causation.
4. **Coroner’s court:** is inquisitorial. It may receive material that would be problematic in an adversarial trial, but it must still avoid resolving contested criminal liability and must fairly examine the circumstances of the death.

On the present bundle, the strongest conclusions are:

- D10 item 10.6 is prima facie legal advice privileged, but the **iniquity exception is likely not engaged merely because the solicitor was asked whether a past or proposed wrong could be regularised and instead firmly refused to assist it**. The advice appears to impede, rather than further, wrongdoing.
- If D20.3 contains the same advice or a note of the same consultation, it is independently privileged. Disclosure of one document does not automatically waive privilege in every other related document, although it may create a serious fairness argument if the client deploys part of the advice selectively.
- Meridian’s application under CPR 31.20 is fact-sensitive. The late disclosure, the clear privileged heading, the substance of the email, and the prompt demand for return favour a finding of an obvious mistake. However, the substantial unexplained disclosure failures materially weaken Meridian’s credibility.
- The recording at D20.2 is not automatically inadmissible merely because it was covertly made. The poor audio and three tenable readings at 02:40 make that passage unsafe as proof of the incriminating wording; that is primarily a reliability and weight issue. The remainder can be admitted if relevant and fairly proved.
- The welfare-cabin surveillance appears seriously difficult to justify under UK GDPR principles and lawful-basis requirements, particularly because the contemporaneous memorandum identifies whistleblower-identification as its real objective. It is nevertheless not automatically excluded in the Employment Tribunal.
- Ms Raghunathan has a strong arguable claim under Employment Rights Act 1996, s 103A. The timing, the prohibition on regulator contact, the absence of any redundancy process, the defective board minute, the later senior-QS advertisement, and the proposed clause 9 all substantially support an inference that the disclosure was the reason or principal reason for dismissal.

# 3.1 D20.3 and D10 item 10.6: privilege, iniquity, mistaken disclosure and Halloway Brant’s duties

## Forum

| Question | Crown Court | TCC | Employment Tribunal | Coroner’s court |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whether D10.6/D20.3 is privileged | Yes, if prosecution seeks to use it | Yes; decisive for CPR 31.20 relief | Potentially, if deployed in the whistleblowing claim | Potentially, though coroner should not casually override privilege |
| Iniquity exception | Same common-law principle | Same common-law principle | Same principle | Same principle |
| Mistaken disclosure | Criminal disclosure/fair-trial context | CPR 31.20 is central | Tribunal case-management/fairness | Inquisitorial fairness and privilege |
| Solicitors’ professional duties | Acute | Acute | Acute | Acute |

The formal application identified in the bundle is a **TCC CPR 31.20 application**, listed for 14 November 2025. D20 identifies that D20.3 is asserted by Meridian to have been privileged and disclosed inadvertently. [D20, p 1, item 20.3 and warning.]

## A. Is D10 item 10.6 prima facie covered by legal advice privilege?

Yes.

D10.6 is a direct confidential communication from Halloway Brant solicitor O. Trelawney-Baptiste to Mr Ferris, following their call, giving legal advice about the legal consequences of: obtaining a signature retrospectively; presenting a document as contemporaneous; and omitting material from the HSE response. [D10, item 10.6.]

That is the classic subject matter of legal advice privilege: confidential lawyer-client advice sought and given in the professional legal relationship. The label “PRIVILEGED & CONFIDENTIAL” is not conclusive, but it accords with the document’s actual character. [D10, item 10.6.]

The fact that the advice records Mr Ferris’s reported account does not remove privilege. A solicitor cannot safely advise on exposure to fraud, regulatory offences, obstruction, or disclosure obligations without being told the client’s account. The privilege question concerns the purpose and character of the communication, not whether the underlying conduct is benign.

## B. Does the iniquity exception displace privilege?

### Short answer

**Probably not on the present material.**

The relevant test is not whether the communication concerns suspected crime or dishonesty. The communication must be created **in furtherance of** a criminal, fraudulent or otherwise iniquitous purpose. It is not enough that the document records, reports on, or gives legal advice about alleged wrongdoing.

The Court of Appeal has described the exception as applicable where a document is intended to further a criminal, fraudulent or otherwise iniquitous purpose; it rejected a formulation extending the exception merely to documents generated by, or reporting on, alleged wrongdoing. [*Karam Salah Al Din Awni Al Sadeq v Dechert LLP* [2024] EWCA Civ 28 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2024/28), para 38.]

The Privy Council has confirmed that the common-law exception concerns documents coming into existence in furtherance of fraud, crime or other iniquity, and identified *R v Cox and Railton* among the historic authorities in that lineage. [*Jardine Strategic Ltd v Oasis Investments II Master Fund Ltd (No 2)* [2025] UKPC 34 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ukpc/2025/34), para 25.]

### Application to D10.6

D10.6 does not advise how to conceal, fabricate, or regularise the alleged misconduct. On its face, the solicitor:

- says that the document must not be retrospectively “made good”;
- says that omission from the HSE response would be improper;
- warns of potential criminal consequences;
- states that the solicitor cannot advise on how the suggested course might be done;
- advises production of the document in its existing state and disclosure of its creation circumstances; and
- says further discussion with Mr Delacroix-Hale should occur only in the solicitor’s presence. [D10, item 10.6.]

That is advice **against** iniquity, not a communication in furtherance of it. The fact that Meridian subsequently produced TWDC-BW-07 to HSE without disclosing the circumstances of its creation may be powerful evidence relevant to Meridian’s conduct; it does not retrospectively convert the solicitor’s prohibitory advice into a communication made to facilitate that conduct. [D10, item 10.6.]

There is an important distinction:

- If the client had sought and obtained operational assistance in concealing the source of the certificate, evading HSE’s notice, or scripting a false account, the exception could potentially arise.
- Where the solicitor unequivocally declines to advise on a proposed unlawful course and tells the client not to pursue it, the policy basis for the exception is absent.

That conclusion applies across the Crown Court, TCC, Employment Tribunal and coroner’s court. The procedural consequences differ, but privilege is not normally lost merely because the advice was morally or legally unwelcome to the client.

## C. Does D20.3 have separate privilege?

It depends on its actual contents, which must be inspected in full before a final ruling.

D20 identifies D20.3 as Halloway Brant’s attendance note of 15 November 2023. [D20, p 1, item 20.3.] An attendance note made by solicitors of a confidential lawyer-client consultation will normally be privileged if it records the client’s instructions and the advice given for the purpose of legal advice.

However, there are three possibilities:

1. **D20.3 is a contemporaneous internal attendance note of the same call underlying D10.6.**  
   It is likely independently privileged.

2. **D20.3 records communications with a third party, such as HSE, Coldbrook, Halbrook, or another non-client.**  
   The relevant part may not be legal-advice privileged unless a different privilege applies.

3. **D20.3 contains material prepared to facilitate a contemplated wrongdoing rather than advice against it.**  
   Those particular passages could potentially fall within the iniquity exception, but only if the strict “in furtherance of” threshold is met.

The present bundle does not permit a principled final answer on D20.3’s exact contents. Its disputed status and the notice of the CPR 31.20 application are known. [D20, p 1.] But a court should inspect the full document, preferably through a judge-only or confidentiality-ring process, before deciding privilege.

## D. Can privilege in D20.3 survive if it is lost in D10.6, and vice versa?

### General position

Yes. Privilege is document-specific, although communications forming part of one transaction or “continuum of communications” may have to be considered together.

The possible outcomes are therefore:

| Position | D10.6 | D20.3 |
|---|---|---|
| Both are confidential legal advice concerning the same consultation | Privileged | Privileged |
| D10.6 was inadvertently disclosed but D20.3 was never disclosed | Privilege may remain in D20.3 | Likely remains, subject to fairness/waiver arguments |
| D10.6 is held within the iniquity exception | Not privileged for that reason | D20.3 may still be privileged unless itself in furtherance of iniquity |
| Meridian selectively deploys part of the advice | May amount to waiver | Court may require related material necessary for fairness |
| D20.3 contains a separate unprivileged factual communication | Privilege may not attach to that part | D10.6 can nonetheless remain privileged |

A loss of privilege in one document does not mechanically destroy privilege in another. Conversely, Meridian cannot use D10.6 or D20.3 selectively to establish its own good faith while suppressing other parts of the same advice sequence that qualify or contradict that contention.

## E. Was the disclosure an “obvious mistake” for CPR 31.20 purposes?

### Factors favouring Meridian

The following factors favour a CPR 31.20 restraint order:

- D10.6 is expressly headed privileged and confidential. [D10, item 10.6.]
- Its contents are unmistakably solicitor-to-client legal advice. [D10, item 10.6.]
- Halloway Brant asserted privilege and demanded return shortly after the late disclosure. [D10, p 1, disclosure note.]
- HSE has sequestered the document rather than deploying it immediately. [D10, p 1, disclosure note.]
- D20 expressly identifies a live inadvertent-disclosure application. [D20, p 1.]

A reasonable recipient would likely recognise that a direct email from external solicitors, marked privileged and giving legal advice, was likely disclosed by mistake. That is the core practical question in the CPR 31.20 analysis.

### Factors against Meridian

The case for relief is materially weakened by the disclosure history:

- D10.2 to D10.6 were disclosed only on 2 April 2024, after having been described as found during a legacy-server migration. [D10, item 10.9.]
- Meridian has not explained why messages from a serving director’s mailbox were not found during the original search. [D10, item 10.9.]
- No disclosure certificate or search protocol has been provided. [D10, item 10.9.]
- There is a striking discrepancy in the Ferris sent-items record: only 11 November messages as compared with 402 October messages. [D10, item 10.9.]
- D01 records late disclosure of 1,417 documents, including nineteen post-dating the HSE preservation notice. [D01, para 21.]

Those facts do not, by themselves, deprive D10.6 of privilege. They do, however, give the TCC legitimate concern about whether Meridian’s disclosure process was competent, sufficiently supervised, or candid. They also make a full witness statement from the relevant Halloway Brant fee-earner, disclosure manager and Meridian IT custodian essential.

### Recommended TCC order

Halloway Brant should seek:

1. immediate continuation of sequestration;
2. an interim non-use order pending determination;
3. disclosure to the judge only of D20.3 and any necessary surrounding material;
4. evidence explaining the search, review, production, discovery of error, notification, and steps taken after discovery;
5. a direction that no party may rely on, quote, circulate or derive questions from D10.6/D20.3 until determination;
6. a limited ruling distinguishing:
   - legal advice;
   - unprivileged underlying documents;
   - facts existing independently of the advice; and
   - any part said to fall within the iniquity exception;
7. if privilege is maintained, delivery-up or deletion of copies, subject to a record of the court’s ruling.

The TCC should not allow the question to be determined simply by asking whether the email is damaging. It is plainly damaging to Meridian, but damaging legal advice remains privileged unless a recognised exception applies.

## F. What must Halloway Brant now do?

Halloway Brant should act immediately and independently of Mr Ferris’s preferred litigation position.

### Immediate steps

1. **Preserve all originals and metadata.**  
   Preserve D10.6, D20.3, the relevant email server records, attendance-note metadata, disclosure-review logs, document-family links, privilege logs, and the instructions given by Meridian concerning disclosure. The absence of sent-item material and unexplained late disclosure makes this especially important. [D10, item 10.9.]

3. **Carry out an urgent internal conflict review.**

# 3.2 The covert recording at D20.2

## Forum-specific answer

| Issue | Crown Court | TCC | Employment Tribunal | Coroner’s court |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Covert private recording | May be admitted, subject to fairness | Relevant and admissible subject to fairness/weight | Usually potentially admissible, subject to relevance and fair-hearing powers | Potentially admissible in the inquiry |
| PACE 1984, s 78 | Directly relevant only if prosecution relies on evidence obtained in circumstances engaging the provision | Not directly applicable | Not directly applicable | Not directly applicable |
| Article 8 | Fair-trial/privacy consideration, but not an automatic exclusion rule | Relevant to fair process | Relevant to fairness, especially if employer relies on covert material | Relevant but inquiry-led |
| Without prejudice | If a genuine settlement communication, the rule may restrict use; not established here | Directly relevant | Potentially relevant | Potentially relevant |
| 02:40 disputed wording | Very weak as proof absent reliable transcription | Same | Same | Same |

## A. The recording is not automatically excluded because it was covert

Mr Aksoy says that he recorded the meeting on his own phone in his jacket pocket because he did not trust Meridian and wanted a record. [D03, paras 14-16.] The transcript identifies the meeting as one attended by Mr Aksoy, Mr Ferris and Meridian’s Head of HR. [D20, item 20.2.]

The fact that a participant covertly records a conversation does not itself establish inadmissibility in any of the fora. The more relevant questions are:

- whether the recording is authentic;
- whether it is complete;
- whether the speakers can be identified;
- whether its probative value exceeds unfair prejudice;
- whether the transcript reliably reflects the audio;
- whether privacy or settlement privilege is engaged;
- whether the proposed use is fair.

## B. PACE 1984, s 78: Crown Court only

PACE 1984, s 78 applies where the prosecution proposes to rely on evidence and admission would adversely affect the fairness of proceedings such that the court ought not admit it. The statutory text applies throughout the United Kingdom. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1984/60/section/78" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984, s 78 Law</a>.

The principal difficulty for a s 78 challenge is that the recording was made by Mr Aksoy, a private participant, not obtained by police misconduct. The Crown did not arrange the recording on the bundle’s case. [D03, paras 14-16; D20, item 20.2.]

That does not end the question. A Crown Court may still exclude evidence if, for example:

- it is so incomplete or unintelligible that its use is unfair;
- the transcript inaccurately supplies incriminating words;
- the prosecution presents contested interpretation as certain fact;
- the jury is likely to overvalue a poor-quality recording.

The correct remedy is unlikely to be exclusion of the entire recording merely because it was covert. It is more likely to be:

1. admission of the native audio;
2. use of an agreed neutral transcript where possible;
3. clear marking of disputed or inaudible passages;
4. exclusion, or strictly limited use, of the 02:40 passage;
5. a jury direction that it is for the jury, not the transcriber, to determine what was said.

## C. CPR 32.1(2): TCC

In the TCC, the court controls the evidence it receives and can exclude evidence otherwise admissible. The relevant practical exercise is proportionality, relevance, reliability, procedural fairness, and whether the recording contributes materially to a disputed issue.

The recording is potentially relevant to:

- Mr Ferris’s knowledge of the Coldbrook-to-Pyle payments before the stated disclosure date;
- Mr Ferris’s knowledge or lack of knowledge of the certificate;
- any attempted discouragement of communication with lawyers, HSE or the inspector. [D20, item 20.2, 01:08-01:29, 02:40-02:52, 03:04-03:14.]

The TCC should not adopt a transcript prepared by Mr Aksoy’s own solicitors as if it were independent evidence. [D20, item 20.2.] It should order production of:

- the original audio file;
- device and extraction metadata;
- a forensic integrity report;
- the solicitor-prepared transcript;
- Mr Aksoy’s alternative account;
- Meridian’s examiner’s report;
- if justified, an independent joint transcription expert.

## D. Article 8

Mr Aksoy attended the meeting in person. [D03, para 14; D20, item 20.2.] That substantially reduces any reasonable expectation that the content of a meeting with the injured person about his financial position would remain private as against that participant.

Even if Article 8 considerations are engaged, they do not create an automatic evidential exclusion rule. The question is one of lawful interference, proportionality and fair proceedings. The private, one-off recording differs sharply from Meridian’s employer-installed covert audio-video surveillance in a welfare cabin, considered below.

## E. Without prejudice objection

Meridian’s assertion that the meeting was without prejudice is weak on the present facts.

The transcript begins with Meridian HR saying the meeting concerned the “sick pay position,” while also saying Mr Aksoy was not Meridian’s employee. [D20, item 20.2, 00:00:04-00:00:22.] Mr Aksoy’s account is that he sought a meeting about sick pay. [D03, para 14.]

The bundle identifies a dispute whether the meeting was a without-prejudice discussion of Mr Aksoy’s claim, and whether proceedings were then contemplated. [D20, p 4.] But the following points favour Mr Aksoy:

- he was not Meridian’s employee according to Meridian’s own HR representative; [D20, item 20.2, 00:00:14-00:00:22]
- no clear “without prejudice” label or express settlement ground rules appear in the transcribed opening; [D20, item 20.2]
- the meeting appears at least partly administrative and welfare-related; [D03, para 14; D20, item 20.2]
- Mr Aksoy says he recorded it because he feared being blamed. [D03, para 16.]

Without prejudice protection requires more than a later assertion that a conversation should not be used. The party asserting it should prove that there was a genuine dispute and a communication made for the purpose of settling it.

### Unambiguous impropriety

Even if the meeting were otherwise without prejudice, the alleged instruction at 03:04 that no part of the conversation be mentioned to a lawyer, inspector or anyone else is capable of engaging the “unambiguous impropriety” exception. [D20, item 20.2, 03:04-03:14.]

The court should be cautious. The exception is narrow. It must not become a device for admitting ordinary settlement pressure or ambiguous negotiation language. But an attempt to suppress reporting to a regulator or prevent legal advice, if proved, is qualitatively different from robust settlement discussion.

The safest course is a preliminary admissibility hearing with the audio, not merely the transcript.

## F. The disputed 02:40 passage

This passage should not be used as proof of an admission in its served form.

There are three materially different readings:

1. solicitor-prepared transcription: Mr Ferris said he did not know “it wasn’t real” until after; [D20, item 20.2, 02:40]
2. Mr Aksoy’s own account: Mr Ferris said he did not know “if it was real” until after; [D20, item 20.2, 02:40]
3. Meridian’s examiner: the relevant words are not reliably resolvable and “it was” also cannot be excluded. [D20, item 20.2, 02:40.]

The difference is legally substantial:

- “I did not know it was not real until after” potentially implies acknowledgment that the certificate was false.
- “I did not know if it was real until after” is compatible with uncertainty.
- “I did not know it was real until after” changes the sense again.

The report’s concession that a significant part of the recording is at or below reliable intelligibility reinforces the problem. [D20, item 20.2.]

### Recommended ruling

The court should:

- admit the original recording only subject to proof of authenticity;
- exclude the solicitor-prepared wording from being treated as an agreed transcript;
- permit the jury, judge or tribunal to listen to the audio only if satisfied it can properly assist;
- prohibit counsel from inviting a tribunal of fact to treat the incriminating wording as established;
- consider excluding the short disputed words entirely as too unreliable.

## G. Can the rest be admitted if 02:40 is excluded?

Yes.

The disputed passage is severable. There is no principled reason why an unreliable sentence should contaminate the entire four-minute recording. The following passages remain separately relevant, though still subject to authenticity, fairness and the without-prejudice ruling:

- 01:08 to 01:29, concerning Pyle’s allegedly inconsistent evidence and payments from Coldbrook; [D20, item 20.2.]
- 02:11 to 02:21, showing the question about certificate authenticity; [D20, item 20.2.]
- 03:04 to 03:14, concerning the alleged instruction not to tell a lawyer, inspector or anyone else. [D20, item 20.2.]

# 3.3 Covert welfare-cabin surveillance: data protection, Article 8, admissibility and whistleblowing detriment

## Forum

| Issue | Crown Court | TCC | Employment Tribunal | Coroner’s court |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| UK GDPR/DPA compliance | May be relevant but not central | Potential civil/privacy relevance | Central to fairness and whistleblowing causation | Contextual only |
| Admissibility of resulting recording | Fairness/reliability | Relevance/fairness | Directly in issue | Potentially relevant to circumstances |
| Detriment under ERA 1996, s 47B | Not the principal forum | Not the principal forum | Direct claim | Contextual evidence only |

## A. Bundle facts

The contemporaneous internal memorandum directs audio and video surveillance in the welfare cabin and drying room, asks for it to be discreet, expressly rejects signage and induction-notice disclosure, and identifies a wish to know who had spoken to HSE and Coldbrook. [D13, Part 4.]

The equipment was installed on 1 December 2023, eleven days after Ms Raghunathan’s 20 November? The bundle actually identifies her written disclosure as 16 November and phone call to HSE on 17 November. [D18, item 18.1; D01, para 20.] The installation date was 1 December. [D13, Part 4.] It therefore occurred:

- 15 days after her internal written disclosure of 16 November; and
- 14 days after her telephone call to HSE on 17 November.

The statement that it was “eleven days after the disclosure” in D13 is internally inconsistent with the dates in D18 and D01 unless “disclosure” refers to a separate event not presently identified. [D13, Part 4; D18, item 18.1; D01, para 20.]

The legal analysis must therefore proceed in the alternative:

- **If the relevant disclosure was 16 November:** the installation was 15 days later.
- **If the relevant disclosure was 17 November:** it was 14 days later.
- **If D13’s eleven-day figure is correct:** the bundle must contain or assume a disclosure on 20 November, but the supplied documents do not identify it.

That inconsistency does not materially remove the causation concern, because the memorandum itself expressly refers to a “leak to the inspector.” [D13, Part 4.]

## B. UK GDPR Article 5 principles

The surveillance is difficult to reconcile with the Article 5 principles described in the question, in particular:

- **lawfulness, fairness and transparency;**
- **purpose limitation;**
- **data minimisation;**
- **storage limitation;**
- **integrity and confidentiality;**
- **accountability.**

The facts pointing against compliance are unusually strong:

- no signage; [D13, Part 4]
- no reference in induction material or privacy notices; [D13, Part 4]
- no DPIA; [D13, Part 4]
- no Article 30 record of processing; [D13, Part 4]
- audio as well as video surveillance in staff welfare and drying spaces; [D13, Part 4]
- an asserted “asset-protection” purpose inconsistent with the recorded objective of identifying persons speaking to HSE and Coldbrook. [D13, Part 4.]

The purported asset-protection explanation is particularly weak because the relevant Hilti theft had occurred in September, the equipment was recovered within a week, an employee was dismissed, and no further thefts were reported. [D13, Part 4.]

## C. Article 6(1)(f): legitimate interests

Meridian relies on legitimate interests for prevention and detection of crime and protection of assets. [D13, Part 4.]

That requires a genuine legitimate interest, necessity, and a balancing exercise against affected individuals’ rights and freedoms. On the evidence available:

- the stated interest in theft prevention is capable of being legitimate in the abstract;
- but covert audio-video monitoring of a welfare cabin and drying room appears disproportionate where the theft had been resolved and no further thefts had been reported; [D13, Part 4]
- a less intrusive option, such as targeted visible security, equipment access controls, or ordinary CCTV in asset-storage areas, would require consideration;
- the contemporaneous instruction suggests the actual aim was identifying whistleblowers and persons communicating with the regulator. [D13, Part 4.]

A controller cannot retrospectively convert an improper purpose into legitimate interests merely by pleading an asset-protection rationale in an ET3.

## D. Articles 13 and 35; Data Protection Act 2018

Article 13 requires provision of privacy information in the ordinary employment context. Meridian’s deliberate decision not to use signage, induction materials or a privacy notice is highly problematic. [D13, Part 4.]

A DPIA under Article 35 is especially likely to have been required before systematic, covert, audio-visual monitoring of workers in private welfare facilities. The absence of a DPIA is not just a technical defect. It removes evidence that Meridian considered necessity, proportionality, less intrusive alternatives, retention, access control, risks to employees, or special concerns arising from covert audio capture. [D13, Part 4.]

The Data Protection Act 2018 provides the domestic framework for general processing. The available statutory table of contents confirms that its Part 2 concerns general processing and that it contains provisions on the Information Commissioner and enforcement. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/12" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Data Protection Act 2018 Law</a>. The bundle invokes DPA 2018 ss 35-40, but the specific operative provisions and their applicability would need to be checked against the precise processing purpose before any final statutory conclusion is drawn. [D13, Part 4.]

## E. Article 8 ECHR

The welfare cabin and drying room are not equivalent to a public construction area. Employees can have a substantial expectation of privacy in staff welfare spaces, especially where covert audio recording occurs and the employer deliberately avoided notice.

The Article 8 concern is stronger in the Employment Tribunal context because Meridian seeks to use the product of the surveillance against its former employee. [D13, Part 4.]

## F. Separate Employment Tribunal admissibility question

An unlawful or improperly obtained recording is not automatically excluded. But the tribunal can regulate evidence to secure a fair hearing.

The offered recording is weakly probative:

- Ms Raghunathan is not audible; [D13, Part 4]
- the alleged statement is attributed to a former colleague, not Ms Raghunathan; [D13, Part 4]
- it is relied upon only as a broad attack on her account of workplace relations; [D13, Part 4]
- the acquisition of the recording appears potentially retaliatory and privacy-invasive. [D13, Part 4.]

The tribunal should exclude it, or at minimum give it very little weight. It risks a collateral trial about data protection, covert surveillance and a non-party’s unattributed statement, while contributing little to the central statutory question: why Meridian dismissed Ms Raghunathan on 8 December 2023. [D18, items 18.1-18.6.]

## G. Was installation itself a detriment under ERA 1996, s 47B?

### Legal test

A worker has the right not to suffer detriment by an employer act or deliberate failure to act done on the ground that the worker made a protected disclosure. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/47B" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 47B Law</a>.

For a s 47B complaint, the employer bears the burden of showing the ground on which the act or failure was done. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/48" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 48 Law</a>.

### Application

There is a strong prima facie case that the surveillance was a detriment, or at least part of retaliatory conduct:

- it was directed at identifying who had been “talking to the HSE” and Coldbrook; [D13, Part 4]
- the contemporaneous memorandum follows Ms Raghunathan’s internal disclosure and HSE contact; [D18, item 18.1; D01, para 20; D13, Part 4]
- the system was covert and installed in welfare facilities. [D13, Part 4]

The key limitation is factual: the monitoring was installed generally in the welfare cabin and drying room, not solely against Ms Raghunathan. [D13, Part 4.] The tribunal must determine whether she personally suffered a detriment. The answer may be yes if:

- she worked in, used, avoided, or reasonably feared use of those facilities;
- the surveillance was part of a campaign to identify and deter her regulator contact;
- the conduct altered her working environment or placed her at a disadvantage.

Even if she did not know about it at the time, covert monitoring can potentially be a detriment if it was directed at obtaining material to her disadvantage. But the lack of contemporaneous knowledge bears on practical disadvantage and compensation.

# 3.4 Hearsay

## A. Governing distinction

The Criminal Justice Act 2003 hearsay provisions apply to criminal proceedings. The relevant provisions expressly apply in criminal proceedings and have England-and-Wales extent. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/114" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 114 Law</a>; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/116" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 116 Law</a>; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/121" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 121 Law</a>.

Accordingly:

- **Crown Court:** Criminal Justice Act 2003 applies directly.
- **TCC:** the CJA 2003 provisions do not govern civil evidence; hearsay is generally admissible subject to civil procedure, notice, weight and fairness.
- **Employment Tribunal:** not bound by the strict criminal hearsay code; reliability and procedural fairness predominate.
- **Coroner’s court:** may receive hearsay for its inquisitorial investigation, but should identify limitations and avoid treating contested hearsay as conclusive proof of criminal conduct.

## B. The deceased’s SMS: D11 section 3

### First-level hearsay

The SMS was received on Mrs Wieczorek’s device from the number registered to the deceased at 09:41. [D11, section 3.] Mr Wieczorek is dead. [D01, para 7.]

In the Crown Court, section 116 permits hearsay if the maker is identified to the court’s satisfaction, the maker’s oral evidence would have been admissible, and one of the statutory conditions is met, including death. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/116" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 116 Law</a>.

The Crown can make a substantial case for admission of the first-level statement:

- the purported maker is identified as Mr Wieczorek;
- he is deceased; [D01, para 7]
- the message was received on his wife’s device from his registered number; [D11, section 3]
- cell-site evidence places the handset within the sector serving the site until 11:22. [D11, section 3]
- it was apparently sent close to the events it describes. [D11, section 3.]

However, the forensic examiner cannot say who physically operated the handset. [D11, section 3 and para 4.1.] That is not necessarily fatal, but it is a material limitation.

### Second-level hearsay: “Gareth said”

The SMS says, in translation, that “boss says load the platform even though Gareth said not to.” [D11, section 3.]

The deceased’s statement that Gareth said not to load is an earlier hearsay statement embedded within the SMS. Section 121 imposes an additional requirement before hearsay can be admitted to prove that an earlier hearsay statement was made. The statute requires one of specified routes, including agreement or the court’s conclusion that the evidential value, taking account of reliability, is so high that the interests of justice require admission. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/121" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 121 Law</a>.

The prosecution’s better route is therefore to use the SMS for the limited proposition that Mr Wieczorek sent a contemporaneous message expressing concern, rather than as definitive proof that:

- Gareth Pyle in fact instructed that loading must not occur;
- an identified “boss” directed loading;
- the “boss” was Mr Ferris, Mr Delacroix-Hale or any other particular person.

The word translated as “boss” can mean a particular individual or management generally. [D11, section 3.] The identity of “Gareth” is also not express. [D11, section 3.]

### Recommended criminal ruling

Admit only if the court is satisfied as to the statutory route and gives a careful limiting direction. The statement should be treated cautiously, particularly in relation to the asserted words of Gareth and identity of the “boss.”

### TCC, ET and coroner

The statement is potentially admissible in all three, but its weight is limited by:

- uncertain handset operator; [D11, para 4.1]
- ambiguous “boss”; [D11, section 3]
- unspecified Gareth; [D11, section 3]
- translation issue. [D11, section 3.]

## C. Crane driver’s statement: D14 Part 2

Mr Kavanagh-Mbatha reportedly said that he was told over radio to carry on, did not know by whom, and assumed it was the Appointed Person because that person normally gave the instruction. [D14, Part 2.]

### Crown Court

This is hearsay if the driver does not give oral evidence. He is outside the United Kingdom and has not responded to correspondence. [D14, Part 2.]

Section 116 permits hearsay where a witness is outside the UK and it is not reasonably practicable to secure attendance. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/116" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 116 Law</a>.

But the prosecution must show reasonable steps to secure attendance. It should explore:

- live video-link evidence;
- a formal request through the appropriate process;
- a commission or other permitted mechanism;
- detailed evidence of contact attempts.

The statement is weak on identity. The driver did not identify the speaker and made an assumption. [D14, Part 2.] It may prove that an instruction was heard, but it is weak evidence that Mr Delacroix-Hale gave it.

### Other fora

The statement is more readily receivable, but still weak. The TCC, ET and coroner should distinguish:

- direct perception: the driver heard an instruction to resume; and
- inference: the driver assumed it was the Appointed Person.

The latter is not evidence of the speaker’s identity.

## D. Mr Ashgrove-Pike’s solicitors’ assertions: D09 Part 4

The bundle reports that, through solicitors in Dubai, Mr Ashgrove-Pike said he never issued a certificate, never approved a two-tonne load, and would not have done so. He has declined to make a signed statement or attend. [D09, Part 4.]

This is at least double hearsay:

1. Mr Ashgrove-Pike’s alleged out-of-court statement;
2. the solicitor’s communication of it.

### Crown Court

The evidence is problematic. It may be possible to seek admission under the “outside the UK” limb only if statutory conditions and reasonable efforts are shown. But the lack of signed statement, lack of cross-examination, and absence of evidence about the precise instructions given to the solicitors make it dangerous evidence.

It should not be admitted as proof of falsity unless the prosecution establishes a proper statutory route and the judge considers the fairness implications. At most, it may support further investigative steps.

The stronger available evidence of the certificate’s provenance lies elsewhere:

- the absent Halbrook register entry; [D09, Part 3]
- absence of a Halbrook job number, invoice and digital signature; [D09, Parts 1 and 3]
- file metadata; [D09, Part 2]
- Mr Ashgrove-Pike’s direct email of 26 October. [D09, Part 4.]

### TCC, ET and coroner

It may be received as hearsay subject to appropriate notice and weight, but should carry little weight. The tribunal should not turn an unsworn solicitors’ assertion into a decisive finding of fraud.

## E. Met Office and Halbrook records

### Met Office record

The Met Office certified record showing a 43 mph gust at Leeds Bradford Airport is described in D01. [D01, para 12.] That is capable of being business or official record evidence. Its material limitation is not necessarily hearsay admissibility; it is the inference from an airport measurement to wind conditions at the specific site and elevation.

The relevant evidential dispute is therefore:

- what the certified airport record proves;
- whether it reliably represents gusts at Brackenmoor Wharf at 21.4 metres;
- how it compares with the site anemometer log, which recorded 21 mph at 11:15 but whose device has not been found or tested. [D01, para 12.]

### Halbrook register and ledger

The register and ledger record entries for LB-1 to LB-6 and no entry for LB-7. [D09, Part 3.] These are capable of being business-record evidence. Their apparent regularity, systematic character and consistency with the six earlier certificates make them potentially probative.

But they do not conclusively establish that no individual at Halbrook did any off-system work. Halbrook itself reserves its position on whether Mr Ashgrove-Pike did work outside its systems. [D09, Part 3.]

The correct formulation is therefore:

- the records are good evidence that Halbrook’s ordinary formal process did not generate TWDC-BW-07;
- they are not conclusive proof that no earlier genuine certificate existed or that Mr Ashgrove-Pike did nothing outside the formal system.

# 3.5 Dr Vantrease’s report

## Forum

| Issue | Crown Court | TCC | Employment Tribunal | Coroner’s court |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expert admissibility | CrimPR Part 19; trial fairness | CPR Part 35 and permission | May receive expert evidence if useful and fair | May receive expert evidence for inquiry |
| Contingent fee | Serious independence issue | Serious independence issue | Weight and fairness | Weight and fairness |
| Financial dependence | Weight, potential independence issue | Weight, potential independence issue | Weight | Weight |
| Ultimate issue opinion | Must not usurp jury | Court controls scope | Tribunal can disregard legal conclusion | Coroner must retain own fact-finding role |
| D20.2 reliance | Affects foundation and weight | Affects foundation and weight | Peripheral unless relied on | Affects weight |

## A. Initial admissibility

Dr Vantrease is clearly qualified as a structural engineer and gives opinion evidence on structural causation, design capacity, ties, loading, wind, and temporary-works control. [D19, sections 1 and 2.]

Those are areas potentially within expert competence. The report should not be excluded wholesale merely because it is contested or because Professor Danecourt disagrees. Professor Danecourt accepts the absence of three ties and agrees with the view that a two-tonne certificate was not the product of a reconstructible calculation. [D20, item 20.1, paras 1 and 7.] The competing experts substantially disagree on wind and causation, which is a classic matter for tested expert evidence rather than automatic exclusion. [D19, paras 1.1-1.5; D20, item 20.1, paras 2-6.]

## B. The false declaration and success uplift

The declaration stated that Dr Vantrease had not entered an arrangement under which his fee or payment depended on the outcome. [D19, section 3.]

Appendix G later disclosed a 25 percent success uplift payable if Coldbrook’s claim succeeded in whole or substantial part. [D19, Appendix G, para G4.]

That produces a direct and serious inconsistency. On the document’s face:

- the contingent-fee term existed when the engagement was countersigned on 14 February 2024; [D19, Appendix G, para G4]
- the report and declaration were dated 8 August 2024; [D19, section 3]
- Dr Vantrease says he did not read the clause and became aware of it only on 27 February 2025. [D19, Appendix G, para G4.]

The question is not merely whether he consciously lied. The declaration was objectively inaccurate if the contractual success uplift existed at the time. His asserted lack of knowledge may affect professional culpability, but it does not remove the conflict or cure the original declaration.

A later statement from Ashen & Fyfe that the uplift will not be enforced does not retrospectively make the original engagement non-contingent. [D19, Annex, letter of 21 March 2025.]

### Consequence

The court should not necessarily exclude all engineering opinion. But it should require:

- production of the full engagement terms;
- an unequivocal deed or binding amendment removing the uplift;
- confirmation whether any uplift has accrued, been invoiced, reserved or insured;
- disclosure of all communications concerning fee arrangements;
- a supplemental independent declaration;
- permission for cross-examination on independence.

The success uplift affects the expert’s duty to the court, not merely the commercial relationship between Coldbrook and its solicitors.

## C. The 61 percent financial dependence

Dr Vantrease’s company obtained 61 percent of its turnover through a framework agreement with Coldbrook’s Luxembourg parent in two successive years. [D19, Appendix G, para G2.]

That framework was said to concern unrelated technical due diligence. [D19, Appendix G, para G3.] The unrelated subject matter is relevant, but it does not eliminate concern. A sole shareholder/director whose company derives a majority of turnover from the instructing party’s corporate group has a significant financial relationship requiring candid disclosure.

The correct conclusion is **weight and independence concern, not automatic exclusion**. It should be assessed alongside:

- the contingent uplift; [D19, Appendix G, para G4]
- the earlier non-disclosure; [D19, Appendix G, para G3]
- previous work involving Ashen & Fyfe. [D19, Appendix G, para G5]
- whether the expert can demonstrate independence in analysis and in instructions.

## D. Service before permission: CPR 35.4

No TCC order permitting expert evidence was made until 16 April 2025. Dr Vantrease’s report had been served on 8 August 2024. [D19, procedural note.]

That means the report was served before the required TCC permission was granted. The usual consequence is not automatic inadmissibility. The court may retrospectively grant permission, regularise service, impose conditions, restrict issues, direct a replacement report, or make costs orders.

Here, the court ultimately gave Meridian permission to rely on Professor Danecourt in the same 16 April 2025 order. [D19, procedural note; D20, item 20.1.] That reduces prejudice, since the evidential field is no longer one-sided.

Nevertheless, the TCC should not simply ignore the procedural breach. It should require explanation of:

- why the report was served without permission;
- whether its circulation caused costs or tactical prejudice;
- whether it was used in mediation, interim applications or disclosure before permission;
- whether the expert was instructed on an impermissibly broad basis.

## E. Reliance on D20.2 at paragraph 2.5

Dr Vantrease says he assumed that the covert recording was accurate and relied on the 02:40 passage in paragraph 6.19 of his full report. [D19, para 2.5.]

That foundation is defective because D20 records three tenable readings and Meridian’s audio examiner says the crucial words are not reliably resolvable. [D20, item 20.2, 02:40.]

An expert in structural engineering should not base a structural causation conclusion on a contested linguistic interpretation without identifying the assumption and providing alternative conclusions. If the assumption is material, the court should require a supplemental report that:

1. identifies the exact proposition derived from 02:40;
2. explains whether the engineering conclusion changes if:
   - “it wasn’t real” is adopted;
   - “if it was real” is adopted;
   - “it was real” is adopted; or
   - the passage is excluded altogether;
3. removes any factual assertion outside engineering expertise.

This is a substantial **weight and methodology** concern. It may justify exclusion of the affected opinion, but not necessarily all of the structural analysis.

## F. Paragraph 1.7: gross negligence and ultimate issue

Dr Vantrease opines that Mr Ferris was grossly negligent and that his conduct substantially caused Mr Wieczorek’s death. [D19, para 1.7.]

The first proposition is impermissible in form. Whether Mr Ferris was grossly negligent is not a structural-engineering question. It is a legal and evaluative issue for the jury in the Crown Court and for the court or tribunal in civil proceedings.

Professor Danecourt correctly identifies that gross negligence is not a question of structural engineering and declines to express a view. [D20, item 20.1, para 8.]

The expert may properly address matters such as:

- technical adequacy of the lifting plan;
- wind-trigger consistency;
- foreseeable effects of loading and missing ties;
- temporary-works practice;
- whether engineering standards were met;
- causative significance of specific operational decisions.

The expert must not tell the fact-finder that the legal threshold of gross negligence is satisfied.

## G. Recommended orders

### TCC

Seek an order that:

1. permits reliance only on an amended report;
2. strikes out or disregards paragraph 1.7;
3. requires a supplemental declaration addressing G2-G5;
4. requires full disclosure of all retainer and fee documents;
5. requires a revised report that removes dependence on the disputed D20.2 wording or states alternative conclusions;
6. orders a meeting of experts and joint statement identifying agreements and disagreements;
7. reserves costs consequences of premature service and non-disclosure.

### Crown Court

The trial judge should conduct a voir dire or pre-trial admissibility ruling. The likely result should be:

- admissible structural opinion within expertise;
- paragraph 1.7 excluded or tightly redacted;
- success-fee and financial-dependence matters available for cross-examination;
- no reliance on a disputed transcript as established fact;
- clear instruction that the jury decides gross negligence and causation in the legal sense.

### Employment Tribunal and coroner

The report can be received as relevant technical material, but neither forum should treat paragraph 1.7 as a legal conclusion it must adopt. The coroner should take particular care not to determine criminal liability.

# 3.6 Ms Raghunathan’s Employment Tribunal claim

## Forum

This is principally an **Employment Tribunal** claim. It is not resolved by the Crown Court, TCC, or coroner’s court.

- The **Employment Tribunal** determines s 103A automatic unfair dismissal, s 47B detriment, wages and contractual notice claims. [D18, p 1.]
- The **Crown Court** may consider evidence relevant to alleged criminal conduct, but does not award whistleblowing remedies.
- The **TCC** resolves the commercial contract dispute; it does not decide unfair dismissal.
- The **coroner’s court** may investigate the death’s circumstances but does not determine employment compensation.

## A. Qualifying service is not needed for s 103A

Ms Raghunathan had 1 year, 10 months and 29 days’ service, so she lacks the two years ordinarily required for an ordinary unfair dismissal claim. [D18, p 1.]

But her claim is not an ordinary s 98 claim. Section 103A provides that an employee is regarded as unfairly dismissed where the reason, or principal reason, for dismissal was that the employee made a protected disclosure. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/103A" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 103A Law</a>.

The Court of Appeal has confirmed that a dismissal is automatically unfair where the reason or principal reason is protected disclosure, and that complaints of detriment or dismissal for a protected disclosure may be brought in the Employment Tribunal. [*Phyllis Sullivan v Isle of Wight Council* [2025] EWCA Civ 379 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2025/379), para 15.]

The lack of two years’ service is therefore no answer to the s 103A claim.

## B. Was there a qualifying and protected disclosure?

### Internal email, 16 November

The email identifies concrete information:

- the LB-7 certificate;
- its late appearance on the shared drive;
- its stated date;
- absence of a register entry;
- absence of a Halbrook job number;
- concern that it had been created after the event;
- concern that use of it to obtain £610,000 would amount to a false representation;
- concern about serious health-and-safety breaches and a death. [D10, item 10.7; D18, item 18.1.]

That is not a bare assertion of wrongdoing. It is information tending to show possible criminal conduct, breach of legal obligations, endangerment of health and safety, and possible deliberate concealment.

Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43B defines a qualifying disclosure as disclosure of information which, in the worker’s reasonable belief, is in the public interest and tends to show one or more specified categories, including a criminal offence, breach of legal obligation, danger to health or safety, or deliberate concealment. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/43B" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43B Law</a>.

The internal email has a very strong prospect of satisfying s 43B.

Meridian’s reliance on an earlier bonus grievance does not answer that question. [D18, item 18.1.] A person can have mixed motives. The decisive question is whether she reasonably believed the disclosed information tended to show the statutory categories and that disclosure was in the public interest.

### HSE telephone call, 17 November

D01 records that Ms Raghunathan made a disclosure to the inspector by telephone at 08:41 on 17 November. [D01, para 20.] D18 states her case that this was disclosure to a prescribed person. [D18, item 18.1.]

Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43F provides a route for a qualifying disclosure to a prescribed person where the worker reasonably believes the relevant failure falls within that person’s prescribed remit and that the information and allegation are substantially true. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/43F" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43F Law</a>.

The actual content of the phone call and proof of Meridian’s knowledge of it remain disputed. [D18, item 18.6.] The internal written disclosure is, however, enough to support the protected-disclosure case if made to the employer.

## C. Reason or principal reason for dismissal

### Evidence supporting Ms Raghunathan

The tribunal is likely to find the following evidence collectively powerful:

1. **Close timing.**  
   The internal disclosure was sent on 16 November. [D10, item 10.7.] The HSE contact occurred on 17 November. [D01, para 20.] Dismissal followed on 8 December. [D18, item 18.3.]

2. **Mr Ferris’s response.**  
   He told her not to contact HSE or anyone else, and that all communications about the incident should go through Halloway Brant. [D10, item 10.8.]

3. **No redundancy process.**  
   Meridian accepts there was no consultation, no pool and no selection criteria. [D18, item 18.3; D18, item 18.6.]

4. **Defective board minute.**  
   The minute is dated 2 November but refers to an incident which occurred on 14 November. [D18, item 18.2.] The electronic copy was created on 29 April 2024, and the alleged manuscript original has not been produced. [D18, item 18.2.]

5. **Attendance defect.**  
   The minute identifies Mr Nightingale-Obi as attending, though he had resigned on 27 October, before its stated date. [D18, item 18.2.]

6. **Post hoc explanation.**  
   Meridian says the minute was prepared later from notes, with the incident reference inserted in error and a departed director’s name carried over from a template. [D18, item 18.2.] That explanation is possible, but it itself concedes that the document is not a reliable contemporaneous record in the form advanced.

7. **Subsequent recruitment.**  
   Meridian advertised for a senior QS in Leeds at £62,000 to £70,000 within a month of dismissal. [D18, item 18.5.] Ms Raghunathan’s salary was £64,500. [D18, item 18.5.] Meridian says it was a different role, grade and project, but the advertisement does not identify a project. [D18, item 18.5.]

8. **The dismissal letter’s confidentiality demand.**  
   The letter says she must not disclose company information relating to Brackenmoor to any third party, including a regulator, without consent. [D18, item 18.3.] That is strongly consistent with concern about reporting, not an ordinary redundancy dismissal.

### Meridian’s case

Meridian will say:

- there was a genuine commercial restructuring;
- the board had already decided to remove one senior QS role;
- the board minute was merely imperfectly typed up later;
- the January role was different;
- the claimant had a pre-existing bonus dispute. [D18, items 18.1, 18.2 and 18.5.]

Those matters may be capable of supporting a non-retaliatory case. But they are presently undermined by the documentary defects and lack of ordinary redundancy safeguards.

### Likely outcome

On the supplied material, Ms Raghunathan has a strong case that the protected disclosure was at least a material reason and likely the principal reason for dismissal. A tribunal need not accept Meridian’s stated “restructuring” label at face value when the record said to prove it is demonstrably problematic.

## D. The board minute and its four defects

The four principal defects are:

1. **Chronological impossibility:** it refers to the Brackenmoor incident even though it is dated twelve days before that incident. [D18, item 18.2.]
2. **Late production:** it was omitted from initial disclosure and produced only after a specific-disclosure order. [D18, item 18.2.]
3. **Metadata problem:** the electronic version was created on 29 April 2024, long after the asserted meeting. [D18, item 18.2.]
4. **Attendance impossibility:** the minute records a director who had resigned before the minute’s stated date. [D18, item 18.2.]

The tribunal should not automatically find fabrication merely because these defects exist. Meridian’s later explanation must be tested. [D18, item 18.2.] But the defects strongly justify an adverse inference that the document is unreliable and may have been created or adapted to rationalise a decision made later.

Strike-out is a severe remedy. The better immediate order is likely to be:

- specific disclosure of manuscript notes, drafts, document versions, board-calendar entries, email circulation, and metadata;
- disclosure of the 12 February 2024 certified list;
- witness evidence from every alleged attendee;
- a costs warning and potential deposit/costs order;
- an adverse inference if Meridian cannot adequately explain non-production and provenance.

## E. Clause 9: voidness and detriment

Clause 9 purported to prohibit reports or communications to HSE, police, coroner, insurer and any regulator about Meridian or Brackenmoor. [D18, item 18.4.] It imposed repayment, indemnity and injunctive-relief consequences. [D18, item 18.4.]

Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43J provides that a term in an agreement is void insofar as it purports to preclude a worker from making a protected disclosure. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/43J" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43J Law</a>.

Clause 9 is therefore void to the extent it purports to stop protected disclosures.

Its withdrawal on 20 December does not erase the fact it was proposed on 11 December, after dismissal and shortly after the whistleblowing events. [D18, item 18.4.]

### Was proposing it a detriment?

There is a substantial arguable s 47B claim.

A reasonable worker could regard a proposed term threatening repayment of the entire settlement sum, indemnity, and injunctions if she reported to regulators as a threat or disadvantage. [D18, item 18.4.] It would be particularly serious where the employee had already made a disclosure about a fatal workplace incident. [D10, item 10.7; D18, item 18.1.]

Meridian’s “junior HR error” explanation must be tested against the fact that the draft was sent with a letter signed by the Head of HR and copied to Mr Ferris. [D18, item 18.4.]

The employer bears the burden of showing the ground for the act alleged to be a detriment. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/48" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 48 Law</a>.

## F. Remedies

### Section 103A dismissal

If the tribunal finds the s 103A claim well founded, the dismissal is automatically unfair. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/103A" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 103A Law</a>.

The bundle does not provide a retrieved statutory source for the detailed dismissal-compensation provisions, so I do not state a numerical cap or an exact statutory enhancement without a verified source. In practical terms, Ms Raghunathan should claim full financial losses flowing from the dismissal, including past and future loss of earnings, pension and benefits, subject to mitigation and any applicable statutory regime.

### Section 47B detriment

Where a s 47B complaint succeeds, the tribunal must make a declaration and may award compensation that is just and equitable for the infringement and loss attributable to it. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/49" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 49 Law</a>.

The statute also permits compensation for reasonably incurred expense and lost benefits, requires mitigation, and allows reduction for contributory conduct where just and equitable. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/49" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 49 Law</a>.

The statute provides a possible reduction of up to 25 percent where a protected disclosure was not made in good faith. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/49" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 49 Law</a>. On the facts currently available, there is no persuasive basis for such a reduction: the email identified detailed concerns after a death and possible false certification. [D10, item 10.7.]

### Uplifts and costs

The tribunal should consider costs consequences if Meridian’s disclosure conduct, defective minute, or surveillance evidence shows unreasonable conduct. [D18, item 18.2; D13, Part 4.]

A separate statutory uplift may arise only if a verified applicable procedural code and its statutory conditions are established. The bundle does not provide enough verified statutory material to state that such an uplift is presently available or quantify it.

## G. Interim relief

The bundle records an Employment Tribunal claim but not its presentation date. [D18, p 1.] Interim relief for qualifying whistleblowing dismissal is a time-critical remedy. It must be sought within the strict statutory period after the effective date of termination. Because the dismissal date was 8 December 2023, [D18, items 18.1 and 18.3] any ordinary interim-relief deadline is necessarily long expired by the time of this bundle. It should nonetheless be considered in any analogous future case immediately after dismissal.

## H. Legal representatives: any obligation arising from D18?

Yes, potentially.

### Meridian’s representatives

The combination of:

- a dismissal letter seeking to prevent reports to regulators; [D18, item 18.3]
- proposed clause 9 threatening repayment, indemnity and injunctions for such reports; [D18, item 18.4]
- the apparently defective board minute; [D18, item 18.2]
- the surveillance memorandum targeting those talking to HSE; [D13, Part 4]

creates a serious need for Meridian’s legal representatives to ensure that they do not advance a misleading factual case, deploy a document without proper basis, or participate in an attempt to suppress protected disclosures.

A confidentiality clause cannot retrospectively remove statutory protection for a protected disclosure already made. Other provisions in a settlement agreement may nonetheless bind the employee, including provisions about waiver of claims, non-disparagement, repayment or confidentiality unrelated to protected disclosures. Independent advice before any signature is essential.

### Ms Raghunathan’s representatives

They should:

- preserve original emails, metadata and HSE-contact records;
- avoid overstating the evidence as proof of criminal guilt;
- make focused disclosure applications concerning the board minute and surveillance;
- seek orders preventing reliance on unlawfully obtained surveillance material;
- keep the whistleblowing case analytically distinct from the criminal allegations.

### Representatives in the Crown Court and TCC

Any representative who receives D10.6 or D20.3 should preserve it but not exploit it before the privilege ruling. If a lawyer comes to possess material apparently disclosed by mistake and plainly privileged, the proper course is to notify the producing side and seek directions rather than mining the material for tactical advantage.
