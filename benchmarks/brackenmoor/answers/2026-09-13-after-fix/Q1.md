# CPS Advice: Criminal Liability and Conflicting Evidence

## Executive conclusion

There is a **realistic prospect of conviction** of Meridian Structures Ltd for corporate manslaughter, subject to two essential evidential qualifications: first, the Crown must prove that the fatal management failure was substantially attributable to persons who were “senior management” within the statutory definition; secondly, expert causation evidence must be presented so that the jury can be sure that the organisation’s gross breach was a more than minimal cause of Tomasz Wieczorek’s death.

The corporate case is materially stronger than the proposed gross-negligence manslaughter case against Mr Ferris personally. The corporation can be convicted through an unsafe organisational system, including the concentration of safety-critical functions, deficient temporary-works control, and potentially senior-management pressure to proceed. A conviction of Mr Ferris for gross negligence manslaughter requires proof of **his own** duty, breach, causation, and grossness. The present evidence gives a proper basis to investigate and potentially charge him, but his personal causative involvement is less secure than Meridian’s organisational liability.

A charge under section 37 HSWA 1974 against Mr Ferris has stronger prospects than a personal manslaughter charge if the corporate HSWA offences are proved. It requires the Crown to prove that Meridian’s offence occurred with Mr Ferris’s consent or connivance, or was attributable to his neglect. The evidence of his safety portfolio, approval of a lifting plan with an apparently unsafe wind threshold, commercial-pressure messages, and the alleged response to the known defect is capable of meeting that test. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/37/section/37" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, s 37 Law</a>

The public-interest limb of the Full Code Test is plainly met if the evidential stage is met: the case concerns a workplace death, very serious injury to another worker, alleged safety-system failures, and possible post-incident manipulation or suppression of evidence. The immediate charging decision should nevertheless await the further enquiries identified at section 1.5 below.

## Applicable legal framework

### Corporate manslaughter

Under section 1 of the Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, a corporation commits corporate manslaughter where:

1. the way in which its activities were managed or organised caused a person’s death;
2. this amounted to a gross breach of a relevant duty of care owed by the organisation to that person; and
3. senior management’s management or organisation of those activities was a substantial element in that breach.

A “gross” breach is one falling far below what could reasonably be expected in the circumstances. “Senior management” means persons who play significant roles either in deciding how all or a substantial part of the organisation’s activities are managed or organised, or in actually managing or organising all or a substantial part of those activities. The offence applies in England and Wales and is punishable by an unlimited fine on indictment. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 1 Law</a>

The relevant duty is a duty under negligence law, including a duty connected with construction operations. Whether that duty exists is a legal question for the judge, who makes the necessary factual findings. On the supplied facts, Meridian’s operation as design-and-build contractor and principal contractor is capable of establishing a relevant duty to a worker performing construction activity on its site. D01, paras 3-5; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/2" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 2 Law</a>

Where gross breach is in issue, the jury must consider failures to comply with relevant health-and-safety legislation, their seriousness, and the risk of death they posed. It may also consider the organisation’s attitudes, policies, systems, and accepted practices which encouraged or tolerated such failures. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/8" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 8 Law</a>

### Gross negligence manslaughter: Ferris

The individual offence requires proof that:

1. Mr Ferris owed the deceased a duty of care;
2. he negligently breached that duty;
3. the breach caused death;
4. the breach exposed the deceased to a risk of death; and
5. the circumstances were so reprehensible as to amount to gross negligence.

The Court of Appeal has stated those elements in *Misra*. [*R v Misra* [2004] EWCA Crim 2375 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2004/2375)

The Crown must prove that Mr Ferris’s breach made a significant, meaning more than minimal, contribution to the death. [*R v Wood Treatment Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim 618 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2021/618)

### Section 37 HSWA: Ferris

Section 37 makes a director, manager, secretary, or similar officer personally guilty where an offence by the company is proved to have been committed with that person’s consent or connivance, or to have been attributable to that person’s neglect. The individual is then punishable as well as the company. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/37/section/37" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, s 37 Law</a>

The underlying corporate duties include the employer’s duty, so far as reasonably practicable, to provide safe systems, instruction, training and supervision for employees, and the duty to conduct its undertaking so that non-employees affected by it are not exposed to health-and-safety risks. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/37/section/2" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, ss 2-3 Law</a>; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/37/section/3" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, s 3 Law</a>

# 1.1 Senior management, attribution, and the divergence between corporate and individual liability

## A. The evidence capable of proving the section 1(3) senior-management element

The statutory question is functional, not merely formal: did the relevant person play a significant role in making decisions about, or actually managing or organising, the whole or a substantial part of Meridian’s activities? <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 1(3)-(4) Law</a>

### 1. Mr Ferris

The evidence capable of placing Mr Ferris within section 1(4)(c) is substantial.

- Mr Ferris was Meridian’s Construction Director. His own prepared statement accepts responsibility for “resourcing, commercial delivery and client relationships across the division,” even while denying day-to-day operational responsibility at Brackenmoor. D07, Part A.
- Meridian had no board-level health-and-safety director after a February 2023 restructure. Ms Kettleborough says that Mr Ferris held the health-and-safety portfolio in addition to construction delivery and that she reported directly to him. D06, para 2.
- Ms Kettleborough says she raised with him, in July and September 2023, the concern that Mr Delacroix-Hale held too many operational roles. D06, paras 3-4.
- The temporary-works register bears Ms Kettleborough’s annotation that the TWC role was to move to her “when resource allows,” which she says followed a discussion with Mr Ferris; no change occurred. D06, para 4.
- The lifting plan identified Mr Delacroix-Hale as Appointed Person, contained a 23 mph working-level wind trigger, and was countersigned by Mr Ferris in the box marked “Director approval — high risk activity.” The crane manufacturer’s stated limit for the relevant panel sail area was 20 mph. D12, Part 3.
- The forensic extraction records Mr Ferris as sending a message on 13 November 2023: “Whatever it takes. Milestone 6 must certify by Friday or we’re funding it ourselves.” D11, section 1. A further email attributed to him stated: “December is not an option”; “I don’t need to know the detail. I need the milestone certified. Find a way through it and tell me when it’s done.” That followed an email referring to a problem “flagged” at levels 6 and 7. D10, items 10.4-10.5.
- The message history records Mr Ferris responding “Good” after Mr Delacroix-Hale said “Sorted. Paper is in order.” D11, section 1.
- Ms Kettleborough says Mr Ferris asked her after the death to “go through the files and make sure everything is tidy before the inspector comes back.” D06, para 25. He accepted in interview that he may have said something to that effect. D07, Part B.
- He was copied into, and replied to, Ms Raghunathan’s 16 November warning that the LB-7 document might have been created after the event, might be used to obtain £610,000, and might involve both criminal conduct and a serious health-and-safety breach. D10, items 10.7-10.8.

This is capable of showing that Mr Ferris did not merely occupy a title. A jury could find he exercised substantial divisional power over construction delivery, safety governance, resource allocation, high-risk activity approval, and commercial decisions creating pressure on the site. That is evidence relevant to both limbs of the definition of senior management. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 1(4)(c) Law</a>

### 2. Mr Delacroix-Hale

The same statutory test can potentially encompass Mr Delacroix-Hale despite his site-level title.

The HSE records that he was simultaneously Site Manager, Temporary Works Coordinator, Appointed Person for lifting, and Permit Issuing Officer. D01, para 13. He is said to have issued the permit for LB-7, with each of its four safety preconditions marked as met, despite the blank TWC countersignature on the certificate, the competing handover records, and the absence of a recorded authority to restart lifting. D14, Part 4.

He was also the administrator of the “BW SITE LEADS” group; that material records him saying “LB-7 loaded today. Don’t overthink it” at 07:12, acknowledging Ms Kettleborough’s suspension message at 10:44, and stating “Last one then we’re off” at 11:07 after another participant queried whether the crane was slewing again. D11, section 1.

Whether that amounts to management of “the whole or a substantial part” of Meridian’s activities is fact-sensitive. The Crown should not assume that management of one construction site automatically qualifies. However, where the company’s relevant activity is the management and delivery of a major redevelopment and the site manager has been entrusted with three safety-critical control functions, the argument is realistic. D01, paras 3, 13; D14, Part 4.

### 3. The organisational-system case

Even if the jury were not sure that Mr Ferris personally ordered the fatal lift, it could find that Meridian’s system was grossly unsafe because it combined or tolerated:

- concentration of the TWC, Appointed Person and Permit Issuing Officer functions in one person, removing independent safety checks, D01, para 13; D19, para 1.6; D20.1, para 7;
- a lack of any effective transition despite the proposed transfer of the TWC role, D06, para 4;
- chronic late or missing temporary-works documentation, D06, paras 5-8;
- a lifting plan approved at director level with a stated 23 mph trigger when the crane limitation for the panels was 20 mph, D12, Part 3;
- an alleged operational decision to resume lifting without the required recorded authority, D12, Part 3; D14, Parts 1-2;
- documents apparently created or altered after the event, including the disputed LB-7 certificate and competing scaffold inspection records. D08, Parts 1-3; D09, Parts 1-3.

That is a statutory systems case. Section 8 expressly permits the jury to examine the company’s practices, policies and systems, not just a single act on the day. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/8" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 8 Law</a>

## B. D16.3: Bilta and Singularis do not answer the corporate-manslaughter question

Meridian relies in D16.3 on attribution principles associated with *Bilta* and *Singularis*, contending that any individual who created the document acted outside his authority and that his knowledge should not be attributed to Meridian. D16.3, para 4.

Those authorities concern civil-law questions of attribution in their own contexts. The authorities named in the bundle have been verified as *Jetivia SA v Bilta (UK) Ltd (in liquidation)* [2015] UKSC 23 and *Singularis Holdings Ltd (in official liquidation) v Daiwa Capital Markets Europe Ltd* [2019] UKSC 50. [*Jetivia SA v Bilta (UK) Ltd (in liquidation)* [2015] UKSC 23 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/23); [*Singularis Holdings Ltd (in official liquidation) v Daiwa Capital Markets Europe Ltd* [2019] UKSC 50 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2019/50)

They should be distinguished for three reasons.

1. **The statutory test is express and functional.** Section 1 does not ask whether a particular employee’s dishonest knowledge is attributed to the company under a general common-law rule. It asks whether the way the company’s activities were managed or organised by senior management was a substantial element in the gross breach. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, s 1 Law</a>

2. **The Crown’s case need not depend on attributing a forged certificate to Meridian through the knowledge of a rogue employee.** It can contend that senior-management failures consisted of governance: placing incompatible functions in one individual; failing to rectify a known concentration of roles; approving a deficient lifting plan; failing to maintain workable checks; and applying commercial pressure in the face of a known safety concern. D01, para 13; D06, paras 2-4; D10, items 10.4-10.5; D12, Part 3.

3. **“Outside authority” is not a complete answer where the alleged organisational breach lies in the delegation, absence of supervision, or tolerance of the very conduct said to be unauthorised.** The jury may find the site manager acted improperly. It may also find that the unsafe organisational structure and absence of independent control enabled that conduct. Those are distinct propositions. D01, paras 10-15, 28-29; D14, Part 4.

## C. Precise divergence between the corporate and personal routes

| Issue | Meridian: corporate manslaughter | Ferris: gross negligence manslaughter |
|---|---|---|
| Duty | Meridian must owe a relevant duty of care to Tomasz, including one connected with construction operations. | Ferris must personally owe Tomasz a duty of care. His title alone is not enough; the Crown must prove sufficient assumption of responsibility, control, or personal proximity to the safety decision. |
| Breach | The company’s management or organisation must amount to a gross breach. A systemic failure can suffice. | Ferris’s own conduct or omission must be negligent and gross. A defective corporate system is evidentially relevant but cannot alone establish his personal offence. |
| Senior management | The Crown must prove senior management was a substantial element in the corporate breach. Ferris need not be the sole senior manager. | There is no “senior management” element. The question is whether Ferris himself committed the grossly negligent breach. |
| Causation | The company’s gross organisational breach must cause death. | Ferris’s own breach must cause death and be more than minimal. |
| Proof of knowledge | The Crown can prove an organisational failing without proving Ferris knew every site-level detail. | The personal case is materially strengthened if Ferris knew of, approved, encouraged, or failed to address the particular risk. |

The distinction is critical. The Corporate Manslaughter Act was designed to focus on the way activities are managed or organised, and the statutory language should be applied as such. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/19/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, ss 1 and 8 Law</a>

# 1.2 Mr Pyle: contradictory statements, adverse witness procedure, payments, and residual case

## A. The contradictions

Mr Pyle’s two accounts are irreconcilable on central matters.

In D04, he said:

- the scaffold was in good order;
- the ties were in;
- LB-7 was satisfactory;
- he recorded the inspection on the TowerLine tablet;
- he signed the paper handover;
- he did not tell Mr Aksoy or anyone else that ties were missing;
- he was at site all morning on 14 November 2023. D04, paras 3-10.

In D05, after caution and legal advice, he said:

- parts of D04 were untrue;
- Mr Braithwaite-Oyelaran told him to “Keep it simple” before the D04 statement;
- three through-ties and façade fixings were missing;
- he recorded the tablet defect as “3 NO TIES MISSING L6/L7 EAST ELEV — DO NOT LOAD BAY LB-7 UNTIL RECTIFIED”;
- he did not sign the paper handover;
- Mr Delacroix-Hale asked him to “tidy up the record” and said the ties could be addressed on Thursday;
- he was not at site that morning until around 11:00, having arranged for another person to use his card. D05, paras 1-10.

D13, Part 1 records that card TL-0119, attributed to Mr Pyle, was presented at 06:48 on 14 November. But biometric verification was disabled, anti-passback was not enabled, the system did not photograph users, and tailgating was undetected. D13, Part 1. Therefore, D13 does not conclusively establish Mr Pyle’s physical presence at 06:48. It is consistent with his D05 admission that another person used his card. It does, however, substantially damage him because he admits committing a serious workplace-record manipulation and because it gives the defence a concrete basis to say that he is untrustworthy on records and timing.

The Crown must not describe D05 as “corroborated” by access data. It is only **not disproved** by it. The card record supports the fact of a presentation, not the identity of the presenter. D05, para 9; D13, Part 1.

## B. Can the Crown properly call Pyle?

Yes, but only with a carefully defined forensic purpose and candid disclosure.

A witness is not disqualified because he has made inconsistent accounts, may have committed offences, or is vulnerable to impeachment. Mr Pyle is a central witness on the alleged missing ties, the tablet record, the purported paper handover and the alleged call from Mr Delacroix-Hale. D05, paras 3-8.

The Crown should call him if, after a pre-trial proof, he confirms that he will give the D05 account and accepts that D04 was false. The Crown must disclose:

- both statements in full;
- the fact that D05 was given after a caution concerning perverting the course of justice and perjury;
- the role of Mr Braithwaite-Oyelaran in D04;
- all payments and any other benefits;
- his unpaid TowerLine wages claim;
- the access-control evidence;
- all material concerning the TowerLine tablet’s provenance and extraction. D04, heading and para 13; D05, heading and paras 1-2, 9, 13-15; D08, Part 2; D13, Part 1.

The Crown should avoid presenting Pyle as a witness of unimpaired credibility. The proper formulation is that he is a compromised witness whose second account is capable of being supported by independent evidence.

## C. Section 3 Criminal Procedure Act 1865 and section 119 CJA 2003

If the Crown calls Pyle and he supports D05, it cannot simply impeach him because it dislikes part of his evidence. However, if he proves adverse, the court may permit the calling party to prove a previous inconsistent statement after the witness has been confronted with the circumstances of the occasion and asked whether he made it. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1865/18/section/3" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Procedure Act 1865, s 3 Law</a>

Where a witness gives oral evidence and admits making a previous inconsistent statement, or it is proved under section 3 of the 1865 Act, section 119 CJA 2003 makes that previous inconsistent statement admissible as evidence of the truth of matters stated in it, provided oral evidence of those matters would itself be admissible. It is not limited to credit. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/119" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 119 Law</a>

Accordingly:

- If Pyle gives D05 evidence and admits D04, D04 may be proved as a prior inconsistent statement under section 119.
- If he retreats from D05, denies it, or adopts elements of D04, the Crown can seek leave under section 3 to confront him with D05 and prove it; section 119 may then render the previous account substantive evidence.
- The jury must receive a clear direction that admissibility does not equal reliability and that it must assess each account against the independent evidence.

This is not an invitation to use both accounts selectively as if both are true. The Crown must frame the case in alternatives: the first statement is evidence of a prior inconsistent account; the later statement is evidence that the first was false; and neither should be accepted unless supported by other material.

## D. Section 120 CJA 2003

Section 120 is not the primary route for either D04 or D05.

Section 120 can make certain previous statements substantive evidence where, for example, a statement is admitted to rebut a suggestion of fabrication, where the witness uses it to refresh memory and it is received in evidence, or under the particular conditions in section 120(4)-(7). <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/120" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 120 Law</a>

Its likely relevance is narrow:

- If the defence alleges that D05 was a recent fabrication induced by Coldbrook’s investigators or by the prospect of assistance, an earlier consistent account could potentially be relevant to rebut fabrication if the statutory conditions are met.
- On the present material, D04 is not a previous consistent statement supporting D05; it is the opposite.
- Pyle’s alleged tablet record is not simply a prior witness statement. Its admissibility and probative value arise chiefly from its digital provenance and the evidence of the forensic extraction, not section 120.

The Crown should rely primarily on section 119 and the 1865 Act procedure, not overstate section 120.

## E. Payments and the TowerLine manager’s presence

The payments are a powerful credibility issue.

Mr Pyle says he was paid £2,500 in January and £2,000 in February 2024 by investigators instructed by solicitors acting for Coldbrook, described as consultancy fees, and that he initially failed to mention them. D05, paras 13-14. The HSE records that it learned in May 2024 that he had received £4,500; neither of his statements disclosed it. D01, para 25. He also has an unpaid wage and holiday-pay claim against TowerLine of £6,340. D05, para 15.

The Crown must not suggest that the payments prove he lied or that Coldbrook bought evidence. There is no evidence in the material that the investigators told him what to say. D05, para 13. But the payments, timing, nondisclosure, financial interests, and change in account are plainly material to bias and credibility.

Conversely, D04 was taken while TowerLine’s contracts manager, Mr Braithwaite-Oyelaran, sat beside him throughout. D04, heading and para 13. Pyle says that, before the inspector arrived, that manager said the company was finished if matters “goes the wrong way” and instructed him to “Keep it simple.” D05, paras 1-2. The HSE inspector’s note records that Pyle was distressed and twice declined the opportunity for Mr Braithwaite-Oyelaran to leave. D04, p 2 note.

That does not prove D04 was coerced. Pyle repeatedly declined to exclude the manager. But it gives a rational evidential explanation for why the earlier statement might be false, while the payments give a rational basis to attack the later account. The jury should receive both features neutrally.

## F. What survives if Pyle is not called?

The Crown’s case remains viable, though materially weakened on prior notice of missing ties and the alleged request to alter records.

The following evidence survives independently:

1. **Physical and expert evidence of absent ties.** The HSE survey found three specified anchor positions with no anchor and no drilled hole. D01, para 10. Professor Danecourt agrees that the three through-ties were absent and says that is established by the HSE survey. D20.1, para 1.

2. **The tablet extraction.** Version B records missing ties, LB-7 as “DEFECT_DO_NOT_USE,” no handover, and a locally committed record made at 16:52 on 13 November. It records no edits after commitment. D08, Part 2. The defence may challenge the device’s custody, but this is independent of Pyle’s oral evidence.

3. **The disputed paper record.** Version A has no original; the document examiner considers the supposed Pyle signature probably a reproduction of a prior signature, although not conclusively. D08, Part 1.

4. **The incomplete or questionable certificate evidence.** The certificate was created on 13 November, produced from a Meridian template, bears no digital signature or job number, has a blank TWC countersignature, and contains a 2,000 kg SWL inconsistent with the authenticated drawing’s 750 kg capacity. D09, Parts 1-3.

5. **The Ashgrove-Pike email.** It warned that the level-7 bay was tight, required the tie schedule to be signed off, and should not be used until a certificate existed. D09, Part 4.

6. **The contemporaneous Delacroix-Hale email.** He reported that scaffolders had flagged “something” at levels 6 and 7 and that LB-7 would realistically load on Wednesday, not Tuesday. D10, item 10.4.

7. **The messages and lift data.** The group messages and machine-generated crane record provide independent evidence of the plan to load, the suspension, the resumption, and the ongoing lift at collapse. D11, section 1; D14, Part 2.

Thus, without Pyle the case no longer depends on proving that he personally observed and reported the missing ties. The Crown can put the absence of ties, deficient documentation, absence of independent verification, and unsafe lifting decision through physical evidence, records, experts and other witnesses.

# 1.3 Suspension and resumption of lifting

## A. What can safely be established

The strongest conclusion is:

1. lifting was suspended at about 10:40;
2. lifting resumed no later than 11:13:26;
3. the resumed lift remained engaged when the collapse occurred at 11:22:04;
4. there is no compliant recorded authority for recommencement;
5. the evidence permits, but does not yet conclusively establish, that Mr Delacroix-Hale authorised or directed the resumption.

### Suspension

Ms Kettleborough says she instructed Mr Delacroix-Hale verbally in the site cabin at 10:40 that lifting was to be suspended and personally entered this in the daily log. D06, paras 9-10. The log records at 10:40: “Lifting ops SUSPENDED - high wind. Instruction of FK.” D14, Part 1. The group message at 10:41 says: “Lifting suspended, wind. Confirmed to Ruaridh in the cabin”; Mr Delacroix-Hale replied “Noted” at 10:44. D11, section 1.

The crane record independently shows no hook load from 10:39:47 to 11:13:26, a 33-minute and 39-second interval. D14, Part 2. This strongly supports the suspension.

### Resumption

Mr Aksoy recalls a stoppage and later seeing the crane again slewing with panels, though he cannot identify the person who restarted it. D03, paras 10-11. Ms Kettleborough says she did not authorise recommencement, was not asked to do so, and at 11:19 messaged “Are we still lifting?? Gusts are well over.” D06, paras 11-13.

The group evidence records:

- 11:06: “crane’s slewing again?”;
- 11:07: Mr Delacroix-Hale: “Last one then we’re off”;
- 11:19: Ms Kettleborough: “Are we still lifting?? Gusts are well over.” D11, section 1.

The crane’s automated logger establishes Lift L-12 engaged at 11:13:26 and not released before the shock-load event and anti-two-block alarm at 11:22:04. D14, Part 2.

The daily log contains an entry, struck through but still legible, at 11:05: “Lifting ops resumed following reassessment.” It is not initialled or dated, and it contains no stated authorisation. D14, Part 1. The mismatch between 11:05 and the machine-recorded 11:13:26 may reflect a rough or pre-emptive entry, later alteration, inaccurate manual timing, or an attempted reconstruction. It cannot be silently resolved in the Crown’s favour.

## B. Who authorised recommencement?

The lifting plan required lifting not to restart after suspension unless the Appointed Person gave express authority and it was recorded in the daily log. It identifies Mr Delacroix-Hale as Appointed Person. D12, Part 3.

The evidence pointing to him is:

- he was Appointed Person, D12, Part 3;
- he was told of the suspension and responded “Noted,” D11, section 1;
- he sent “Last one then we’re off” at 11:07, D11, section 1;
- the permit and safety-control structure placed him in the relevant roles, D14, Part 4;
- the log contains no compliant record of a different person’s authority. D14, Part 1.

That evidence is capable of supporting an inference that he authorised or at least directed resumption. But it is not conclusive. The crane driver reportedly said he was told over the radio to carry on but could not identify the speaker and merely assumed it was the Appointed Person. D14, Part 2. Mr Aksoy thought a radio voice was probably Mr Delacroix-Hale but could not be certain. D03, para 12.

The CPS case should therefore be framed in tiers:

- **Proved:** lifting resumed in breach of the recording requirement.
- **Strong inference:** it did so with Mr Delacroix-Hale’s authority or direction.
- **Not presently proved:** the precise person who gave the radio instruction.

## C. The site anemometer log

The manual site log purports to show readings of 18 mph at 11:00 and 21 mph at 11:15, each marked “Proceed” or “Proceed - below 23 trigger.” D12, Part 2. It is vulnerable because:

- the instrument’s make, serial number and calibration are absent;
- the instrument was not recovered;
- all readings were recorded at ground level despite the form requiring working-level readings where practicable;
- the 11:30 entry post-dates the collapse;
- the whole day’s entries are in one ink and hand;
- the log fails to record the documented suspension;
- the single comment at 11:15 specifically refers to the 23 mph trigger. D12, Part 2.

The Met Office data are not a direct reading at the site, but it recorded a 43 mph maximum gust at 11:20, while the CPS meteorologist estimated deck-level gusts at 33 to 47 mph, centrally 39 mph. D12, Part 1. Ms Kettleborough says she cannot reconcile the 43 mph record with the site readings and would not have suspended lifting based upon a reading of 18 mph. D06, paras 14-15.

## D. Least susceptible evidence

The **automatic crane data logger** is least susceptible to challenge on the critical questions of whether lifting stopped and restarted, and when. It is machine-generated, downloaded by the crane-hire company on 16 November, and expressly not dependent on human entry. D14, Part 2.

It does not identify the person who gave authority. But it powerfully corroborates:

- the 10:40 suspension;
- the later resumption;
- Mr Aksoy’s account that the crane began moving again;
- the fact that the daily log’s struck-through resumption entry relates to a real resumption, notwithstanding its defective form and inaccurate time. D03, paras 10-11; D14, Parts 1-2.

# 1.4 Causation: the Vantrease and Danecourt analyses

## A. The competing opinions

Dr Vantrease’s opinion is that:

- the primary cause was the absence of three through-ties;
- the secondary cause was loading at approximately 1,410 kg against a 750 kg SWL;
- wind was not a cause;
- a compliant bay would have survived a 43 mph gust with a margin of safety over 2.0;
- absent ties and compliant loading would probably have led to collapse within days;
- the temporary-works system fell far below that expected of a competent principal contractor. D19, paras 1.1-1.6.

Professor Danecourt agrees that three through-ties were absent and that the temporary-works role concentration was indefensible. D20.1, paras 1, 7. However, she disputes the static analysis and considers the dynamic effects of landing a 1,218 kg load with 4.32 square metres of sail area in gusting wind to be critical. D20.1, paras 2-5.

Her conclusion is that the collapse resulted from the “coincidence” of missing ties, overload and wind, that none was individually sufficient, and that removal of any one would probably have led to survival that morning. D20.1, para 6.

## B. Does Danecourt’s view assist or defeat causation?

It assists the Crown, provided the case is properly put.

The Crown does not need to prove that missing ties were the sole cause. It must prove that the relevant gross breach caused death and, in the individual case, that the breach made a more than minimal contribution. [*R v Wood Treatment Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim 618 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2021/618)

Professor Danecourt’s “remove any one” analysis is expressly counterfactual and causative. On her opinion:

- remove the missing ties: the bay probably survives that morning;
- remove the overload: it probably survives that morning;
- remove the wind: it probably survives that morning. D20.1, para 6.

That does not absolve the company or Mr Ferris merely because other wrongs contributed. It is capable of proving that the missing ties and defective temporary-works system were necessary or substantial operative contributors to the death.

The defence will submit that the real cause was wind or the decision to resume lifting, which may have been the act of Mr Delacroix-Hale rather than senior management or Mr Ferris. Professor Danecourt’s account does create that risk. But it does not identify an innocent or disconnected mechanism. It identifies three interacting conditions, each linked on the current evidence to alleged safety failures:

- missing ties, D01, para 10; D20.1, para 1;
- loading above the 750 kg controlled drawing capacity, D01, para 11; D09, Part 5;
- resumption of lifting in potentially excessive winds without recorded authority, D11, section 1; D12, Parts 1-3; D14, Parts 1-2.

Thus, unlike an alternative explanation which excludes negligence, Danecourt’s alternative attributes causal significance to multiple alleged breaches.

## C. The real causation risk

The true vulnerability is not the existence of multiple causes. It is whether the Crown can connect each cause to the **charged defendant’s** gross breach.

The Court of Appeal has warned that serious health-and-safety failures which heighten risk do not, without a forensic link, establish that they caused the specific death. The prosecution must exclude realistic possibilities consistent with innocence, rather than convert an increased risk into proof of causation. [*R v Wood Treatment Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim 618 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2021/618)

Accordingly:

- For **Meridian**, the Crown can say the same defective organisational structure permitted missing ties, improper certification, defective permit controls and lifting resumption. The causal linkage is potentially coherent.
- For **Ferris**, the Crown needs a tighter evidential bridge from his own approval, safety governance failure, communications or deliberate pressure to one or more operative causes.
- For the **section 37** charge, the Crown need only prove the underlying company offence and Ferris’s consent, connivance or neglect in relation to it. That remains easier than proving his personal gross-negligence manslaughter.

## D. Expert admissibility and weight

Dr Vantrease has significant credibility and admissibility vulnerabilities:

- he was instructed by Coldbrook’s solicitors and later adopted by the CPS, D19, heading;
- he relied on supplied materials and had not been provided with the TowerLine tablet extraction, D19, para 2.1;
- he assumed the controlled Revision C drawing was on site, while that is disputed, D19, para 2.3;
- he did not personally verify component positions, D19, para 2.4;
- he assumed the disputed covert recording was accurate for an opinion in his full report, D19, para 2.5;
- his company had a framework agreement with Coldbrook’s parent producing about 61 percent of its turnover, D19, Appendix G, paras G1-G3;
- his engagement contained a 25 percent success uplift, contrary to his original declaration that payment was not outcome-dependent, D19, Appendix G, para G4;
- he gave an opinion that Ferris was “grossly negligent,” which Professor Danecourt correctly identifies as outside structural-engineering expertise. D19, para 1.7; D20.1, para 8.

The Crown should not rely on Dr Vantrease’s conclusion on the ultimate question of gross negligence. It should rely on his structural analysis only, subject to an explicit acknowledgement of the limitations and an independent review of all source material.

Professor Danecourt’s evidence is not a defence-only exculpatory opinion. It concedes absent ties, an indefensible role concentration, and the impossibility of a competent 2,000 kg certification. D20.1, paras 1, 7. Her opinion may therefore be used by the Crown, fairly and without distortion, to prove causation through combined safety failures.

# 1.5 Silence, deletions, reset, and further enquiries before charge

## A. Section 34 CJPOA 1994

Section 34 permits proper inferences where an accused, when cautioned and questioned before charge, fails to mention a fact later relied on in defence which he could reasonably have been expected to mention at the time. It permits the court or jury to draw such inferences as appear proper. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/33/section/34" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994, s 34 Law</a>

Mr Ferris gave a prepared statement and then answered some questions but declined others on legal advice. D07, Part A and Part B. The Crown should distinguish four categories.

### 1. Facts actually advanced in interview

No section 34 inference arises where he did answer.

He asserted, among other things:

- he had no operational role;
- he was not on site in November;
- any visitor badge in his name was used by his driver;
- he was in Manchester at 09:58;
- he wiped his phone as part of an upgrade;
- “whatever it takes” was commercial rhetoric;
- “find a way through it” meant lawful commercial and programme measures. D07, Parts A-B.

Those accounts can be tested directly against the evidence. They do not become adverse-inference material merely because they are arguably untrue.

### 2. Silence concerning certificate knowledge and the SWL discrepancy

Mr Ferris made no comment when asked whether he knew before 14 November that the LB-7 certificate did not exist and whether he knew the stated two-tonne capacity did not match the drawing. D07, Part B.

If, at trial, he advances a positive factual explanation that he did not know, learned later, relied on another person, or had a particular basis for belief, section 34 may be engaged because these were matters he could reasonably have been expected to mention when specifically asked under caution.

However, the court must consider the solicitor’s note. It says the defence had not received the forensic extraction report, the access-control data or the photograph metadata despite a request. D07, p 3 solicitor’s note. The missing material was relevant to some questions, especially presence on site and digital attribution, but it was not necessarily relevant to every question about certificate knowledge. The Crown should not seek a blanket adverse inference from all silence.

### 3. Silence concerning deleted material and device reset

He gave a limited answer to the reset allegation, then made no comment when confronted with the claimed reset time and warrant timing. D07, Part B. If he later advances a detailed innocent explanation for the timing, section 34 may be available, subject to the same fairness assessment.

### 4. Board minute and dismissal

He said the redundancy was decided on 2 November and referred to a board decision, but made no comment when challenged with the supposed reference to an incident which occurred later and with his purported role as chair. D07, Part B. This may become relevant to consciousness of wrongdoing or motive, but it is collateral to the homicide charge and should not be allowed to distract from the central safety evidence unless carefully controlled.

## B. Deletions and factory reset

The forensic material is potentially powerful circumstantial evidence but cannot itself prove guilt.

The forensic examiner records:

- five messages from the Ferris account were deleted for everyone at 22:07 on 14 November, approximately 11 hours after the fatality;
- disappearing messages were enabled at 22:03;
- cloud backup was disabled at 21:58;
- the Ferris iPhone was reset at 06:12 on 22 November, before a 07:40 warrant;
- the examiner records no finding as to why this happened but says the pattern was not consistent with routine housekeeping in his experience. D11, section 2.

That evidence can support an inference of consciousness of guilt if the Crown first proves reliable attribution and excludes realistic innocent explanations. It is especially relevant because the preserved messages include the alleged pressure to certify, subsequent statements that “paper is in order,” and the plan to load LB-7. D11, section 1.

But the examiner expressly limits attribution: device attribution was based on account registration, contact conventions and self-description; he does not opine on who physically composed each message. D11, para 4.1. That limitation must be made explicit.

The absence of footage from the three cameras covering the east elevation is also suspicious but not determinative. Meridian says those cameras were not recording due to a fault reported on 30 October. Yet its own maintenance ticket says the relevant fault was closed as resolved on 3 November, and the unproduced cameras are precisely those covering the collapse. D13, Part 3. This is evidence capable of supporting an adverse factual inference at trial, but it requires technical investigation before it can safely be treated as deliberate destruction or concealment.

## C. Further enquiries required before a charging decision

### 1. Recover the primary digital evidence

The CPS should direct:

- recovery and forensic examination of all available NVR-2 hardware, storage media, configuration logs, replacement records, and maintenance communications concerning cameras 3, 4 and 7;
- evidence from the engineer or contractor who opened and closed ticket MTL-3348;
- analysis of whether footage was overwritten after the section 20 preservation notice;
- recovery of gateway, cloud, backup, handset-management and mobile-network records concerning Mr Ferris’s devices and accounts;
- full extraction and audit trail for the messaging group, including account-session and linked-device data;
- a forensic examination of deletion functionality and the stated time window, because the report’s description of “delete for everyone” should be technically reconciled with the age of the deleted messages. D11, section 2; D13, Part 3.

### 2. Identify the person who ordered the resumed lift

The Crown should seek:

- further evidence from the crane driver, including a formal video-link request if feasible;
- radio-system records, handset allocations, recordings if any, and voice-comparison evidence;
- evidence from all site leads and the two unidentified agency workers;
- fuller recovery of Northgate assignment records and payroll, transport, tax and agency communications to identify NSS-4402 and NSS-4419. D01, para 9; D02, para 5.6; D14, Part 2.

### 3. Establish whether Ferris was present and what he controlled

The visitor badge is not proof of physical presence. The Crown should obtain:

- Ferris’s complete diary, expense, hotel, vehicle, fuel, toll, mobile-cell and location records;
- witness evidence from Mr Olusanya-Reid, using tracing enquiries;
- native image-file provenance for IMG_4471, upload logs, device records and any cloud or shared-drive history;
- evidence concerning the driver’s ordinary use of the visitor badge on the eleven recorded occasions. D07, Part B; D13, Parts 1-2.

### 4. Clarify the technical causation case

The CPS should commission an independent joint or court-facing structural review addressing:

- the actual controlled drawing available at site on 14 November;
- the effect of the 750 kg versus 1,000 kg position;
- whether a compliant bay would have survived the actual dynamic wind/load conditions;
- whether ties, load, and wind are each independently causative;
- the precise effect of the crane manufacturer’s 20 mph limit;
- the relationship between the load on the crane logger, the HSE 1,410 kg calculation, and the claimed two-panel operation. D01, paras 11-12, 34; D12, Part 3; D14, Part 2; D19, paras 1.1-1.5, 2.3; D20.1, paras 3-6.

### 5. Resolve Pyle’s position before trial

The CPS should obtain a fresh, independently conducted proof from Pyle, with no Coldbrook investigator or TowerLine representative present, covering:

- each inconsistency between D04 and D05;
- his relationship with Coldbrook and the investigators;
- the payment schedule, invoices, tax treatment, instructions and communications;
- his unpaid wage claim;
- the proxy-card allegation and the identity of the person who used his card;
- the provenance of the tablet and whether he personally accessed it after 13 November. D05, paras 9, 13-15; D08, Part 2; D13, Part 1.

### 6. Privilege and post-incident material

The Crown should not rely on D10 item 10.6 or D20.3 unless and until the privilege dispute is judicially resolved. Both documents are expressly asserted to be privileged and inadvertently disclosed. D10, item 10.6 and administrative note; D20, pp 1, 5.

The Crown must avoid using the alleged legal advice as proof of guilt unless the court rules that privilege does not attach, was waived, or is displaced by the iniquity exception. It can continue to pursue the same underlying facts through unprivileged digital, documentary and witness evidence.

# Charging recommendation

## Meridian Structures Ltd

**Recommendation:** subject to the further enquiries, authorise a charge of corporate manslaughter.

The evidence is capable of establishing:

- a relevant duty connected with Meridian’s construction operations;
- death following failure of LB-7;
- absent ties, questionable certification, unsafe loading, and lifting in adverse conditions;
- a grossly deficient safety-management system;
- senior-management involvement through Ferris’s divisional and safety role, and potentially through Mr Delacroix-Hale’s substantial management of the site;
- a causal connection between the organisational failure and death. D01, paras 3, 6-15, 28-29; D06, paras 2-4; D10, items 10.4-10.5; D11, section 1; D12, Part 3; D14, Parts 1-4; D19, paras 1.1-1.6; D20.1, paras 1-7.

The greatest charging risk is causation if the Crown cannot present a coherent and independent expert account after addressing Dr Vantrease’s conflicts and Professor Danecourt’s dynamic analysis. The “three conditions” formulation is not fatal; it is capable of proving joint causation. But the Crown must prove that the conditions were caused or materially enabled by Meridian’s gross organisational breach, not merely that a dangerous event occurred. [*R v Wood Treatment Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim 618 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2021/618)

## Mr Ferris: gross negligence manslaughter

**Recommendation:** do not yet charge until the presence, knowledge, approval and causation enquiries are completed.

There is a realistic evidential case that Mr Ferris exercised safety-relevant control and may have applied pressure to proceed despite a known safety concern. D06, paras 2-4, 17, 25; D10, items 10.4-10.8; D11, section 1; D12, Part 3; D13, Part 2; D07, Part B.

However, personal gross-negligence manslaughter requires proof beyond reasonable doubt that **his own** breach caused death and was gross. The unresolved questions about physical presence, precise knowledge of the defect, the meaning of his messages, and the identity of the person authorising resumption make that charge presently less secure than the corporate case. [*R v Misra* [2004] EWCA Crim 2375 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2004/2375); [*R v Wood Treatment Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim 618 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2021/618)

## Mr Ferris: section 37 HSWA

**Recommendation:** subject to proof of Meridian’s underlying HSWA offences, there is presently a stronger basis to charge Mr Ferris under section 37 than for personal manslaughter.

The evidence is capable of supporting consent, connivance, or neglect through his safety portfolio, failure to correct the incompatible-role structure, director-level approval of a lifting plan with the identified wind-limit discrepancy, and messages capable of showing pressure to certify and proceed. D06, paras 2-4; D10, items 10.4-10.5; D11, section 1; D12, Part 3. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/37/section/37" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, s 37 Law</a>

The CPS should therefore treat the section 37 charge as the principal personal criminal route at the charging stage, with gross negligence manslaughter retained for reassessment after the directed evidential work is complete.
