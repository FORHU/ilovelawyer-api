# Memorandum: admissibility, privilege, disclosure, professional conduct and the protected-disclosure claim

## Executive conclusions

1. **D20.3 and D10 item 10.6:** each is, at the outset, a confidential lawyer-client communication made for legal advice and would ordinarily attract legal advice privilege. But the stronger conclusion is that the **iniquity exception probably prevents privilege attaching** if the court finds a sufficiently cogent prima facie basis that Mr Ferris was seeking assistance in deploying, concealing, or retrospectively “making good” a false certificate in response to a statutory investigation. It does not matter that Ms Trelawney-Baptiste refused to advise how to commit the wrongdoing: the relevant question is the client’s purpose in making the communication, not whether the solicitor joined the scheme. The issue arises principally in the **TCC**, but the ruling will materially affect the **Crown Court**, **Employment Tribunal**, and potentially the **coroner’s court**. The firm should cease acting if it cannot do so without being used to facilitate misleading conduct, preserve the material, obtain independent advice, and make a careful report to its COLP. It must not disclose privileged material voluntarily unless privilege is held not to apply, waived, or disclosure is otherwise legally required.

2. **D20.2, Mr Aksoy’s covert recording:** covert private recording is not automatically inadmissible. In the **Crown Court**, the relevant statutory discretion is section 78 of PACE; it is unlikely to justify wholesale exclusion merely because Mr Aksoy secretly recorded a meeting to which he was a party. In the **TCC**, CPR 32.1(2) permits tailored control of the evidence. The **Employment Tribunal** likewise has a broad procedural discretion and should focus on relevance, fairness, reliability and proportionality. The “without prejudice” objection is weak on the present record: the meeting appears to have concerned sick pay and liability, not a genuine settlement negotiation. The disputed 02:40 passage should not be admitted for its exact verbal content unless the original audio can be reliably interpreted; however, that does not require excluding the independent passages at 01:08 to 01:29 and 03:04.

3. **D13 Part 4, welfare-cabin surveillance:** the surveillance appears highly vulnerable as unlawful and disproportionate processing. The absence of signage, a privacy notice, a DPIA, a documented assessment of necessity, and the express aim of identifying those who spoke to HSE are serious features. The asserted “asset protection” purpose is undermined by the timing and wording of Mr Ferris’s instruction. Nonetheless, unlawfulness under data-protection law does **not automatically make the material inadmissible** in the Employment Tribunal. The tribunal should instead make a specific fairness and proportionality ruling. Installation of the system eleven days after Ms Raghunathan’s disclosure is capable of being a separate **section 47B detriment**, independently of whether its recording is admitted.

4. **Hearsay:** the deceased’s SMS is admissible in the **Crown Court** through the unavailable-witness route because its maker is dead, but its contents contain a further hearsay layer concerning what “Gareth” said. That second layer requires separate justification under section 121 of the Criminal Justice Act 2003. The crane driver’s statement may be admissible if his absence abroad and reasonable efforts to secure attendance are proved. Mr Ashgrove-Pike’s unsworn assertions through solicitors are much weaker and should not be admitted for their truth without a proper hearsay gateway and a fairness assessment. The Met Office and Halbrook business records are potentially admissible business records, although their authorship, provenance and limitations remain matters of weight.

5. **Dr Vantrease:** his report should not be excluded wholesale merely because of his financial interests and the success-uplift clause. But it requires serious qualifications. The 25 percent outcome-related uplift is inconsistent with his declaration that his remuneration was not dependent on outcome. The 61 percent turnover relationship with the claimant’s parent is a substantial apparent-independence issue. Paragraph 1.7 should be excised: whether conduct is “grossly negligent” is for the tribunal of fact, not a structural engineer. His reliance on a contested transcription at paragraph 2.5 must be identified and either removed, made conditional, or deferred pending an admissibility/reliability ruling on the recording. In the **TCC**, the report was served before CPR 35.4 permission and should be regularised retrospectively only on strict terms. In the **Crown Court**, the court should admit only properly confined expert evidence and give an appropriate direction on the limits of expertise.

6. **Ms Raghunathan:** she has a strong Employment Tribunal case that the 16 November email was a qualifying and protected disclosure and that her dismissal on 8 December was automatically unfair under section 103A. She does not require two years’ service for that claim. The board minute is not merely weak corroboration of a redundancy explanation: its four identified defects are capable of supporting a finding that it was retrospectively created or unreliable. Clause 9 was void insofar as it purported to bar protected disclosures. Its proposal, together with the dismissal letter’s regulator-facing confidentiality warning and the surveillance instruction, is capable of amounting to a detriment. If section 103A succeeds, the ordinary statutory cap on the compensatory award does not apply.

# 3.1 D20.3 and D10 item 10.6: privilege, iniquity, mistaken disclosure and Halloway Brant’s position

## Forum map

| Issue | Crown Court | TCC | Employment Tribunal | Coroner’s court |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whether D20.3 and D10.6 are privileged | Relevant to prosecutorial use and disclosure, but ordinarily determined through criminal-case management and legal professional privilege principles | Central issue: CPR 31.20 application is listed in the TCC | Relevant if either document is sought or relied upon, but the tribunal will not simply disregard privilege | Relevant only if material is sought for the inquest; the coroner should not determine disputed privilege casually |
| Iniquity exception | Same substantive common-law principle | Same substantive common-law principle | Same substantive common-law principle | Same substantive common-law principle |
| Consequence of inadvertent disclosure | Criminal disclosure obligations and fairness management | CPR 31.20 is the direct procedural mechanism identified in the bundle | Case-management and fairness powers | Coroner must avoid misuse of privileged material absent a proper ruling |

The substantive privilege rule does not materially differ between the four fora. The **procedure and immediate remedy** differ.

## A. Does legal advice privilege initially attach?

### D10 item 10.6

Item 10.6 is a written communication from a solicitor at Halloway Brant to Mr Ferris, following their call, giving advice about the certificate, the response to the HSE notice, potential criminality and the solicitor’s professional position. On its face, that is a confidential solicitor-client communication for the purpose of legal advice. It therefore begins within legal advice privilege.

The fact that the email is labelled “PRIVILEGED & CONFIDENTIAL” is not determinative, but its subject matter, addressee and setting support the claim. The email records advice following a direct client communication and gives legal advice about possible fraud, perverting the course of justice, an HSE response and the solicitor’s continuing retainer. **D10 item 10.6.**

### D20.3

The attendance note records a telephone call between Mr Ferris and Ms Trelawney-Baptiste and the advice she says she gave. A solicitor’s contemporaneous record of a privileged lawyer-client call is ordinarily itself privileged to the same extent as the communication it records. The note was apparently prepared at 21:20 on 15 November 2023 after a 20:40 to 21:12 call. **D20.3.**

The analysis therefore starts with privilege, not with an assumption that the documents are admissible simply because they have reached the bundle.

## B. Does the iniquity exception displace privilege where the solicitor refused to participate?

### Governing principle

The bundle names *R v Cox and Railton*, *Barclays Bank plc v Eustice* and *JSC BTA Bank v Ablyazov*. The older authorities named in the bundle were not directly retrievable from the available judgment database. However, the High Court’s verified treatment of the iniquity principle in *JSC BTA Bank v Ablyazov* confirms the critical distinction: privilege ordinarily protects confidential lawyer-client communications, but communications made in furtherance of an iniquitous purpose lack the necessary quality of confidence. The principle is concerned with the **purpose for which the lawyer is consulted**, not simply with whether the lawyer gives improper advice. [*JSC BTA Bank v Ablyazov* [2014] EWHC (Comm) 2788 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/comm/2014/2788).

In particular, the court explained that confidential communications made in furtherance of an iniquitous purpose do not satisfy the necessary condition of confidentiality, whereas a solicitor who is properly instructed to defend a client against a criminal charge remains acting in the ordinary course of professional engagement. [*JSC BTA Bank v Ablyazov* [2014] EWHC (Comm) 2788 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/comm/2014/2788).

The line is therefore:

- **Protected:** seeking advice after an event about legal exposure, how to respond lawfully to an investigation, or how to defend an allegation.
- **Not protected under the exception:** using the legal relationship to facilitate, plan, conceal or advance fraud, false evidence, misleading regulatory disclosure, or interference with justice.

### Application to D20.3

The attendance note contains two analytically distinct parts.

#### 1. The purported iniquity

On the recorded account, Mr Ferris told the solicitor that:

- the LB-7 certificate did not exist and never had existed;
- Mr Delacroix-Hale produced it using a Halbrook template on Monday evening;
- Mr Ferris knew this by Tuesday afternoon;
- he asked whether the certificate could be retrospectively “made good” by getting a signature; and
- he asked whether it could be left out of the response to the section 20 notice. **D20.3.**

If the court accepts that account at the prima facie stage, it is not merely a client asking “is this lawful?” in the abstract. It is evidence capable of showing that he sought advice in connection with presenting or concealing a document said to be false in the course of a live statutory investigation.

That conclusion is reinforced by independent bundle material:

- the certificate file was created on 13 November 2023, bears the author account “r.delacroix-hale”, uses a Halbrook template, has no digital signature and was printed on 16 November; **D09 Parts 1 and 2;**
- Halbrook’s register contains no LB-7 job, invoice or certificate; **D09 Part 3;**
- the purported certificate’s 2,000 kg safe working load is contradicted by the authenticated current drawing’s 750 kg figure and by both structural experts’ criticism of the 2,000 kg figure; **D01 paras 11, 34; D09 Part 5; D19 paras 1.2 to 1.5; D20.1 paras 1 to 7;**
- the email to Ms Raghunathan asserts that all communications should go through Halloway Brant and tells her not to contact HSE or anyone else; **D10 item 10.8;**
- the certificate was supplied to HSE without the alleged circumstances of creation being disclosed. **D10 item 10.6; D20.3.**

The TCC should therefore find a sufficiently strong prima facie basis for the exception **if** the court considers that the purpose of the consultation was to advance or conceal the alleged false certification, rather than simply obtain lawful advice after the event.

#### 2. The refusal by the solicitor

Meridian’s best point is that the solicitor did not facilitate the alleged wrongdoing. On the account in D20.3, she positively rejected both courses, warned of potential criminal offences, said she could not advise how they might be carried out, and contemplated whether she could continue to act. **D20.3.**

That matters, but it is not decisive. The relevant question is not whether the solicitor became a conspirator or gave enabling advice. A client cannot obtain privilege for communications made to use the lawyer as a protected channel for furtherance of wrongdoing merely because the lawyer refuses the request. The refusal is powerful evidence of the solicitor’s proper conduct; it does not necessarily restore privilege to a communication made for the alleged iniquitous purpose.

The court must nevertheless avoid circularity. It cannot assume the truth of D20.3 simply because D20.3 says it is true and then use that assumption to defeat privilege. The exception should be assessed against the independent evidential matrix set out above: the metadata, Halbrook register, late certificate, alleged absence of a valid check, internal communications, and the unexplained handling of the certificate. **D09; D10 items 10.1 to 10.8; D11; D14 Part 4; D20.3.**

### Outcome

**TCC:** the better conclusion is that the court should dismiss Meridian’s CPR 31.20 application, or at least order a focused preliminary hearing to determine whether the iniquity exception applies using the contested documents only alongside independent evidence. On the present material, Coldbrook and the CPS have a strong argument that the exception applies.

**Crown Court:** the prosecution should not deploy D20.3 or D10.6 as evidence until privilege has been ruled upon. If the exception is upheld, the Crown can consider admissibility, disclosure and fairness separately. If it is not upheld, the material should be quarantined and excluded from the prosecution team’s consideration to the extent possible.

**Employment Tribunal:** the tribunal should not treat the material as available merely because it appears in a shared bundle. It should await, respect or make an appropriately confined privilege determination. It may, however, rely upon non-privileged evidence of the timing, certificate metadata, disclosed documents and conduct.

**Coroner’s court:** the coroner should not decide this complex privilege issue by an informal evidential ruling. The appropriate course is to await the TCC/Crown Court determination or direct focused submissions from the privilege holder and interested persons.

## C. Was disclosure an “obvious mistake” under CPR 31.20?

CPR 31.20 is expressly engaged in the TCC proceedings because Meridian’s application to restrain use is listed for 14 November 2025. **D20 p.1.**

The relevant practical question is whether a reasonable recipient, taking the circumstances as they appeared on receipt, should have appreciated that privileged material had been disclosed by mistake.

### Factors supporting Meridian

- D10 item 10.6 was expressly headed “PRIVILEGED & CONFIDENTIAL.” **D10 item 10.6.**
- It is a lawyer’s direct advice to Mr Ferris after a client call. **D10 item 10.6.**
- D20.3 is marked as asserted to be privileged, was identified as inadvertently disclosed, and a return was demanded nine days later. **D20 p.1; D20.3.**
- The note’s nature is unmistakably legal-advisory on its face. **D20.3.**

These points strongly support the proposition that, absent the iniquity issue, a recipient should have recognised the material as potentially privileged and stopped using it until the position was resolved.

### Factors against a simple “obvious mistake” finding

- Item 10.6 was included within late disclosure of 1,417 documents apparently produced by Meridian’s solicitors, and there is no supplied disclosure certificate or search protocol explaining the scope and checking process. **D01 para. 21; D10 item 10.9.**
- Meridian disclosed the email on 2 April 2024 but the bundle records only a later letter asserting privilege. **D10 disclosure note; D10 item 10.9.**
- The note and email are, on the bundle, substantially the same communication, raising a genuine issue about whether privilege has been waived or whether selective treatment is being attempted. **D10 item 10.6; D20.3.**
- D20.3 records that Meridian has not sought return of D10 item 10.6 from HSE, which sequestered rather than returned it. **D20.3.**
- The material was not simply commercially embarrassing legal advice; it potentially concerns a communication in furtherance of crime, fraud or regulatory deception. **D09; D10 item 10.6; D20.3.**

### Conclusion on CPR 31.20

There are **two sequential questions**, which should not be collapsed:

1. Was the material disclosed inadvertently and was that apparent?  
   **Probably yes**, as to the initial lawyer-client nature of the documents.

2. Was there, in law, privilege capable of being protected?  
   **Possibly no**, if the iniquity exception applies.

If privilege is displaced, CPR 31.20 cannot be used to restore it. If privilege survives, the obviousness of the error and the prompt assertion of privilege would strongly support injunctive protection, subject to the complicated issue of waiver through the paired communication.

## D. Can privilege survive in one document if it is lost in the other?

The answer depends on **why** it is lost.

### If the iniquity exception applies

If the court holds that the communications were made in furtherance of the same alleged iniquitous purpose, neither the attendance note nor the confirming email attracts privilege. Privilege cannot be preserved for one manifestation of the communication while denied for the other merely because one document is a note and the other an email. **D10 item 10.6; D20.3.**

### If privilege exists but there was inadvertent disclosure

If the court finds no iniquity exception, the position is more nuanced:

- D20.3 and D10.6 are separate documents;
- each may be privileged;
- accidental production of one may not automatically waive privilege in the other;
- but a party cannot selectively deploy the favourable part of a privileged transaction while suppressing the adverse part.

The court should determine whether Meridian’s conduct amounts to a waiver of privilege over the communications as a whole. The fact that the email and note were said to document the same call and advice is likely to support a broad “transaction” analysis. **D10 item 10.6; D20.3.**

Meridian’s failure to seek return of D10.6, while asserting privilege over D20.3, is particularly difficult. **D20.3.** It is not necessarily conclusive waiver, but it materially weakens a claim that the privilege was consistently and promptly maintained.

## E. What Halloway Brant should do now

### Immediate steps

1. **Stop substantive engagement with disputed evidence.**  
   The firm should erect a strict internal information barrier so that lawyers continuing to act do not use D20.3 or D10.6 pending judicial determination.

2. **Preserve every original.**  
   Preserve the original email, server metadata, attendance note, file opening records, time records, instructions, conflict checks, disclosure review logs, and the late-disclosure search records. No document should be amended, “tidied”, deleted, renamed, or re-created.

3. **Notify and consult the COLP.**  
   The note itself says Ms Trelawney-Baptiste intended to raise the position with the COLP. **D20.3.** That should now be done formally, with a written record.

4. **Obtain independent professional-regulatory advice.**  
   The firm should obtain independent advice because its own position is potentially in issue. It should not advise Meridian on whether the firm’s conduct, disclosure process, or knowledge is relevant to potential offences or regulatory reports.

5. **Consider ceasing to act.**  
   If the firm reasonably believes its client may persist in presenting false material, may use the firm to facilitate misleading disclosure, or may require the firm to advance a case inconsistent with what the firm knows, withdrawal is likely required. The firm must avoid prejudicing the client unfairly but cannot continue in a way that assists deception.

6. **Do not make a voluntary merits disclosure without lawful basis.**  
   Confidentiality and privilege remain fundamental unless the court holds the exception applies, the client waives privilege, disclosure is legally compelled, or a narrow professional exception is engaged. The firm should seek independent advice before making any report beyond internal compliance channels.

### Evidence and disclosure application

The TCC should order:

- a sealed, restricted-access procedure;
- evidence from the solicitors who conducted the disclosure review, confined to the mechanics of disclosure, the document review process and the assertion of privilege;
- a disclosure log identifying when D10.6 and D20.3 were found, reviewed, produced, challenged and sequestered;
- preservation and production of relevant metadata;
- a preliminary determination of iniquity and waiver;
- costs reserved or costs in the case, unless the court finds unreasonable disclosure conduct.

# 3.2 D20.2: Mr Aksoy’s covert recording

## Forum map

| Issue | Crown Court | TCC | Employment Tribunal | Coroner’s court |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Covert recording | PACE section 78 fairness discretion | CPR 32.1(2) case-management discretion | Tribunal fairness and case-management discretion | Strict evidence rules do not apply; weight and fairness are central |
| Article 8 | May inform fairness, but does not automatically exclude | May inform proportionality and fairness | May inform fairness, privacy and data-protection context | Relevant to fairness but not a conventional exclusionary rule |
| Without prejudice | Can exclude genuinely privileged settlement communications | Same | Same basic principle | Coroner should be cautious and focused on scope |
| 02:40 disputed words | Reliability and weight; possible exclusion/redaction of this segment | Same | Same | Same |

## A. The recording’s factual and reliability limits

Mr Aksoy recorded the 4 March 2024 meeting on his own telephone, concealed in his jacket pocket, because he said he wanted a record and was frightened of being blamed. **D03 paras 14 to 16.** The meeting involved Mr Aksoy, Mr Ferris and Ms Hargreave-Siddiqui and concerned Mr Aksoy’s sick pay position, although Meridian disputes the characterisation. **D03 para. 14; D20.2.**

The recording is not a clean, agreed transcript:

- the audio quality is poor;
- substantial background noise exists;
- Meridian’s examiner says much is at or below reliable intelligibility;
- Mr Aksoy disputes the transcript at 02:40;
- three constructions of the words are said to be tenable. **D03 para. 15; D20.2.**

That affects the admissibility and weight of the **particular disputed segment**, not necessarily the entire recording.

## B. Crown Court: PACE section 78

Section 78 permits the court to refuse prosecution evidence where admission, considering all circumstances including how it was obtained, would adversely affect trial fairness to such an extent that it ought not be admitted. It applies throughout the UK, including England and Wales. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1984/60/section/78" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984, s 78 Law</a>.

### Why section 78 is unlikely to exclude the recording wholesale

The principal features are:

- the recorder was a participant in the conversation;
- the recording was privately obtained, not procured by police misconduct;
- it was not obtained through an unlawful interview, oppressive state conduct, or a breach of a statutory suspect safeguard;
- it has potential probative value regarding Mr Ferris’s knowledge, the existence of information about Mr Pyle’s payments, and the effort at 03:04 to prevent further disclosure. **D20.2.**

A secret recording can still be unfair or unreliable, but covert acquisition alone is not enough. The Crown Court should not permit a jury to treat an unclear transcript as an admission. It should instead require the original audio, an agreed or competing transcript protocol, and potentially expert audio evidence.

### Appropriate Crown Court ruling

The court should:

1. admit the recording provisionally, subject to transcript and audibility rulings;
2. exclude or redact the disputed 02:40 wording as a purported verbal admission unless a judge can safely determine that the audio supports one version;
3. permit the jury to hear any intelligible portions directly, with a direction that the recording is evidence only of what they can reliably hear;
4. prevent any advocate from suggesting that the disputed words constitute a clear confession;
5. permit use of independent, intelligible passages if relevant and otherwise admissible.

## C. TCC: CPR 32.1(2)

In the TCC, the issue is not section 78 PACE but the court’s broad case-management control over evidence under CPR 32.1(2), as identified in the bundle. **D20.3 to D20.4.**

The TCC should approach the evidence through:

- relevance to the contractual, insurance, causation or credibility issues;
- whether the recording is authentic;
- whether the exact words can be reliably established;
- whether the method of acquisition unfairly affects the proceedings;
- proportionality;
- whether the evidence can be admitted for a limited purpose.

The TCC should admit it only in a carefully confined form. The strongest potentially admissible uses are:

- the exchange at 01:08 to 01:29, subject to proving who knew what and when;
- the 03:04 passage, if the audio bears out the words;
- the fact and context of the meeting;
- credibility and knowledge issues, rather than proof of every factual assertion made by either speaker.

The 02:40 passage should either be excluded, treated as ambiguous, or placed before the judge with all three versions clearly identified. It should not be treated as an unequivocal admission.

## D. Employment Tribunal

The Employment Tribunal need not import the criminal section 78 test. It should conduct its own fairness assessment. The relevant distinction is important:

- **Mr Aksoy’s private recording** concerns a meeting with Mr Ferris and HR; it may be relevant indirectly to the wider narrative.
- **Meridian’s covert welfare-cabin recording** is a separate issue and raises much more serious employer-surveillance concerns. **D13 Part 4.**

If D20.2 is deployed in the tribunal, the tribunal should admit only parts probative of the pleaded employment issues. The 03:04 passage could be relevant to whether Meridian was seeking to suppress reporting to regulators, which may bear on motive and detriment. The 01:08 passage could be relevant to knowledge and credibility. The 02:40 passage should be treated with special caution.

## E. Coroner’s court

The coroner expressly records the provisional view that strict rules of evidence do not apply and that the recording is likely admissible “for what it is worth,” while reserving fairness and excision issues. **D02 para. 5.4.**

That is correct in principle. The coroner should:

- admit the recording only with a clear warning about its limitations;
- avoid making a definitive finding from 02:40;
- distinguish between admitted evidence and reliable evidence;
- use intelligible passages only insofar as they assist the statutory inquiry into how the deceased came by his death;
- avoid allowing the inquest to become a trial of Mr Ferris’s criminal liability.

## F. Article 8

Article 8 considerations may be engaged by covert recording, but they do not create an automatic common-law rule excluding evidence acquired by a private participant. The strongest Article 8 and data-protection issues in this bundle concern Meridian’s systematic workplace surveillance, not Mr Aksoy’s one-off recording of a meeting to which he was personally party. **D13 Part 4; D20.2.**

The courts should account for privacy in the fairness balance. But there is a substantial difference between:

- a participant privately preserving a conversation involving his own statutory sick pay and potential liability claim; and
- an employer secretly installing audio-video equipment at work to identify persons communicating with a regulator. **D03 paras 14 to 16; D13 Part 4.**

## G. The without prejudice objection

The “without prejudice” objection should fail on the material presently available.

The hallmarks of a genuine without prejudice negotiation are not established:

- no “without prejudice” designation is recorded;
- the meeting begins as a discussion about sick pay; **D20.2 at 00:00:04 to 00:00:22;**
- Mr Aksoy was not Meridian’s employee; **D20.2 at 00:00:14;**
- there is no identified existing dispute settlement proposal, concession or negotiation;
- the record suggests a meeting about the legal position, not an attempt to compromise litigation. **D20.2.**

Meridian may establish further facts showing a genuine settlement context. On the current record, however, merely discussing legal exposure or an injured person’s entitlements does not make a conversation without prejudice.

Even if a without prejudice occasion were established, the 03:04 passage is capable of supporting an argument that the evidence falls within the alleged “unambiguous impropriety” exception, because it is said to involve an instruction not to tell a lawyer, inspector or anyone about the conversation. **D20.2 at 03:04 to 03:14.** That is a demanding exception and should not be stretched. But this is a materially stronger case for considering it than an ordinary dispute about what settlement was offered.

## H. Can other parts be admitted if 02:40 is excluded?

Yes.

There is no necessary evidential indivisibility. The court can:

- exclude 02:40 to 02:41.1;
- prevent the disputed transcript from being put to the fact-finder as a definitive account;
- retain audible passages before and after it;
- admit 01:08 to 01:29 for the limited issue of apparent knowledge of Pyle’s payments, subject to the temporal contradiction;
- admit 03:04 to 03:14, subject to audibility, for the limited issue of conduct towards future disclosure.

The court must not use an excluded 02:40 statement indirectly as the foundation for Dr Vantrease’s opinion or an allegation of admission. **D19 para. 2.5; D20.2.**

# 3.3 D13 Part 4: covert welfare-cabin monitoring

## Forum map

| Question | Crown Court | TCC | Employment Tribunal | Coroner’s court |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Data-protection compliance | May be relevant to criminal conduct, disclosure and fairness | May be relevant to admissibility and disclosure | Directly relevant to the detriment and evidential fairness issues | Peripheral unless it bears on the circumstances of death or scope |
| Admissibility | No automatic exclusion; possible fairness implications | Case-management and fairness analysis | Direct issue under tribunal procedural powers | Weight and fairness, with strict evidence rules not applying |
| Section 47B detriment | Not itself an Employment Rights Act claim in the criminal case | Not the primary forum | Central question | Not a determination for the coroner |

## A. The facts that matter

The internal memo was sent by Mr Ferris on 27 November 2023, eleven days after Ms Raghunathan’s formal 16 November disclosure and ten days after her call to HSE. **D10 item 10.7; D18 Part 18.1; D13 Part 4.**

The instruction was to install covert audio and video equipment in the welfare cabin and drying room, without signage and without inclusion in the site induction. The stated purpose was to identify who had been speaking to HSE and to Coldbrook. **D13 Part 4.**

The equipment was installed from 1 December 2023 to 14 March 2024. There was no signage, privacy notice, DPIA or Article 30 processing entry. Meridian later relied on “legitimate interests” and asset protection, despite the recovered theft having occurred and been resolved in September 2023. **D13 Part 4.**

## B. UK GDPR and DPA 2018

The bundle identifies the following provisions as directly engaged: UK GDPR Articles 5, 6(1)(f), 13 and 35, together with Data Protection Act 2018 provisions. **D13 Part 4.**

The available legislative source resolves the retained EU instrument as Regulation (EU) 2016/679, but did not provide a resolved official section URL for the relevant UK GDPR articles. The following analysis therefore proceeds from the articles identified in the bundle and the documented facts, rather than purporting to quote statutory wording not retrieved in full. Regulation (EU) 2016/679.

### Article 5: data-protection principles

The documented absence of transparency and the mismatch between stated and actual purpose are particularly serious.

The memo’s true stated objectives were:

- no signage;
- no induction disclosure;
- discovering who had communicated with HSE;
- discovering who had communicated with Coldbrook;
- direct reporting to Mr Ferris rather than site management. **D13 Part 4.**

Those facts are difficult to reconcile with fair, transparent and purpose-limited processing. The later “asset protection” explanation is weakened because the theft cited as the ostensible reason had been resolved months earlier, no further theft had been reported, and the instruction expressly focused on a regulatory “leak.” **D13 Part 4.**

### Article 6(1)(f): legitimate interests

Meridian must show more than a legitimate business interest in the abstract. On the facts, it would need to establish that covert audio-video surveillance of employees and workers in welfare spaces was necessary and proportionate for a legitimate purpose, and that the individuals’ interests and rights did not outweigh the asserted interest.

Its case is weak because:

- it used audio as well as video;
- the locations were a welfare cabin and drying room, where privacy expectations are higher than in open work areas;
- the surveillance was intentionally covert;
- it operated for more than three months;
- it had no documented DPIA;
- the contemporaneous purpose was to identify communications with HSE and Coldbrook, rather than investigate an unresolved theft. **D13 Part 4.**

A less intrusive measure was plainly available if genuine asset protection were the aim: visible cameras, notices, access controls, targeted investigation, or time-limited monitoring.

### Article 13: information obligations

The absence of signage, induction information or privacy notice strongly supports a finding that Meridian failed to provide the required information to data subjects. **D13 Part 4.**

The contention that the monitoring was covert does not itself answer the question. Any reliance on a crime-prevention justification would require a compelling and documented basis. The bundle’s facts undermine that basis.

### Article 35: DPIA

A prolonged covert audio-video system monitoring workers in workplace welfare facilities is inherently likely to trigger the need for a documented data-protection impact assessment. The bundle states that none was carried out. **D13 Part 4.**

The absence of a DPIA is not merely a technical omission here. It supports the conclusion that there was no recorded assessment of:

- necessity;
- proportionality;
- privacy impacts;
- less intrusive alternatives;
- risks to whistleblowers;
- duration;
- retention;
- access controls; or
- the special sensitivity of monitoring a welfare environment.

### DPA 2018

The DPA 2018 is the domestic statutory framework supplementing the UK GDPR. The Data Protection Act 2018 provision retrieved in the available source concerns law-enforcement processing and does not itself determine the general employer-processing issue. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/12/section/35" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Data Protection Act 2018, s 35 Law</a>. It follows that the detailed statutory analysis of the employment surveillance must remain anchored to the UK GDPR provisions identified in D13 and any full statutory text placed before the tribunal.

## C. Employment Tribunal admissibility

The tribunal must separate two questions:

1. **Was the surveillance lawful?**  
   On the present facts, Meridian faces a strong case that it was not.

2. **Should the 9 January 2024 recording nevertheless be admitted?**  
   Illegality does not automatically make evidence inadmissible. The tribunal must assess relevance, fairness, reliability, proportionality and whether admission would reward or worsen the alleged retaliatory conduct.

The content Meridian relies on is that a former colleague, not Ms Raghunathan, described her as “out for what she can get.” Ms Raghunathan was not audible. **D13 Part 4.**

The evidential value is limited:

- it is hearsay;
- it is opinion or characterisation rather than direct evidence of the protected disclosure or dismissal reason;
- the claimant is not the speaker;
- the surveillance is alleged to have been installed to identify persons speaking to HSE and Coldbrook;
- admitting it may distract from the real statutory question: the reason or principal reason for dismissal on 8 December 2023. **D13 Part 4; D18 Part 18.6.**

The appropriate Employment Tribunal order is likely:

- exclude the recording as unfair and of marginal relevance; or
- if admitted, confine it to a narrow credibility issue, give it very limited weight, and prohibit Meridian from using it as propensity evidence.

## D. Is installation itself a section 47B detriment?

Yes, it is capable of being a distinct detriment.

Section 47B protects a worker from detriment by an employer’s act or deliberate failure to act on the ground that the worker made a protected disclosure. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/47B" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 47B Law</a>.

The core evidential sequence is compelling:

- Ms Raghunathan’s email disclosure was sent on 16 November 2023. **D10 item 10.7; D18 Part 18.1.**
- She contacted HSE on 17 November 2023. **D01 para. 20; D18 Part 18.1.**
- Mr Ferris’s memo was sent on 27 November 2023. **D13 Part 4.**
- It referred expressly to “the leak to the inspector” and sought to discover who had spoken to HSE and Coldbrook. **D13 Part 4.**
- The equipment was installed on 1 December 2023. **D13 Part 4.**

A reasonable worker could view covert surveillance installed to identify regulatory reporters as a disadvantage or a threat to their position. The fact that Ms Raghunathan may not have known of the installation at the time does not necessarily eliminate detriment; the key issue is whether the act subjected her to a disadvantage or formed part of retaliatory treatment. At the least, it is powerful circumstantial evidence of Meridian’s attitude towards whistleblowing and relevant to the reason for dismissal.

The tribunal should determine:

- whether the monitoring targeted Ms Raghunathan specifically or whistleblowers generally;
- who authorised it;
- whether the 27 November instruction followed knowledge of her HSE call;
- what recordings were reviewed;
- whether any action was taken against her using the monitoring.

# 3.4 Hearsay

## Forum map

| Evidence | Crown Court | TCC | Employment Tribunal | Coroner’s court |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deceased’s SMS | Criminal Justice Act 2003 hearsay gateway and multiple-hearsay analysis | Civil hearsay may be admitted, but weight and notice issues arise | Tribunal can receive hearsay but must evaluate fairness and weight | Strict evidence rules do not apply; weight is central |
| Crane driver statement | Section 116 unavailable-witness analysis | Civil hearsay rules and weight | Same broad approach | Rule 23 issue expressly flagged |
| Ashgrove-Pike solicitors’ assertions | Weak hearsay; should not be used for truth without gateway | Weak civil hearsay absent witness evidence | Weak and likely low weight | Potentially read only with appropriate safeguards |
| Met Office and Halbrook records | Business-document route, subject to foundation and reliability | Documentary/business evidence | Same | Usually admissible, weight assessed |

## A. The deceased’s SMS

The SMS was received on Mrs Wieczorek’s handset at 09:41 on 14 November 2023. In certified translation, it communicated the deceased’s concern that management had told him to load the platform despite a reported warning from “Gareth”, and reassured his wife that he would be home at 6. **D11 section 3.**

### First hearsay layer: Tomasz’s statement

In the Crown Court, the SMS can satisfy section 116 because the maker is dead. Section 116 permits hearsay where oral evidence would have been admissible, the maker is identified to the court’s satisfaction, and a statutory unavailability condition is met, including death. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/116" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 116 Law</a>.

The maker is identified through:

- the number registered to the deceased;
- the recipient handset belonging to his widow;
- the timing;
- the continuous cell-site location within the sector serving the site from 06:52 to 11:22. **D11 section 3.**

There remains a factual caveat: the examiner cannot say who physically operated the deceased’s handset. **D11 section 3.** That caveat goes to weight, not necessarily admissibility.

### Second hearsay layer: “Gareth said”

The message contains a further assertion: that Gareth said not to load. This is multiple hearsay. Section 121 imposes an additional requirement before hearsay can prove that an earlier hearsay statement was made. It requires, among other possibilities, party agreement, an applicable statutory route such as a business-document route, or a finding that the value of the evidence, considering apparent reliability, is so high that the interests of justice require admission. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/121" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 121 Law</a>.

This is not a straightforward section 116 case because Mr Pyle is alive and has made conflicting statements. The Crown should not be permitted simply to use the deceased’s text to prove as a fact that Mr Pyle said not to load.

The correct distinction is:

- The SMS is strong evidence that Tomasz communicated concern to his wife at 09:41.
- It is weaker evidence that “Gareth” actually made the reported statement.
- It is still weaker evidence as to the identity of “boss,” since the Polish word “szef” can mean a particular person or management generally. **D11 section 3.**

### Crown Court conclusion

The court should admit the SMS for the limited purpose of showing the deceased’s contemporaneous state of mind and communication of concern, and potentially as evidence that he had been told not to load. It should be very cautious about treating it as proof that Mr Pyle made the statement or that a particular person directed the load.

The prosecution must seek a section 121 ruling if it relies on the “Gareth said” proposition for its truth.

### TCC, Employment Tribunal and coroner

In the civil and tribunal fora, hearsay is more readily receivable but remains vulnerable on weight. The coroner has already recognised the authenticity and multiple-hearsay objections and indicated that weight is a real question. **D02 para. 5.5.**

The coroner should use it as contextual evidence, not the sole foundation of a conclusion identifying the decision-maker. The TCC should similarly avoid treating it as proving a specific instruction without corroboration. The Employment Tribunal should not need the SMS to decide Ms Raghunathan’s claim, except perhaps as background.

## B. The crane driver’s statement

The crane driver said he was told over the radio to carry on, did not know by whom, and assumed it was the Appointed Person. **D14 Part 2.** He emigrated to Australia in June 2024 and has not responded to correspondence. **D14 Part 2.**

### Crown Court

The statement can be admitted under section 116 only if the prosecution proves that he is outside the United Kingdom and it is not reasonably practicable to secure his attendance, or that he cannot be found despite reasonably practicable steps. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/116" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 116 Law</a>.

The Crown must show more than “he emigrated” and “has not responded.” It should document:

- his last known Australian address;
- contact attempts;
- email, telephone and employer inquiries;
- whether video-link evidence is practicable;
- whether an international evidence request was considered;
- whether travel arrangements could reasonably be made.

If admitted, its weight is limited because:

- he did not identify the speaker;
- he did not undertake voice recognition;
- his belief that it was the Appointed Person was an assumption;
- the machine-generated crane record independently proves lifting resumed at 11:13:26 but does not prove who authorised it. **D14 Part 2.**

### Coroner

The coroner expressly identifies possible use of section 116. **D14 Part 2.** The coroner’s court is less formally bound by criminal hearsay rules, but should apply the same practical safeguards and give the evidence limited weight unless the driver can be examined.

### TCC and Employment Tribunal

The statement could be admitted as civil hearsay, but it should be treated as evidence only that the driver received an unidentified radio instruction. It is not reliable proof that Mr Delacroix-Hale, Mr Ferris or any other named person gave it.

## C. Mr Ashgrove-Pike’s solicitors’ assertions

Halbrook’s managing director states no certificate was issued. That is a direct corporate record assertion and may be supported by the register and ledger. **D09 Part 3.**

By contrast, Mr Ashgrove-Pike, through solicitors in Dubai, is said to have stated that he never issued a certificate, never approved two-tonne loading and would not have done so. He has declined to make a signed statement or attend. **D09 Part 4.**

The latter is particularly weak evidence:

- it is an unsworn account filtered through solicitors;
- it is not a witness statement;
- it is not subject to cross-examination;
- its precise wording, instructions and basis are untested;
- it risks becoming a solicitor’s assertion rather than admissible proof.

### Crown Court

It should not be admitted for the truth of the matters asserted without a proper hearsay application. The Crown would need to establish an appropriate section 116 gateway, such as that he is abroad and attendance is not reasonably practicable. Even then, the court should scrutinise whether a signed statement, video link or other evidence could be obtained.

### TCC, Employment Tribunal and coroner

The statement may be referred to as explaining why a witness is unavailable, but it should receive little or no weight as proof of the certificate issue. The much stronger evidence is:

- the Halbrook register;
- invoice ledger;
- absence of job number;
- absence of a digital signature;
- metadata;
- the contemporaneous 26 October email;
- the experts’ evidence. **D09 Parts 1 to 5; D19; D20.1.**

## D. Met Office record

The Met Office certificate is expressly certified under section 9 of the Civil Evidence Act 1995 and section 117 of the Criminal Justice Act 2003 as a business record. **D12 Part 1.**

Section 117 permits documentary statements created or received in the course of a business, subject to conditions including that the relevant person had or may reasonably be supposed to have had personal knowledge, and subject to a judicial power to exclude where reliability is doubtful. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/44/section/117" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 117 Law</a>.

The record is likely admissible in the Crown Court. But the certificate itself expressly cautions that winds at the station may differ materially from conditions at the site and at deck level. **D12 Part 1.** Its admissibility does not prove actual deck-level wind; that issue requires expert interpretation.

Its evidential value is strengthened, but not made conclusive, by Dr Fenwick-Abiodun’s estimate of 33 to 47 mph at deck level, with a central estimate of 39 mph. **D12 Part 1.**

## E. Halbrook records

The Halbrook register and ledger are potentially admissible business records. They show LB-1 to LB-6 but no LB-7 job, invoice or issued certificate. **D09 Part 3.**

They are strong evidence of Halbrook’s formal systems, but not necessarily conclusive proof that Mr Ashgrove-Pike did no informal work outside those systems. The managing director expressly reserves that possibility. **D09 Part 3.**

The correct conclusion is therefore:

- the records strongly undermine the proposition that Halbrook formally issued TWDC-BW-07;
- they do not by themselves prove that Mr Ashgrove-Pike performed no calculation, gave no oral view, or never communicated with Mr Delacroix-Hale;
- D09 Part 4 provides evidence that he did offer an informal technical view but stated that the tie schedule had to be signed off and no use made before a certificate. **D09 Part 4.**

# 3.5 Dr Vantrease’s report

## Forum map

| Issue | Crown Court | TCC | Employment Tribunal | Coroner’s court |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expert admissibility | Criminal expert-evidence controls and jury function | CPR Part 35, permission and independence | Tribunal can receive expert material but must control relevance and fairness | Expert evidence can assist, subject to scope and weight |
| Success uplift and financial dependence | Independence, reliability and weight; may lead to exclusion of parts | Direct CPR 35 independence issue | Weight only unless directly relevant | Weight and scope |
| Opinion on “gross negligence” | Impermissible usurpation of jury function | Improper legal/ultimate-issue opinion | Not central | Improper if it determines criminal/civil liability |
| Reliance on disputed transcript | Cannot assume disputed factual material | Same | Same | Same |

## A. The report’s central opinions

Dr Vantrease gives structural causation opinions, including:

- absence of three ties as primary cause;
- loading at 1,410 kg against a 750 kg SWL;
- wind not being a cause;
- a 2,000 kg certificate being incapable of a competent Category 2 check;
- Meridian’s system being grossly inadequate; and
- Mr Ferris being “grossly negligent” and a substantial cause of death. **D19 paras 1.1 to 1.7.**

His opinion rests on disclosed material with acknowledged limitations:

- he was not provided with the TowerLine tablet extraction; **D19 para. 2.1;**
- he did not see the original HSE or police photographs, only 214 photographs supplied by instructing solicitors; **D19 para. 2.2;**
- he assumed Rev. C was the controlled drawing on site; **D19 para. 2.3;**
- he could not independently verify the original position of recovered components; **D19 para. 2.4;**
- he assumed the covert-recording transcript was accurate. **D19 para. 2.5.**

These are not technicalities. They must be made explicit to every tribunal using the report.

## B. The false declaration and success-related remuneration

Dr Vantrease declared that he had not entered an arrangement under which fees depended in any way on outcome. **D19 section 3.**

Appendix G records an engagement clause providing for a 25 percent uplift if the client’s claim succeeded in whole or substantial part. **D19 Appendix G, para. G4.**

That creates a direct and serious inconsistency:

- on 8 August 2024, the declaration says no outcome-dependent fees;
- the engagement terms signed in February 2024 contain an outcome-dependent uplift;
- he says he did not read the relevant term and became aware only in February 2025;
- the term had not been removed by the time of the appendix. **D19 Appendix G, para. G4.**

The declaration was objectively inaccurate when made, whether or not he subjectively appreciated the clause. That does not automatically prove dishonesty. It does, however, create a powerful challenge to his independence, care and compliance with his overriding duty.

### Correct consequence

The report should not automatically be excluded in full. Courts distinguish between:

- an expert’s **admissibility**: whether the witness has relevant expertise and the evidence can assist the court; and
- **weight**: whether the court should accept the evidence in light of independence, methodology, instructions and financial interest.

But a contingent or success-related fee can be so incompatible with independent expert evidence that the court may exclude the report or refuse permission unless the position is remedied and the expert gives a full explanation.

The TCC should require:

1. the complete engagement letter and all amendments;
2. a binding, written and irrevocable waiver of the uplift;
3. confirmation that no other outcome-related remuneration, bonus or indirect benefit exists;
4. a supplemental compliant expert declaration;
5. disclosure of all instructions and communications relevant to factual assumptions;
6. an opportunity for Meridian to cross-examine on independence before trial if required.

## C. The 61 percent financial dependence

Dr Vantrease’s company received 61 percent of its turnover from a framework consultancy agreement with Coldbrook’s Luxembourg parent in the relevant year. **D19 Appendix G, paras G1 to G3.**

He says the framework was unconnected with the dispute and he did not consider disclosure necessary. **D19 Appendix G, para. G3.**

That judgment was unsound. A long-running commercial dependence on a party’s parent is plainly relevant to apparent independence, even if the framework work is unrelated to the site dispute. It does not prove bias. But it should have been disclosed in the original report.

The report’s initial failure to disclose:

- the 61 percent dependency;
- the success uplift; and
- the previous instructions from Ashen & Fyfe,

collectively gives Meridian a substantial basis for challenging independence. **D19 Appendix G, paras G2, G4 and G5.**

## D. Service before CPR 35.4 permission

The TCC did not grant permission for expert evidence until 16 April 2025, but Dr Vantrease’s report was served on 8 August 2024. **D19 procedural note.**

In the TCC, this was procedurally irregular. The immediate consequence should not necessarily be exclusion. The court can regularise service retrospectively, but should do so only after considering:

- why the report was commissioned and served before permission;
- whether the premature service caused prejudice;
- whether the report complied with expert duties;
- whether an amendment, replacement report or fresh permission is appropriate;
- whether costs consequences should follow.

The court has already granted Meridian permission to rely on Professor Danecourt, in the same 16 April 2025 order. **D19 procedural note.** That supports a case-managed solution: permit both experts, require compliant supplemental reports, identify the issues truly requiring expert assistance, and direct an experts’ meeting and joint statement.

## E. Reliance on the contested 02:40 transcript

Dr Vantrease says that paragraph 6.19 of his full report relies on the 02:40 passage in D20.2 and that he assumed the recording was accurate. **D19 para. 2.5.**

That is unacceptable without qualification because:

- Mr Aksoy disputes the transcript;
- Meridian’s audio examiner says the wording is not reliably resolvable;
- three alternative interpretations are possible. **D03 para. 15; D20.2.**

The court should direct Dr Vantrease to identify exactly:

- what proposition he takes from the passage;
- whether that proposition is necessary to any structural opinion;
- whether he can express the opinion without it;
- how his conclusion changes under each competing reading;
- whether he has impermissibly treated disputed factual material as established.

If the factual proposition is irrelevant to structural causation, paragraph 6.19 should be removed. If it is material, he should give conditional opinions, not assert a single assumed fact.

## F. Paragraph 1.7: “grossly negligent”

Paragraph 1.7 says Mr Ferris’s conduct was “grossly negligent” and a substantial cause of death. **D19 para. 1.7.**

The structural expert may properly say:

- what the lifting plan required;
- whether its wind trigger was technically appropriate;
- whether an engineer should recognise a particular risk;
- whether the role combination removed independent checks;
- whether particular acts or omissions materially increased structural risk.

He should not tell a jury whether Mr Ferris was “grossly negligent.” That is an evaluative legal conclusion going to criminal liability and, in the Crown Court, risks usurping the jury’s function.

Professor Danecourt correctly declines to give that opinion, stating that gross negligence is not a question of structural engineering. **D20.1 para. 8.**

### Required order

The Crown Court and TCC should exclude or redact paragraph 1.7 insofar as it states that Mr Ferris was “grossly negligent.” The witness may give properly bounded engineering evidence on causation and standards of practice.

## G. Recommended order

### TCC

Seek an order that:

1. permits reliance on Dr Vantrease only after a supplemental report and corrected declaration;
2. requires disclosure of engagement terms, framework arrangements, previous instructions and communications relevant to factual assumptions;
3. records that the 25 percent uplift is irrevocably waived;
4. excludes paragraph 1.7;
5. requires paragraph 6.19 or any opinion founded on the disputed recording to be withdrawn, revised as conditional, or deferred;
6. directs an experts’ meeting and joint statement with Professor Danecourt;
7. directs a schedule identifying agreements, disagreements, assumptions, source materials and sensitivity analyses;
8. reserves costs of the defective original disclosure and premature service.

### Crown Court

Seek a voir dire or pre-trial admissibility ruling that:

- confines Dr Vantrease to structural engineering;
- precludes use of “gross negligence” or other ultimate legal conclusions;
- identifies the financial-interest material to be disclosed to the defence;
- requires the prosecution to disclose the original instructions, engagement terms and all relevant material bearing on independence;
- ensures the jury receives an appropriate direction on contested expert evidence and the report’s assumptions.

### Employment Tribunal and coroner

The report is not central to the employment claim. The Employment Tribunal should avoid allowing a structural causation dispute to overwhelm the protected-disclosure issues.

The coroner may receive the engineering evidence, but should distinguish technical causation from any impermissible conclusion about criminal culpability.

# 3.6 Ms Raghunathan’s Employment Tribunal claim

## Forum

This claim belongs in the **Employment Tribunal, Leeds**. It is not determined by the Crown Court, TCC or coroner’s court. Findings in those proceedings may be relevant evidence, but they do not automatically decide the statutory employment issues.

The key statutory provisions are:

- qualifying disclosure: <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/43B" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43B Law</a>;
- disclosure to a prescribed person: <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/43F" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43F Law</a>;
- detriment: <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/47B" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 47B Law</a>;
- automatic unfair dismissal: <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/103A" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 103A Law</a>;
- contractual restriction on whistleblowing: <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/43J" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43J Law</a>.

## A. Does the 16 November email qualify as a protected disclosure?

### Qualifying disclosure

Section 43B defines a qualifying disclosure as information which, in the worker’s reasonable belief, is made in the public interest and tends to show one of the specified failures, including a criminal offence, breach of legal obligation, danger to health or safety, or concealment. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/43B" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43B Law</a>.

The email contains information, not merely a bare allegation. It identifies:

- the date the LB-7 certificate was placed on the shared drive;
- the date on the document’s face;
- the absence of an entry in the temporary works register;
- the absence of a Halbrook job number;
- the proposed use of the document to secure a £610,000 payment;
- the possible criminality;
- the death and potential health-and-safety breach. **D10 item 10.7; D18 Part 18.1.**

This plainly tends to show matters within section 43B:

- potential criminal offending;
- potential breach of legal obligations;
- danger to health and safety;
- potential deliberate concealment.

The fact that she had a pre-existing bonus grievance does not prevent the email from being protected. It may be deployed by Meridian as context affecting reasonable belief or motive, but it does not transform a detailed whistleblowing disclosure into a mere commercial disagreement. **D18 Part 18.1.**

### Public interest

The public-interest limb is strongly met. The subject matter was a fatal workplace incident, potentially dangerous temporary works, potential false regulatory evidence, and potential procurement of a major payment by false representation. **D10 item 10.7; D18 Part 18.1.**

### Disclosure to HSE

Ms Raghunathan called the HSE inspector on 17 November 2023. **D01 para. 20; D18 Part 18.1.** Section 43F provides for disclosure to a prescribed person if the worker reasonably believes the relevant failure falls within that person’s prescribed remit and reasonably believes the information and any allegation are substantially true. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/43F" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43F Law</a>.

The bundle identifies that route as her case. **D18 Part 18.1.** On the information available, a report to HSE about fatal construction-site safety, temporary works and workplace health-and-safety compliance is strongly capable of satisfying that route.

### Conclusion

The tribunal should find that the 16 November email was a qualifying disclosure and that the HSE call was capable of being a further protected disclosure.

## B. No two-year qualifying period is required for section 103A

Ms Raghunathan had 1 year, 10 months and 29 days’ service and therefore cannot pursue ordinary unfair dismissal under section 98. **D18 p.1.**

That does not defeat section 103A. Section 103A provides that an employee is unfairly dismissed if the reason, or principal reason, is that the employee made a protected disclosure. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/103A" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 103A Law</a>.

The statutory question is therefore not whether she had enough service. It is whether Meridian proves that the reason or principal reason for dismissal was genuine redundancy rather than whistleblowing.

## C. The reason or principal reason for dismissal

The Supreme Court has confirmed that where a person higher in the hierarchy determines that an employee should be dismissed for a hidden reason and conceals it behind an invented reason adopted by the formal decision-maker, the hidden reason is the reason for dismissal. [*Royal Mail Group Ltd v Jhuti* [2019] UKSC 55 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2019/55).

That principle is particularly important because:

- Mr Ferris was the recipient of the disclosure; **D10 item 10.7;**
- the board resolution gave the Construction Director the selection role; **D18 Part 18.2;**
- Mr Ferris was stated to chair the purported board meeting; **D18 Part 18.2;**
- the dismissal occurred 22 days after the disclosure and 21 days after the HSE call; **D18 Part 18.1; D18 Part 18.3;**
- no consultation, pool or selection criteria existed; **D18 Part 18.3; D18 Part 18.6;**
- a senior QS role at a comparable salary was advertised less than a month later. **D18 Part 18.5.**

The tribunal should not accept the formal redundancy explanation merely because HR signed the dismissal letter. It must investigate whether the actual reason was the protected disclosure.

## D. The board minute: four defects and their evidential effect

The minute is central to Meridian’s case. It is said to record a 2 November decision to restructure the commercial function and make one senior QS redundant. **D18 Part 18.2.**

It has four distinct defects.

### 1. Impossible reference to the Brackenmoor incident

The minute refers to “the Brackenmoor incident review,” but the incident occurred on 14 November, 12 days after the minute’s stated date. **D18 Part 18.2.**

This is the most serious defect. Meridian’s explanation is later typographical insertion. **D18 Part 18.2.** The tribunal may accept that explanation only if supported by contemporaneous notes, drafts, email circulation, calendar invitations, board packs and a credible account of how the error arose.

### 2. Late creation metadata and no manuscript original

The electronic file was created on 29 April 2024, and Meridian says it was a re-typing of manuscript minutes. The manuscript original has not been produced. **D18 Part 18.2.**

That does not conclusively prove fabrication, because re-typing can occur. But it materially weakens the document unless the original notes or a reliable provenance trail are produced.

### 3. Mr Nightingale-Obi’s attendance after resignation

The minute records Mr Nightingale-Obi as attending by telephone, but he resigned on 27 October 2023, six days before the minute date. **D18 Part 18.2.**

Meridian’s explanation is that his name was carried over from a template. **D18 Part 18.2.** Again, that is possible, but it is a second error in a document relied upon as the central contemporaneous explanation for dismissal.

### 4. Late disclosure

The minute was produced only on 3 May 2024 after an order for specific disclosure and was absent from Meridian’s earlier disclosure list certified by its solicitors on 12 February 2024. **D18 Part 18.2.**

That supports a request for disclosure into the document’s provenance and may support an adverse inference if Meridian cannot provide an adequate explanation.

### Overall evidential result

The tribunal should not silently choose between “genuine but badly transcribed minute” and “retrospectively manufactured minute.” It should reason in the alternative:

- **If genuine:** the minute is still weak proof of a real redundancy process because there was no consultation, pool or selection criteria, and an ostensibly comparable role was advertised on 4 January. **D18 Parts 18.3, 18.5 and 18.6.**
- **If not genuine or materially unreliable:** it strongly supports an inference that redundancy was invented after the protected disclosure.

On either route, Meridian has difficulty proving redundancy was the reason or principal reason.

## E. The dismissal letter and contemporaneous conduct

The letter says dismissal was redundancy and that there was no appeal because Ms Raghunathan had less than two years’ service. It also says she must not disclose information about the project to third parties, including regulatory bodies, without written consent. **D18 Part 18.3.**

That regulatory-body warning is highly relevant because:

- it followed her written threat to report to HSE; **D10 item 10.7;**
- she had in fact contacted HSE the day after the email; **D18 Part 18.1;**
- it is inconsistent with a neutral, routine redundancy process;
- it reinforces the inference of an attempt to deter reporting.

It is not a defence that the claimant lacked two years’ ordinary unfair-dismissal service. The correct legal question is the section 103A reason-or-principal-reason test.

## F. Clause 9: voidness and detriment

Section 43J makes void any agreement term insofar as it purports to prevent a worker from making a protected disclosure. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/43J" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 43J Law</a>.

Clause 9.1 purported to prohibit any report or communication to HSE, police, coroner, insurer or any other regulator concerning the company, project or incident. Clause 9.2 imposed repayment and indemnity consequences; clause 9.3 threatened injunctive relief. **D18 Part 18.4.**

The statutory result is clear: clause 9 was void insofar as it purported to prevent protected disclosures. Its withdrawal on 20 December does not retrospectively validate it or erase its evidential significance. **D18 Part 18.4.**

### Was proposing the clause itself a detriment?

It is strongly arguable that it was.

A proposed settlement term that threatens financial repayment, indemnity liability and injunctive relief for reporting to regulators is objectively capable of deterring a worker from making further disclosures. **D18 Part 18.4.**

The tribunal should determine:

- who drafted the term;
- whether it was truly a template error;
- who reviewed and authorised it;
- why it was included in a case involving a recent HSE disclosure;
- whether Mr Ferris’s copied involvement is relevant;
- whether it formed part of a continuing course of detriments with the dismissal letter and covert monitoring.

The fact that the term was withdrawn promptly is relevant to remedy and intent, but does not necessarily defeat liability.

## G. Likely result on section 103A

The claimant has a strong case that the protected disclosure was the reason or principal reason for dismissal.

The principal indicators are:

- detailed disclosure on 16 November; **D10 item 10.7;**
- HSE contact on 17 November; **D18 Part 18.1;**

## H. Remedies

### Compensation

Section 123 provides for a just-and-equitable compensatory award based on loss caused by dismissal, including reasonably incurred expenses and lost benefits, subject to mitigation and potential contributory-fault reduction. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/123" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 123 Law</a>.

The ordinary statutory cap does not apply where the claimant is regarded as unfairly dismissed under section 103A. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/124" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 124 Law</a>.

The claimant should claim and evidence:

- net lost salary;
- pension loss;
- car allowance and contractual benefits;
- notice pay, subject to the separate breach-of-contract claim;
- reasonable job-search expenses;
- loss of future earnings if unemployment or reduced earnings continued;
- any loss caused by the detriments separately from dismissal;
- interest where available.

The tribunal must consider mitigation. Ms Raghunathan should retain all job applications, recruiter correspondence, interview records, payslips and evidence of efforts to obtain equivalent work.

### Injury to feelings

For section 47B detriment claims, injury-to-feelings compensation may be available, subject to proof of detriment and causation. The claimant should keep a contemporaneous account of the impact of:

- the regulatory gagging language;
- proposed clause 9;
- covert surveillance;
- treatment after the disclosure;
- dismissal and its aftermath.

### Reinstatement and re-engagement

These remedies should be considered but may be impracticable on the facts because of the alleged breakdown in trust, the purported surveillance, the regulatory dispute and the senior nature of the role. The claimant should nevertheless preserve the option until remedy-stage evidence is complete.

### Interim relief

For a section 103A dismissal claim, an employee may apply for interim relief, but the application must be presented within seven days immediately following the effective date of termination. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/18/section/128" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Employment Rights Act 1996, s 128 Law</a>.

The termination date was 8 December 2023. **D18 p.1; D18 Part 18.3.** That seven-day deadline has long passed, so interim relief is no longer available in this case. It should nevertheless be recognised as the urgent remedy that would have been available at the time.

### Uplifts

The bundle does not provide a retrieved statutory source establishing the detailed legal conditions for an Acas Code uplift in this particular whistleblowing context. The claimant should plead any applicable procedural uplift only after verifying the precise statutory basis and whether the relevant Acas Code applied to the pleaded claims. The tribunal’s costs and case-management powers remain separately relevant given the late and potentially unreliable board-minute disclosure.

## I. Obligations on legal representatives arising from D18

### Halloway Brant / Meridian’s representatives

The key obligations are practical:

2. **Do not mislead the tribunal or permit a client to do so.**

3. **Reassess conflicts and continuing retainer.**  
   Halloway Brant’s role is complicated by the disputed privileged communications, the late disclosure, and the note recording possible concern that it could not continue if the certificate were produced without qualification. **D10 item 10.6; D20.3.**

4. **Preserve evidence.**  
   The firm and client must preserve the manuscript minute if it exists, meeting notes, board calendars, document metadata, disclosure logs, surveillance data, CCTV records and communications about Ms Raghunathan’s dismissal.

5. **Obtain independent advice where the firm’s conduct may be in issue.**  
   The firm should not advise Meridian on the propriety of its own disclosure conduct or its own potential professional obligations without independent review.

# Overall conclusion

The bundle presents interlocking but distinct proceedings:

- **Crown Court:** criminal admissibility, hearsay, expert-evidence boundaries, disclosure fairness and possible use of the recording;
- **TCC:** CPR 31.20 privilege application, contract/insurance evidence, expert permission and civil evidential management;
- **Employment Tribunal:** section 103A dismissal, section 47B detriment, void gagging provisions, covert monitoring and remedies;
- **coroner’s court:** broad factual inquiry into the death, with less rigid evidential rules but careful treatment of hearsay, disputed recordings and privileged material.

The most important immediate litigation steps are:

1. obtain a preliminary privilege/iniquity/waiver ruling on D20.3 and D10.6;
2. require original audio and a judicial transcript protocol for D20.2;
3. seek disclosure and a preliminary fairness ruling concerning D13 surveillance;
4. seek narrow hearsay rulings rather than allowing broad untested assertions;
5. confine Dr Vantrease to genuine structural expertise and require full independence disclosure;
6. in the Employment Tribunal, pursue focused disclosure proving or disproving the alleged pre-existing redundancy rationale and the true purpose of the surveillance.

## Do not overlook
- **Burden without qualifying service:** where the employee lacks two years' service the s.98 route is closed and it is for the *claimant* to prove that the protected disclosure was the reason or principal reason for dismissal (the *Smith v Hayle Town Council* / *Kuzel v Roche* line) — verify against current authority before relying on it.
