# Advice for the TCC Trial: Commercial and Insurance Consequences

## Overall conclusions

1. **The stronger provisional view is that Meridian obtained a payment-notice entitlement to the GBP 1,842,500 notified sum**, because Coldbrook’s Pay Less Notice was served on 12 December 2023, whereas clause 4.9.4 required it no later than five days before the 15 December final date for payment. On the ordinary computation, that deadline was 10 December. The direct-fraud exception is Coldbrook’s substantial answer, but it is strongest only as to the GBP 610,000 Milestone 6 component, whose claimed entitlement depended on TWDC-BW-07. The remaining GBP 1,232,500 requires separate analysis of the validity and substantiation of Interim Application 18, which the present extract does not permit. The statutory payment regime remains subject to a later true-value determination after payment. [*Grove Developments Ltd v S&T (UK) Ltd* [2018] EWHC (Tcc) 123 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/tcc/2018/123); [*Bexheat Ltd v Essex Services Group Ltd* [2022] EWHC (Tcc) 936 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/tcc/2022/936).

2. **Coldbrook’s 19 December termination was probably ineffective under clause 8.4.1**, both because the 8 December default notice was not emailed, despite clause 1.7 stating that a notice not sent by email is “of no effect”, and because termination was purportedly effected only 11 days after the notice rather than after the stipulated 14-day cure period. Coldbrook’s better case is that clause 8.4.4 operated independently and permitted immediate termination on its reasonable belief of fraud. That depends upon establishing what information Coldbrook actually possessed by 19 December 2023, not what later forensic evidence revealed. On the supplied material, the absence of the May 2024 metadata report is a serious obstacle, but not necessarily decisive.

3. **Caldera has an arguable, but not presently clear, basis to avoid or decline cover.** The proposal answers concerning Category 3 checks, an independent Temporary Works Coordinator, and a SHEQ Director were apparently inaccurate. However, Meridian has a substantial statutory-answer case based on the broker’s 4 September email and Mr Pilbeam’s contemporaneous underwriting note. Caldera must also prove that, but for a qualifying breach, it would not have written the risk or would have written it on different terms. A post-loss condition requiring compliance with temporary-works procedures is unlikely to assist Caldera under section 11 unless Meridian cannot show that non-compliance could not have increased the risk of the actual loss. On these facts it plainly could have increased that risk. The alleged false certification is much more likely to be material to the claim than a merely collateral lie.

4. **For Mr Aksoy and the dependants, the best practical route is a direct negligence claim against Meridian, pursued in parallel with a statutory claim against TowerLine’s possible insurer.** TowerLine’s liquidation enables a claim under the Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010 if cover existed. But the oral “hold covered” issue is uncertain and may require expensive satellite litigation concerning the broker’s authority. Meridian, by contrast, was the principal contractor, controlled the permit-to-load system, appointed the same individual to safety-critical operational roles, and is alleged to have resumed lifting without the required recorded authority. Those facts provide a materially stronger direct-duty and breach case. The three-year limitation deadline for the personal injury, estate and Fatal Accidents claims is likely to expire on **14 November 2026**, subject to any later date-of-knowledge argument. Proceedings should be issued well before then. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/10/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010, s 1 Law</a>; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1980/58/section/11" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Limitation Act 1980, ss 11–12 Law</a>.

## 2.1 Meridian’s entitlement to the GBP 1,842,500 notified sum

### Conclusion

Meridian has the better argument that **the full GBP 1,842,500 became the contractual “sum due” for the December payment cycle**, because neither a timely Payment Notice nor a timely Pay Less Notice was served. The strongest qualification is that the payment mechanism should not be used to compel payment of the portion of the application that was itself procured by a directly relevant fraud, particularly the GBP 610,000 Milestone 6 claim.

The practical result is likely to be one of the following:

- Meridian succeeds in establishing an immediate payment entitlement to the whole notified sum, subject to Coldbrook’s later true-value and damages claims;
- Coldbrook defeats enforcement only to the extent of the fraud-tainted Milestone 6 amount;
- Coldbrook establishes that Interim Application 18 was not a valid payment application at all, in which event there was no valid sum due under clause 4.9.5.

The last is Coldbrook’s most important alternative route, but it turns on clause 4.7.4 and the actual application documents, neither of which is reproduced in the available contract extract.

### A. The contractual payment timetable

The payment timetable in the amended JCT contract is clear:

| Event | Contractual date |
|---|---:|
| Interim Valuation Date | 24 November 2023 |
| Due date, seven days later | 1 December 2023 |
| Latest Payment Notice date, five days after due date | 6 December 2023 |
| Final date for payment, 21 days after due date | 15 December 2023 |
| Latest Pay Less Notice date, five days before final date | 10 December 2023 |

Clause 4.9.2 required Coldbrook to issue its Payment Notice not later than five days after the due date. No Payment Notice was served by 6 December 2023. Clause 4.9.4 required a Pay Less Notice not later than five days before the final date for payment. Clause 4.9.5 then provided that, where neither notice was given, the sum stated as due in Meridian’s Interim Application was the sum due. Interim Application 18 sought a net GBP 1,842,500, expressly including the GBP 610,000 Milestone 6 sum. The final date was 15 December 2023. Coldbrook’s Pay Less Notice was dated and emailed on 12 December, and received by recorded delivery on 13 December. **D15 Part 1; D15 Part 2; D15 Part 3.**

The construction-payment legislation requires a construction contract to contain a notice mechanism after the payment due date that specifies the sum considered due and the basis of its calculation. The statutory requirement applies in England and Wales. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/53/section/110A" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996, s 110A Law</a>.

### B. Computation of “five days before” the final date

#### Meridian’s case: 10 December was the deadline

On the ordinary and commercially natural reading, “not later than five days before the final date for payment” means that the notice had to be served by **10 December 2023**. A notice on 12 December was only three calendar days before 15 December. The amendment does not use words such as “five clear days”, nor does the available contract extract include a provision disapplying ordinary calendar-day computation. **D15 Part 1; D15 Part 2.**

That reading accords with the function of a Pay Less Notice. It is intended to give the contractor a defined period before the final payment date to understand the payer’s position and the calculation behind it. Coldbrook’s notice gave Meridian only three days before 15 December. **D15 Part 3.**

#### Coldbrook’s case: five “clear” days

Coldbrook argues that “five days before” means five clear days, excluding the date of service and the final date. That submission is weak on the available wording. Its difficulty is not merely that 12 December is too late under the ordinary calendar-day count; it is that a “clear days” construction normally requires a textual basis or a relevant contractual interpretation clause. No such basis appears in D15 Part 1. **D15 Part 1; D15 Part 2.**

Even if the day of service and the final date were excluded, 13 and 14 December are the only clear intervening days between service by email on 12 December and final payment on 15 December. The recorded-delivery version was not received until 13 December, leaving only 14 December as an intervening day. On either service route, the notice cannot sensibly be described as five clear days before 15 December. **D15 Part 3.**

### C. Effect of a late Pay Less Notice: the Grove and Bexheat line

The payment authorities do not ordinarily permit the payer to use a later valuation or adjudication as a device to avoid its immediate obligation to pay a notified sum. In *Grove*, the TCC held that an employer whose payment or Pay Less Notice was deficient or absent could pay the amount due under the contractor’s application and then pursue the true value in a later adjudication. [*Grove Developments Ltd v S&T (UK) Ltd* [2018] EWHC (Tcc) 123 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/tcc/2018/123).

The Court of Appeal decision in the same dispute is identified in the available materials as *S&T (UK) Ltd v Grove Developments Ltd*. It confirms the hierarchy under which the immediate notified-sum payment obligation is not displaced by the later adjudication process. The later TCC decision in *Bexheat* applies that approach: the paying party could not commence a true-value adjudication for the relevant application before complying with the immediate notified-sum obligation. [*Bexheat Ltd v Essex Services Group Ltd* [2022] EWHC (Tcc) 936 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/tcc/2022/936).

Thus, if Coldbrook’s Pay Less Notice was late, the ordinary consequence is:

1. Meridian had an immediate right to the notified sum;
2. Coldbrook could later pursue adjudication, litigation, counterclaim, set-off where contractually permitted, or a final-account adjustment concerning the true value;
3. Coldbrook could not simply refuse payment by asserting its own valuation in place of a valid, timely payment notice.

That result gives Meridian a strong payment claim. It does **not** determine the final value of the works or prevent Coldbrook recovering any overpayment in later proceedings.

### D. The fraud exception: SG South and Gosvenor

#### Principle

The usual “pay now, argue later” result does not mean that the court must enforce a claim which is itself directly fraudulent or fraudulently induced. In *SG South*, the TCC held that fraud may be raised as a defence in adjudication enforcement if it is a real defence to the claim. It must be supported by clear and unambiguous evidence. Crucially, the court distinguished fraud that directly affects the subject matter of the decision from fraud which is independent of it, such as a separate cross-claim. [*SG South Ltd v King’s Head Cirencester LLP* [2009] EWHC (Tcc) 2645 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/tcc/2009/2645).

The later appellate decision in *Gosvenor London Ltd v Aygun Aluminium UK Ltd* confirms that questions concerning enforcement and a stay are fact-sensitive and discretionary in the adjudication context. [*Gosvenor London Ltd v Aygun Aluminium UK Ltd* [2018] EWCA Civ 2695 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2018/2695).

#### Application to Milestone 6

The alleged fraud is not merely collateral to Milestone 6. The milestone was contractually triggered only if all loading platforms were independently certified in accordance with BS 5975, evidenced by relevant Category 2 check certificates, and the Employer’s Agent was satisfied. **D15 Part 1.**

TWDC-BW-07 was the certificate said to satisfy that condition for LB-7. It purported to be a Category 2 certificate dated 27 October 2023, but:

- its Temporary Works Coordinator countersignature box was blank;
- it had no Halbrook job number;
- it did not carry a wet or digital signature;
- the PDF metadata records creation on 13 November 2023 under Mr Delacroix-Hale’s Meridian-domain account;
- the source template was modified on the Meridian share shortly before the PDF’s creation;
- Halbrook’s register contains no instruction, job number, invoice, or issued certificate for LB-7;
- the Halbrook managing director stated that Halbrook had no record of instructing or issuing TWDC-BW-07;
- the underlying document stated a 2,000 kg safe working load, although the authenticated drawing gives a 750 kg load limit. **D09 Parts 1–5.**

The factual allegation is therefore that Meridian deployed the very document required to trigger payment, while knowing it was not a genuine independent certificate. If proved, that is direct fraud affecting the payment claim, not an unrelated cross-claim. It provides Coldbrook with a serious *SG South* answer to enforcement of the Milestone 6 element. [*SG South Ltd v King’s Head Cirencester LLP* [2009] EWHC (Tcc) 2645 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/tcc/2009/2645).

The evidence is strengthened by the 13 November email chain. At 17:58 Mr Delacroix-Hale wrote that scaffolders had “flagged something” at levels 6 and 7 and that LB-7 would realistically load on Wednesday, not Tuesday. At 19:44, Mr Ferris replied that December was not an option, stated that he needed Milestone 6 certified, and said, “Find a way through it and tell me when it’s done.” **D10 items 10.4–10.5.**

It is further strengthened by the BW SITE LEADS messages recording, after concerns that the east end was not ready, Mr Ferris’s message “Whatever it takes. Milestone 6 must certify by Friday”, followed later that evening by Mr Delacroix-Hale’s message: “Sorted. Paper is in order.” **D11 para 1.**

#### The strongest point against Coldbrook’s fraud defence

There are four substantial points for Meridian.

1. **Forgery has not yet been finally proved.** The digital examiner expressly states that file-system and PDF dates can be manipulated and cannot conclusively establish authorship. The examiner cannot exclude the possibility that the 13 November file was a retyping of an earlier genuine certificate, even though no evidence of such a certificate was found. **D09 Part 2.**

2. **Mr Ashgrove-Pike had apparently undertaken some analysis.** His 26 October email said that he had “run the level 7 bay through by hand”, thought it worked but was tight, and intended to formalise matters later. He said no panel should be placed until a certificate had been obtained. That may support an argument that the document was an imperfect reconstruction of a real engineering exercise rather than an invented certification. **D09 Part 4.**

3. **The allegedly defective payment component is only GBP 610,000.** The application total was GBP 1,842,500. Coldbrook must explain why an alleged fraud concerning Milestone 6 defeats the whole amount, rather than only the milestone and any genuinely connected items. **D15 Part 2; D15 Part 3.**

4. **The fraud evidence became materially stronger after the payment date.** The Northmoor metadata report was served on 6 May 2024, after the December 2023 payment timetable. **D17 Part 4.** At the time when Coldbrook had to serve a notice, it may have had suspicions but not the later forensic proof.

### E. Coldbrook’s alternative: no valid application was made

Coldbrook also says that Interim Application 18 was invalid because it lacked substantiation required by clause 4.7.4. **D15 Part 3.**

This point could be decisive, but the available bundle extract does not reproduce:

- clause 4.7.4;
- the full Interim Application 18;
- the substantiation said to be missing;
- the contractual consequence of non-compliance with clause 4.7.4.

There is an important distinction between:

- an application that is valid but overstated or fraudulently inflated; and
- a document which fails the contractual conditions required even to constitute an interim payment application.

If the first, clause 4.9.5 and the notified-sum mechanism are engaged, subject to the direct-fraud argument. If the second, there may have been no valid default payment notice and no sum due under clause 4.9.5. **D15 Parts 1–3.**

Meridian’s answer is that Coldbrook’s own conduct is inconsistent with treating the application as invalid: Coldbrook’s Employer’s Agent issued the Milestone 6 certificate on 30 November, and Coldbrook served a Pay Less Notice that treated the application as an application but assessed the sum due at nil. **D15 Part 2; D15 Parts 3–4.** That does not necessarily amount to a legal waiver of the invalidity point, but it is factually powerful.

### F. Effect of Vellacott Marr’s 30 November Milestone 6 certificate

The Employer’s Agent certified that the frame was complete to level 9 and that all loading platforms were complete and independently certified, having reviewed TWDC-BW-01 to TWDC-BW-07. **D15 Part 4.**

That certificate assists Meridian in three ways:

1. **Contractual satisfaction:** Schedule 3 made the Employer’s Agent’s satisfaction part of the milestone trigger. The certificate is facially strong evidence that condition was met. **D15 Part 1; D15 Part 4.**

2. **Estoppel and waiver:** Meridian can contend that Coldbrook, acting through its authorised certifier, represented that the milestone conditions were met and cannot now deny that representation after Meridian relied upon it in its application. **D15 Part 4.**

3. **Reasonable-belief issue:** Coldbrook’s later assertion that it reasonably believed Meridian had committed fraud is weakened by the fact that its own professional agent issued a certificate after the incident and after Coldbrook had instructed solicitors. **D15 Part 4.**

However, the certificate is not conclusive. Mr Vellacott-Ahn’s stated position is that he merely reviewed the documents as a set and did not verify their authenticity. He did not notice the absent job number, absent signatures, blank countersignature box, or different typeface. **D15 Part 3.** If TWDC-BW-07 was forged or falsely represented as an independent certificate, Coldbrook can argue that the agent’s satisfaction was induced by fraud and is therefore ineffective for the relevant purpose.

### Bottom line on 2.1

Meridian should advance the following primary position:

- the 12 December Pay Less Notice was two days late;
- clause 4.9.5 therefore made GBP 1,842,500 the sum due;
- Coldbrook must pay first and litigate the true value later;
- the Employer’s Agent’s 30 November certificate reinforces the prima facie payment entitlement.

Coldbrook’s strongest response is not its late Pay Less Notice, nor a later true-value exercise. It is the contention that the Milestone 6 application was directly induced by a fabricated certificate and therefore falls within the narrow anti-fraud exception. The likely battleground is whether Coldbrook can establish that allegation sufficiently clearly, and whether it infects all or only GBP 610,000 of the notified sum.

## 2.2 Whether Coldbrook’s termination on 19 December 2023 was effective

### Conclusion

The better view is that **the termination was ineffective under clause 8.4.1**. The default notice did not comply with the express service mechanism in clause 1.7, and, even if it had, Coldbrook gave its termination notice before the 14-day cure period had expired.

Coldbrook’s viable route is clause 8.4.4. It needs to establish that the clause created an independent, immediate right to terminate where Coldbrook reasonably believed that Meridian had acted fraudulently. The word “notwithstanding” is important. But whether Coldbrook held the requisite reasonable belief on 19 December 2023 is fact-sensitive. The later metadata report cannot retrospectively create a reasonable belief on that date, though it may corroborate a belief independently held at the time.

### A. Contractual structure

The relevant contractual provisions are:

- clause 8.1: section 8 is the sole means by which Meridian’s employment may be brought to an end;
- clause 8.4.1: Coldbrook may issue a default notice, and if the default continues for 14 days after receipt, may terminate on or within 21 days after the 14-day period;
- clause 8.4.1.6: a material misrepresentation concerning statutory health-and-safety compliance or a Schedule 3 certificate is a specified default;
- clause 8.4.4: notwithstanding clause 8.4.1, Coldbrook may terminate with immediate effect where it reasonably believes Meridian acted fraudulently;
- clause 1.7: notices must be delivered by hand or recorded delivery and additionally sent by email; a notice not emailed is “of no effect”. **D15 Part 1; D16 Parts 1–4.**

The provisions create a deliberate distinction:

- ordinary material misrepresentation, including a false certificate, is treated as a remediable “specified default” under clause 8.4.1.6;
- fraud potentially activates the separate immediate-termination route in clause 8.4.4.

The force of “notwithstanding clause 8.4.1” is that clause 8.4.4 is not necessarily constrained by the 14-day cure regime. If every fraud allegation had to follow the 14-day machinery, clause 8.4.4 would have little independent work to do. **D15 Part 1.**

### B. The 8 December default notice was probably ineffective

The default notice was hand-delivered on 8 December at 14:20, but it was not emailed. **D16 document 16.1; D16 document 16.4.**

Clause 1.7 requires both a prescribed primary mode of service and email, and then says in express terms that a notice not sent by email “shall be of no effect.” **D15 Part 1.**

That is powerful mandatory language. This is not a minor defect where the recipient did in fact receive notice; the parties stipulated the consequence of failure to email. Meridian received the notice by hand, but the contractual consequence remains that the notice was “of no effect” if clause 1.7 is construed according to its ordinary wording. **D16 document 16.1; D16 document 16.4.**

Coldbrook may argue that email was directory rather than mandatory because:

- Meridian received the notice on the date it was sent;
- Meridian suffered no practical prejudice;
- a contrary interpretation would elevate form over substance.

That is its strongest service point. It is nevertheless difficult to reconcile with the express agreed consequence: “shall be of no effect.” **D15 Part 1.**

### C. Prematurity: eleven days rather than fourteen

Even if the 8 December notice were effective, it afforded Meridian 14 days from receipt to cure the specified default. Receipt was on 8 December. The cure period would therefore expire on 22 December. Coldbrook’s termination notice was served on 19 December, only 11 days later. **D15 Part 2; D16 documents 16.1–16.2.**

That is premature on the straightforward construction of clause 8.4.1. It is also not a merely technical point: the clause expressly gives the contractor a defined contractual opportunity to cure the alleged default before employment may be terminated.

The strongest point for Coldbrook is that a fraudulent certificate cannot meaningfully be “cured” by the passage of time. If Meridian had falsely represented that a certificate existed, it could not retrospectively convert a false contemporaneous representation into a true one. Yet that submission goes to the appropriateness of relying on clause 8.4.4, not to rewriting the clear sequence in clause 8.4.1. **D15 Part 1; D16 document 16.2.**

### D. Clause 8.4.4: did Coldbrook have a reasonable belief in fraud?

#### The legal and contractual test

Clause 8.4.4 does not require Coldbrook to prove fraud conclusively before giving notice. It requires that Coldbrook “reasonably believes” Meridian “has acted fraudulently.” **D15 Part 1.**

The natural reading is a mixed subjective-objective test:

1. Coldbrook must actually have held the belief at the time of termination; and
2. the belief must have been objectively reasonable in light of the material then available.

A later report may prove that the suspicion was correct, but it cannot itself establish that Coldbrook possessed a reasonable belief on 19 December 2023. The question is what Coldbrook knew, reasonably inferred, or was told on that date.

#### Material potentially available by 19 December

By 19 December, Coldbrook knew at least that:

- the fatal collapse occurred on 14 November 2023;
- Meridian had produced TWDC-BW-07;
- the certificate was dated 27 October 2023;
- the certificate carried no Halbrook job number;
- the certificate had no digital or wet signature;
- the certificate’s Temporary Works Coordinator countersignature box was blank;
- the certificate stated a 2,000 kg safe working load;
- the authentic controlled drawing known to HSE later stated 750 kg, although it was not established which revision was on site;
- Halbrook had no register entry, invoice or issued certificate recorded for LB-7, if that information had then been obtained;
- the Milestone 6 payment sought GBP 610,000 and depended on the certificate. **D01 paras 10–15 and 34; D09 Parts 1–5; D15 Parts 1–4.**

The precise difficulty is evidential: the bundle does not identify what Coldbrook knew by 19 December as distinct from what became known through later investigation. The metadata report was served on 6 May 2024. **D17 Part 4.** The Halbrook letter is dated 3 March 2024. **D09 Part 3.** Coldbrook cannot rely on either document as material actually held on 19 December without further evidence that the underlying facts were already known.

#### Evidence favouring Coldbrook’s reasonable-belief case

There was potentially a compelling contemporaneous basis for suspicion even without the later metadata report:

- TWDC-BW-07 was anomalous on its face when compared with the certificates for LB-1 to LB-6;
- it lacked the signature and job-number features carried by the earlier certificates;
- its countersignature requirement was unfulfilled;
- the 2,000 kg safe working load was markedly higher than the 750 kg figure in the controlled drawing;
- its use followed a fatal collapse involving a load calculated at approximately 1,410 kg;
- the certificate was central to an immediate claim for a GBP 610,000 milestone payment. **D01 paras 10–15; D09 Parts 1–5; D15 Parts 1–4.**

If Coldbrook can prove that it had identified a combination of those matters before 19 December, the court may find that it had a reasonable belief in fraud even though it did not yet have final forensic proof.

#### The strongest point against Coldbrook

The strongest point is that the central forensic evidence was not available until months later. The metadata report showing that the PDF was generated on 13 November 2023 was served on 6 May 2024. **D09 Part 2; D17 Part 4.**

Further, Coldbrook’s own Employer’s Agent certified Milestone 6 on 30 November 2023, after the collapse. The agent certified that all platforms were complete and independently certified, expressly listing TWDC-BW-01 to TWDC-BW-07. **D15 Part 4.**

That certificate does not prevent Coldbrook from alleging it was deceived. But it permits Meridian to argue:

- Coldbrook’s professional agent had the certificate and accepted it;
- Coldbrook cannot plausibly say that it held an objectively reasonable belief in fraud only 19 days later without identifying genuinely new evidence;
- the termination notice’s assertion of fraud may have been an attempt to bypass the 14-day default-notice procedure.

### E. Does the common-law right to accept repudiation survive clause 8.1?

Clause 8.1 provides that the provisions of section 8 are “the sole means” by which Meridian’s employment may be brought to an end. **D16 document 16.4.**

That wording gives Meridian the better argument that the common-law right to terminate for repudiatory breach was contractually excluded. A clause stating that contractual termination provisions are the “sole means” is difficult to reconcile with a parallel common-law right to terminate for the same conduct.

Coldbrook argues that, even if the contractual machinery failed, the fabrication and deployment of a safety certificate after a fatal incident amounted to a repudiatory breach and that the 19 December notice was an acceptance of it. **D16 document 16.2; D16 document 16.4.**

The strength of that argument depends on the court’s construction of clause 8.1:

- If section 8 is an exhaustive termination code, Coldbrook cannot use common law to avoid the contractual service and timing requirements.
- If clause 8.1 is construed as prescribing only contractual termination rights while leaving fundamental common-law repudiation rights intact, Coldbrook may rely on the alternative acceptance.

The better construction is the first. The phrase “sole means” is unusually explicit. The strongest point against that conclusion is that the alleged misconduct is not an ordinary default but alleged fraud connected with a fatality, safety certification, and a payment claim. A court may hesitate to construe a standard-form exclusivity clause as depriving the innocent party of a common-law right in such extreme circumstances. But that policy argument cannot displace clear contractual language without a strong interpretative basis.

### F. Consequences if termination was wrongful

If Coldbrook wrongfully terminated Meridian’s employment:

1. **Meridian remains entitled to treat Coldbrook’s conduct as repudiatory**, as its solicitors did on 21 December 2023. **D16 document 16.3.**

2. **Meridian may claim damages for wrongful termination**, potentially including:
   - loss of profit on the unperformed balance of the works;
   - losses caused by demobilisation;
   - overhead and financing losses, subject to proof, causation, remoteness and mitigation;
   - the payment consequences of the unpaid notified sum.

3. **Coldbrook may still pursue substantive claims** for defective works, safety failures, fraud if proved, delay, completion cost and other damages. Wrongful termination does not eradicate Coldbrook’s underlying causes of action; it changes the termination consequences and may give Meridian substantial counterclaims.

4. **The final account must be determined on the counterfactual that Meridian’s employment was improperly ended**, rather than under the employer’s contractual post-termination machinery.

5. **Meridian’s alleged fraud remains critically important.** A wrongful termination decision does not exonerate Meridian. It may be liable for damages and set-off if Coldbrook proves the alleged falsification, defective temporary works, and causal loss.

### G. Consequences if termination was effective

If the court upholds termination under clause 8.4.4 or common-law repudiation:

1. Coldbrook was entitled to take possession of the site and arrange completion. **D16 document 16.2.**

2. Coldbrook may claim losses flowing from Meridian’s breach, including reasonably incurred completion costs, delay losses, and losses causally connected with fraudulent certification or unsafe temporary works, subject to proof.

3. Meridian’s entitlement to payment is not automatically extinguished. The valuation and final-account consequences depend on the contract and on the value of work properly carried out, subject to Coldbrook’s valid contractual rights of set-off and damages.

4. Meridian’s GBP 1,842,500 payment claim may remain enforceable as a discrete notified-sum issue, though Coldbrook’s direct-fraud defence is stronger if the same fraud is found sufficient to justify termination.

### Bottom line on 2.2

Meridian should lead with the service defect and prematurity. They are short, clear, and contractually rooted. Coldbrook should not rely principally on clause 8.4.1.

Coldbrook’s strongest position is clause 8.4.4. But it must prove, through witness evidence and contemporaneous communications, what it actually knew on 19 December 2023 and why that information amounted to a reasonable belief of fraud notwithstanding its agent’s 30 November certification.

## 2.3 Caldera’s coverage position

## A. Fair presentation and avoidance

### Applicable statutory framework

The Insurance Act 2015 applies throughout England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland in the relevant respects.

Before a non-consumer insurance contract is entered into, the insured must make a fair presentation of the risk. A fair presentation must disclose every material circumstance known or which ought to be known, or give sufficient information to put a prudent insurer on notice to make further enquiries. Material representations of fact must be substantially correct. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/3" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 3 Law</a>.

A matter is material if it would influence a prudent insurer’s judgment on whether to take the risk and, if so, on what terms. A fair presentation may be spread across more than one document or oral presentation; a representation may be corrected before the contract is entered into. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/7" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 7 Law</a>.

Caldera has a remedy only if it proves that, but for the breach, it would either not have written the contract at all or would have written it on different terms. The burden is Caldera’s. It must also prove deliberate or reckless breach if it seeks the more severe remedies for such a breach. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/8" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 8 Law</a>.

### The alleged misrepresentations

The proposal, signed by Mr Ferris on 1 September 2023, stated:

- all temporary works were managed under BS 5975 with a Temporary Works Coordinator independent of site production;
- all temporary works were Category 3 checked by an independent external consultant;
- the board-level health-and-safety role was held by a SHEQ Director;
- there were no known circumstances likely to give rise to a claim. **D17 Part 1.**

The evidence apparently contradicts the first three statements:

- at Brackenmoor Wharf the Temporary Works Coordinator was the Site Manager, Mr Delacroix-Hale, who also held the Appointed Person and Permit Issuing Officer roles; **D01 para 13; D12 Part 3; D14 Part 4.**
- Meridian’s actual checking practice was said to be Category 2, not Category 3; **D17 Parts 1 and 3.**
- the SHEQ Director role had been removed in February 2023, and Mr Ferris held the health-and-safety portfolio alongside construction delivery; **D06 paras 1–4; D17 Part 1.**

The Q16 allegation is weaker. The June concern predated the proposal, but Meridian says it was not notified of it. The October concern postdated the proposal. An undisclosed anonymous regulatory concern is not necessarily a known “enforcement action, notice or investigation” within the wording of the question. **D02 para 3.3; D17 Part 1.**

### The broker email and Caldera’s knowledge

The key evidence is the broker’s email of 4 September 2023 to Mr Pilbeam at Caldera, copied to underwriting support. It recorded that:

- the proposal said Category 3 but Meridian “actually run day to day” Category 2 checks with an external checker;
- the SHEQ role had been folded into the Construction Director role;
- Mr Pilbeam had allegedly been told orally and had said underwriters were content and that it made no difference to the rate. **D17 Part 3.**

The email was sent after the 1 September signature date. It matters only if it was sent before the insurance contract was finally entered into or was otherwise incorporated into the underwriting process. The policy period began on 1 October 2023. **D17 Part 1.** The bundle does not state the precise date on which Caldera accepted the risk, and that must be established.

An insurer “knows” a matter if it is known to an individual participating on the insurer’s behalf in the decision whether to write the risk and on what terms. The insurer “ought to know” a matter where an employee or agent knows it and ought reasonably to have passed it to the relevant decision-maker, or where information is held by the insurer and readily available to that decision-maker. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/5" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 5 Law</a>.

Accordingly:

- If Mr Pilbeam was an underwriter or otherwise participated in accepting the risk, and he did receive and understand the information, the Category 2 and SHEQ matters may be actual knowledge attributable to Caldera.
- Even if he did not make the final decision, the email copied to underwriting support and the handwritten underwriting-file note “W&T call — TW query — see email” are potentially powerful evidence that Caldera ought to have known the position. **D17 Part 3.**
- The absence of a reply is not decisive. It may support Caldera’s case that no acceptance occurred, but the handwritten note is a contemporaneous fact that tends in the opposite direction. **D17 Part 3.**

### The strongest points for each side

#### Caldera’s strongest case

1. Mr Ferris signed a proposal containing apparently untrue statements on core risk-management matters. **D17 Part 1.**

2. The broker email corrected only Category 2 versus Category 3 and the SHEQ role. It did not expressly correct the statement that the Temporary Works Coordinator would be independent of site production. **D17 Parts 1 and 3.**

3. There is no written confirmation from Caldera accepting the broker’s alleged oral explanation. Mr Pilbeam denies recollection and says he would not have accepted a Category 2 check without a rating adjustment. **D17 Part 3.**

4. Caldera may argue that the evidence of Meridian’s safety-control failures proves materiality, although the statutory test concerns influence on a prudent insurer at placement, not hindsight after a serious loss.

#### Meridian’s strongest case

1. The Insurance Act expressly permits a fair presentation to be made in more than one document or oral communication. The 4 September email may therefore correct the 1 September proposal before binding. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/7" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 7 Law</a>.

2. The contemporaneous note in Caldera’s own underwriting file refers to the Wrenmoor & Tibb call and says “see email.” **D17 Part 3.** This is stronger than bare oral assertion.

3. Caldera bears the burden of proving that it would not have written the policy or would have written it on different terms. Mr Pilbeam’s later assertion is not necessarily sufficient, particularly where the file note indicates the issue may have been considered. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/8" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 8 Law</a>.

4. Caldera must prove deliberateness or recklessness if it seeks the most severe remedies. The broker’s attempt to correct the proposal is evidence inconsistent with an intention to conceal.

### Provisional conclusion on avoidance

Caldera’s avoidance case is **arguable but far from secure**. Its best point is the uncorrected Temporary Works Coordinator independence statement. Meridian’s best answer is that Caldera had actual or deemed knowledge of the Category 2 and SHEQ information through its own underwriter and file.

The missing evidence is critical:

- the precise contract-formation date;
- Mr Pilbeam’s underwriting role and authority;
- Caldera’s underwriting guidelines;
- evidence from a competent underwriting witness about the terms Caldera would actually have offered;
- the full underwriting file and email retention records.

Without that evidence, Caldera is unlikely to establish that it would have declined the risk entirely.

## B. General Condition 7 and section 11

General Condition 7 states that Meridian must at all times comply with statutory obligations and its own written temporary-works procedure, and describes compliance as a condition precedent to insurer liability. **D17 Part 2.**

Section 10 of the Insurance Act abolished the rule under which breach of warranty automatically discharged an insurer from liability for the whole contract. It instead addresses liability for losses occurring after breach and before remedy, subject to stated qualifications. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/10" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 10 Law</a>.

Section 11 is more directly important. It prevents an insurer from relying on non-compliance with a term, other than one defining the risk as a whole, where the term tends to reduce the risk of a particular kind, location or time, if the insured proves that non-compliance could not have increased the risk of the actual loss in the circumstances in which it occurred. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/11" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 11 Law</a>.

### Application

GC7 appears to be a risk-mitigation term rather than a term defining the risk as a whole. It seeks compliance with health-and-safety and temporary-works obligations and therefore tends to reduce the risk of a particular class of liability loss.

However, Meridian’s section 11 argument is extremely weak on the facts. The alleged non-compliances are central to the accident:

- the temporary-works control procedure required a Category 2 independent check and proper appointment of a Temporary Works Coordinator; **D01 para 13.**
- the three safety-critical roles were concentrated in Mr Delacroix-Hale; **D01 para 13; D14 Part 4.**
- three through-ties were absent; **D01 para 10; D20 item 20.1 para 1.**
- the permit relied on the disputed certificate and inspection record; **D14 Part 4.**
- lifting restarted without the expressly required recorded authority; **D12 Part 3; D14 Parts 1–2.**
- the bay carried a load of about 1,410 kg, against a 750 kg controlled-design figure if Revision C governed. **D01 para 11; D09 Part 5.**

Meridian cannot realistically prove that those failures “could not have increased” the risk of the loss which occurred. The competing experts disagree on the ranking of causes, but both accept that the absent ties and the defective temporary-works system mattered. **D19 paras 1.1–1.7; D20 item 20.1 paras 1–9.**

### Conclusion on GC7

GC7 is likely capable of surviving section 11 in this case because the alleged breach was capable of increasing, and on the evidence probably did increase, the risk of the actual collapse. Meridian’s strongest argument is that GC7 is drafted excessively broadly, applying to all statutory obligations at all times, and should not be treated as a term sufficiently connected to the particular risk. But the temporary-works failures alleged here are not remote: they are closely connected to the loss.

## C. General Condition 19, fraudulent devices, and Versloot

### The policy term and the statutory baseline

GC19 states that if any claim is fraudulent, or fraudulent means or devices are used by Meridian or anyone on its behalf to obtain policy benefit, all benefit under the policy is forfeited. **D17 Part 2.**

The statutory baseline is section 12. If an insured makes a fraudulent claim, the insurer is not liable for the claim, may recover payments already made in respect of it, and may terminate the contract from the fraudulent act. The statutory termination does not affect rights and obligations arising from a relevant event before the fraudulent act. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/12" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 12 Law</a>.

In *Versloot*, the Supreme Court held that the fraudulent-claims rule does not apply to a justified claim supported by collateral lies. A collateral lie is one that, once the facts are established, has no relevance to the insurer’s liability or the claim’s value. [*Versloot Dredging BV v HDI Gerling Industrie Versicherung AG* [2016] UKSC 45 Jurisprudence](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2016/45).

### Is TWDC-BW-07 a collateral lie?

Probably not.

Meridian sent Caldera a claim pack on 24 November 2023 which included TWDC-BW-07 and described it as the independent Category 2 design check certificate for the bay, dated 27 October 2023. **D17 Part 4.**

That representation was potentially relevant to:

- how the collapse occurred;
- whether Meridian complied with its temporary-works obligations;
- whether GC7 was satisfied;
- the credibility of Meridian’s insured defence;
- whether the public-liability claim arose from deliberate or expected conduct under Exclusion 4;
- Caldera’s exposure to civil claims;
- the allocation of fault among Meridian, TowerLine, Coldbrook and individuals.

The certificate is therefore not comparable to an irrelevant lie told merely to speed an insurer’s investigation. It goes to the factual foundation of liability and potentially to coverage itself.

The strongest point for Meridian is that the certificate’s authenticity does not necessarily determine whether Meridian was legally liable for the collapse. Caldera may still be liable under a public-liability policy if the collapse was caused by other failures, such as missing ties, weather, loading and lifting decisions. **D01 paras 10–15; D12 Part 4; D19 paras 1.1–1.7; D20 item 20.1 paras 1–9.** But that does not make the certificate immaterial. It was submitted as an important explanation of safety compliance in a claim arising from an alleged safety-system failure.

### Does GC19 require a section 17 transparency statement?

A non-consumer policy term that puts an insured in a worse position than under Parts 2, 3 or 4 of the Insurance Act is ineffective unless the insurer complied with section 17’s transparency requirements. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/16" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, ss 16–17 Law</a>; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/17" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 17 Law</a>.

GC19 appears potentially more onerous than section 12 because it provides for “all benefit under this Policy” to be forfeited, whereas section 12 preserves rights and obligations in respect of relevant events occurring before the fraudulent act. **D17 Part 2; <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/12" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Insurance Act 2015, s 12 Law</a>.**

The policy contains no statement complying with section 17 in respect of GC19. **D17 Part 2.**

Accordingly, Meridian has a substantial argument that GC19 is ineffective to the extent that it puts Meridian in a worse position than the statutory section 12 remedy. The fact that Meridian may have actual knowledge of the clause could answer a section 17 notice argument, but the bundle contains no evidence establishing actual knowledge by Meridian or its broker of the term’s disadvantageous effect.

### Conclusion on GC19

Caldera can likely rely on section 12 if it proves that Meridian made a fraudulent claim. It may have more difficulty relying on GC19’s broader “all benefit” forfeiture language without showing compliance with section 17 or actual knowledge by Meridian.

That may amount to:

- an election to affirm the policy;
- waiver by election;
- waiver by estoppel;
- estoppel by convention, if Meridian reasonably proceeded on the shared footing that Caldera would provide cover.

Meridian’s reliance case is strengthened by the practical consequences of insurer-funded control of the defence. Caldera appointed panel solicitors, funded them, and participated in litigation management. **D17 Part 4.**

Caldera will say:

- investigating and defending under a liability policy is not necessarily an unequivocal representation of indemnity;

- it had not made a final coverage decision until it received legal advice and completed investigation;
- the 2 February 2025 reservation expressly stated that neither funding nor appointment of lawyers was an admission, waiver or election. **D17 Part 5.**

### Provisional conclusion

Meridian’s election and waiver case is substantial, but its success will depend on whether Caldera had sufficient knowledge of the essential facts and whether its conduct was unequivocally inconsistent with avoidance.

The most difficult point for Caldera is the delay after 6 May 2024 and after coverage advice on 18 July 2024. **D17 Part 4.** The most difficult point for Meridian is proving detrimental reliance or an unequivocal representation where an insurer was funding a defence while investigating a major fatal-accident claim.

The claimants should not wait for the TowerLine coverage dispute to be resolved before issuing against Meridian. The limitation deadline is close.

## B. TowerLine, insolvency and the 2010 Act

TowerLine entered creditors’ voluntary liquidation on 3 February 2025. **D01 para 4; D17 Part 7.**

A body corporate being wound up voluntarily in England and Wales is a “relevant person” for the 2010 Act. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/10/section/6" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010, s 6 Law</a>.

If TowerLine incurred a liability insured under a contract of insurance, TowerLine’s rights against the insurer are transferred to the person to whom the liability was incurred. The third party may bring proceedings against the insurer without first establishing TowerLine’s liability, but cannot enforce those transferred rights until both liability and amount have been established. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/10/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010, s 1 Law</a>.

### Application

Mr Aksoy was TowerLine’s employee. **D01 para 8; D03 paras 1–4.** TowerLine’s potential liability to him for defective scaffolding, failure to maintain ties, unsafe inspection records and inadequate supervision is therefore a straightforward potential route.

The widow and dependants of Mr Wieczorek may also have a TowerLine-related claim if TowerLine’s work in erecting, tying or handing over LB-7 caused or materially contributed to his death. Mr Wieczorek was engaged through Northgate Site Solutions, rather than employed by TowerLine. **D01 paras 4 and 7–9.** That affects employment-law characterisation but does not prevent a negligence claim if TowerLine’s acts or omissions caused the injury.

### The “hold covered” problem

TowerLine’s previous policy expired at midnight on 31 October 2023. **D17 Part 7.** A broker email dated 30 October said:

- underwriters had agreed to “hold covered” on existing terms pending receipt of a completed proposal;
- TowerLine was covered from 1 November;
- TowerLine should return the forms that week. **D17 Part 7.**

The proposal was never returned. Brackenridge Mutual denies cover was bound and says the broker lacked authority. **D17 Part 7.**

The issue is therefore whether, before the old policy expired, there was:

- an oral insurance contract;
- binding authority vested in the broker;
- actual or apparent authority for the broker to bind Brackenridge;
- a “hold covered” arrangement conditional upon a proposal subsequently being returned;
- any insurer estoppel arising from the broker’s communication.

The email is strong evidence that the broker represented that cover existed from 1 November. But it is not itself conclusive evidence that the insurer authorised that statement. The missing evidence includes the broker’s terms of appointment, binding authority, underwriting records, telephone notes, and contemporaneous insurer communications.

### Best tactical route under the 2010 Act

The claimants should seek:

1. a declaration that TowerLine was insured by Brackenridge on 14 November 2023;
2. a declaration of TowerLine’s liability to each claimant;
3. enforcement against Brackenridge if insurance is established.

The claim should plead the position in the alternative:

- the broker had actual authority;
- the broker had apparent authority;
- the broker’s statement evidenced an insurer-approved hold-cover arrangement;
- if no cover existed, TowerLine remains liable, although recovery against an insolvent company may be limited.

## C. Direct claim against Meridian

### Meridian’s role and control

Meridian was the principal contractor. **D01 paras 3–5.**

The factual case for a direct duty of care is substantial because Meridian did not merely occupy the position of an upstream employer or principal contractor. It allegedly controlled the operative safety system:

- Meridian’s own procedure required a Category 2 check and appointment of a Temporary Works Coordinator; **D01 para 13.**
- Mr Delacroix-Hale held the positions of Site Manager, Temporary Works Coordinator, Appointed Person for lifting, and Permit Issuing Officer; **D01 para 13; D12 Part 3; D14 Part 4.**
- the permit to load LB-7 was issued by Mr Delacroix-Hale, relied on the disputed TWDC-BW-07 certificate and TL/BW/INSP/113 handover, and recorded no independent checker of the preconditions; **D14 Part 4.**
- the lift plan required express recorded authority from the Appointed Person before lifting could resume after suspension; **D12 Part 3.**
- lifting stopped at about 10:40 but recommenced at 11:13, with no recorded authority to restart; **D14 Parts 1–2.**
- the crane’s machine-generated record shows a final lift engaged at 11:13:26 and still engaged at the point of collapse at 11:22:04. **D14 Part 2.**

These facts are materially different from a case in which a principal contractor merely appoints a competent specialist subcontractor and leaves the technical work to it.

### CDM Regulations 2015

The Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 apply throughout England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

A principal contractor must plan, manage and monitor the construction phase and coordinate health-and-safety matters so far as reasonably practicable to ensure construction work is carried out without risks to health or safety. It must organise contractor cooperation, coordinate legal compliance, ensure appropriate preventive measures, provide suitable site induction and prevent unauthorised access. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/51/section/13" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015, reg 13 Law</a>.

A contractor must plan, manage and monitor work under its control, ensure so far as reasonably practicable that it is done without risks, comply with the principal contractor’s directions and construction-phase plan, appoint only suitably competent people, and provide workers with proper supervision, instructions and information. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/51/section/15" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015, reg 15 Law</a>.

### Application to Meridian

The following facts are capable of establishing breach by Meridian:

1. **Safety-critical role concentration.** The overlapping appointments meant that one person was responsible for coordinating temporary works, issuing loading permits and authorising lifting. **D01 para 13; D14 Part 4.**

2. **Defective control of the loading bay.** The recovered structure showed three absent through-ties at levels 6 and 7. **D01 para 10; D20 item 20.1 para 1.**

3. **Potential overloading.** The controlled design drawing stated 750 kg; the calculated load at collapse was approximately 1,410 kg. **D01 para 11; D09 Part 5.**

4. **Disputed documentation.** The tablet inspection record described three missing ties and said not to load LB-7 until rectified. The paper version said the ties and LB-7 were satisfactory. **D08 Parts 1–3.**

5. **Wind and lifting management.** The lifting plan used a 23 mph trigger, though the crane manual imposed a 20 mph maximum for loads with the relevant sail area. Mr Ferris countersigned the lifting plan as director approval for high-risk activity. **D12 Part 3.**

6. **Unauthorised lifting resumption.** There is no record of the required Appointed Person authority to resume lifting following the 10:40 suspension. **D12 Part 3; D14 Parts 1–2.**

7. **Induction concerns.** The purported induction record identified Ms Kettleborough as trainer, but she says she was on leave and did not deliver it. **D01 para 16; D06 paras 21–24.**

### Causation and the competing expert evidence

The causation case is strong but not uncomplicated.

Dr Vantrease’s opinion is that absent ties were the primary cause, overloading was secondary, and wind was not causative. **D19 paras 1.1–1.7.**

Professor Danecourt agrees that the three ties were absent and agrees that the temporary-works control system was indefensible. However, she considers that the collapse resulted from the combination of missing ties, overload and wind, and cannot rank them. **D20 item 20.1 paras 1–9.**

The claimants do not need to prove that Meridian’s breach was the sole cause. They need to prove that Meridian’s breach caused or materially contributed to the relevant injury and loss. The evidential difference between the experts affects allocation of responsibility and potentially contribution claims, but both opinions leave Meridian exposed to a substantial direct-liability case.

### The strongest point against Meridian liability

Meridian will argue that:

- TowerLine was the specialist subcontractor responsible for erecting and inspecting the scaffold;
- Mr Pyle’s first statement says the ties were in place and the bay was properly handed over; **D04 paras 1–12.**
- the weather was exceptional;
- the actual safe working load may have been 1,000 kg under Revision B or 2,000 kg under the disputed certificate; **D01 para 34; D09 Part 5.**
- the direct operational failures were those of TowerLine, Mr Pyle, Mr Delacroix-Hale or the crane operation, rather than Meridian itself.

Those points raise serious contribution and causation questions. They do not negate the evidence that Meridian itself operated the permit, temporary-works, lifting and role-allocation systems.

## E. Claims for the dependants of Mr Wieczorek

Mr Wieczorek died following a fall of approximately 21 metres after the failure of LB-7. **D01 paras 6–7.**

### Fatal Accidents Act 1976

Where a death is caused by a wrongful act, neglect or default that would have entitled the deceased to recover damages had he survived, an action lies for the benefit of the deceased’s dependants. The statutory definition of dependant includes a spouse, civil partner, qualifying cohabitant, parents, children and other specified relatives. The Act applies in England and Wales. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/30/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Fatal Accidents Act 1976, s 1 Law</a>.

The widow, Mrs Agnieszka Wieczorek, is an identified interested person in the inquest proceedings. **D02 p.1.** On the available information, she is prima facie a statutory dependant and eligible for the spouse’s bereavement award. Other dependants must be identified and their dependency proved.

Damages, apart from bereavement damages, are assessed by reference to the injury resulting from the death to each dependant. Recoverable funeral expenses may also be awarded where dependants incurred them. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/30/section/3" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Fatal Accidents Act 1976, s 3 Law</a>.

The statutory bereavement award is available to the spouse, civil partner or qualifying cohabitant of an adult deceased, subject to the statute’s conditions. The currently retrieved statutory sum is GBP 15,120. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/30/section/1A" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Fatal Accidents Act 1976, s 1A Law</a>.

### Heads of loss

The dependants should investigate and plead:

- financial dependency;
- services dependency, including domestic work, childcare, maintenance, care and household contributions;
- bereavement damages for the eligible spouse;
- funeral expenses;
- the deceased’s likely earning trajectory and work pattern;
- pension and employment benefits;
- support remittances, if any;
- the deceased’s agency work arrangements and tax records.

The available bundle identifies Mr Wieczorek as a Polish national engaged through Northgate Site Solutions. **D01 para 7.** It does not identify his income, dependants other than his widow, or whether he had children. Those matters require immediate evidence gathering.

## F. Limitation as at 13 September 2026

### Mr Aksoy

A personal-injury claim for negligence, nuisance or breach of duty must generally be brought within three years of accrual or the injured person’s later date of knowledge. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1980/58/section/11" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Limitation Act 1980, s 11 Law</a>.

Mr Aksoy was injured on 14 November 2023 and plainly knew of the injury and its cause on that date. **D01 para 8; D03 paras 8–13.** The prudent limitation deadline is therefore:

- **14 November 2026.**

### Mr Wieczorek’s estate and dependants

For a Fatal Accidents Act claim, the normal deadline is three years from the date of death or the date of knowledge of the person for whose benefit the action is brought, whichever is later. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1980/58/section/12" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Limitation Act 1980, s 12 Law</a>.

Mr Wieczorek died on 14 November 2023. **D01 para 7.** The prudent deadline for the dependant claim is therefore also:

- **14 November 2026.**

There may be an argument for a later date of knowledge for an individual dependant who did not know material facts until later. That should not be relied upon. The litigation should be issued before 14 November 2026.

### The insurer route

The 2010 Act transfers the insured’s rights to the third party if the conditions are met. It permits proceedings against the insurer before liability is established, but enforcement still requires liability and quantum to be established. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/10/section/1" class="legal-ref law" target="_blank">Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010, s 1 Law</a>.

The safest course is to issue the substantive negligence and fatal-accident proceedings before the primary limitation deadline and include appropriate declaratory relief concerning TowerLine’s insurance. That avoids an argument that an insurer dispute can be pursued after the underlying tort claim is time-barred.
